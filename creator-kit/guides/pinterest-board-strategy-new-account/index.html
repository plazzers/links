Pinterest Board Strategy for a New Account (Creators' Guide)

How to set up Pinterest boards for a new creator account: how many to start with, how to name them, board descriptions, sections and where each pin goes.

When you open a new Pinterest account to promote your videos, guides or products, boards are one of the first things you set up, and one of the easiest to overthink. Boards organize your pins, tell Pinterest what your content is about and help people find more of your work. This guide gives you a simple setup for a new account: how many boards to start with, how to name and describe them, and which board each pin should go to.

It's a starting structure, not a formula. Your own analytics will tell you what to change after a few months.

Start with a business account

If you plan to promote anything, a free Pinterest business account is worth setting up first. It gives you analytics, lets you claim your website and allows bulk pin uploads with a CSV. You can convert a personal account or create a new one. If you'll upload images in bulk, also see the bulk CSV guide.

How many boards to start with

There's no required number. A practical starting point for a creator is a small set of focused boards, often somewhere between three and ten, each about one clear topic your audience searches for.

Fewer, focused boards are easier to fill with relevant pins. An empty board, or one with a couple of pins, doesn't help anyone. A good test for each board: can you think of at least 15 to 20 pins you could add to it over the next couple of months? If not, merge it with a related board or use a board section instead.

For a faceless YouTube channel about home cooking, for example, a starting set might be:

  • Easy Weeknight Dinners
  • Budget Meal Prep
  • Kitchen Tips and Tricks
  • Pasta Recipes

Each one matches something people actually search for on Pinterest, and each will hold pins for several videos.

Name boards the way people search

Board names show up in search and help Pinterest understand your pins. Clear beats clever:

  • Use plain search phrases. "Pinterest Marketing for Creators" tells people and Pinterest what's there. "Pin Magic ✨" doesn't.
  • Keep them short. A few words that read naturally.
  • One topic per board. "Recipes and Travel and Tips" is three boards.
  • Avoid your channel name as the board name, unless it's a board for your own content only. Topic names help discovery more.

If you're unsure which phrases to use, type a few words into Pinterest's search bar and look at the suggestions that appear. They show what people search for. Pinterest Trends is also free to browse.

Write a short board description

Each board can have a description. Write one or two plain sentences about what the board holds, using the words people would search for:

Simple tips for faceless YouTube channels: titles, thumbnails, Shorts, chapters and a weekly workflow for one-person channels.

Don't stuff keywords or list hashtags. A sentence a person would happily read is the goal.

Use sections inside bigger boards

Board sections let you split a board into smaller groups without creating many thin boards. For example, a "YouTube Tips" board could have sections for Titles, Thumbnails and Shorts.

Sections are useful when a board grows, but you don't need them on day one. Start with the board, and add sections once you have enough pins to sort.

Where each pin goes

Every pin you create goes to one board first. Choose the board that matches the pin's topic most closely:

  • A pin about CSV upload errors goes to "Pinterest Marketing for Creators", not to a general tips board.
  • A pin for a recipe video goes to the board for that kind of recipe.

If you later save the same pin to a second relevant board, space it out by a few days and keep it relevant. Saving the same pin to many boards at once looks repetitive. Pinterest tends to prefer new pin images over the same image saved again and again, so making a few different designs for each page is usually a better use of time. The pin design guide covers how to make varied designs quickly.

When you schedule pins in a batch, it helps to rotate boards so one board doesn't get every pin on the same day. The scheduling guide has a simple pattern.

Board covers and order

Your profile shows your boards, with covers taken from their pins. Two small touches make the profile look tidy:

  • Choose a cover pin for each board that represents it well and has readable text.
  • Put your most important boards first. You can reorder boards on your profile, so the boards that hold your own content can appear at the top.

Secret boards and group boards

  • Secret boards are only visible to you (and collaborators you invite). They are handy for collecting ideas or drafting pins before you publish them.
  • Group boards have several collaborators. They can be useful with people you know and trust in your niche, but check what a group board is about before joining. A board full of unrelated pins doesn't help your account.

Review after a few months

After a couple of months, look at Pinterest analytics:

  • Which boards have pins that get outbound clicks or saves?
  • Are any boards still nearly empty?
  • Is there a topic you keep pinning about that doesn't have its own board yet?

Merge or archive boards that never fill up, and create a new board when a topic clearly earns one. The weekly content review includes a short Pinterest check you can repeat.

Before your first bulk upload

If you plan to schedule pins with a CSV, create your boards first. Bulk upload needs board names that match your existing boards exactly, including capital letters and spaces. The free Pinterest CSV Checker lists every board name in your file so you can compare them against your profile before uploading.

Quick checklist

  • Business account set up
  • A small set of focused boards, each with room for 15+ pins
  • Plain, searchable board names
  • A short description on every board
  • Each pin saved to its most relevant board first
  • Cover pins chosen and boards ordered
  • A review every couple of months
