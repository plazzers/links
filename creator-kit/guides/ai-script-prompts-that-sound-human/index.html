How to Write AI Video Script Prompts That Sound Human

A practical way to write AI script prompts for faceless YouTube videos that sound natural, with a full example prompt you can copy and adapt.

Most AI-written video scripts sound the same: a big opening question, a stack of adjectives, "let's dive in", and a conclusion that repeats everything. The problem is usually not the AI tool. It is the prompt. A vague prompt gets a generic script; a specific prompt with your own points, a clear voice and firm rules gets something much closer to how a person actually talks. This guide shows you how to build that kind of prompt, step by step, with a full example at the end.

Why AI scripts sound robotic

Before fixing the prompt, it helps to know what you are fixing. Generic scripts usually come from three gaps:

  • No real content. If you only give a topic, the AI fills the space with safe, general statements anyone could have written.
  • No voice. Without a description of who is speaking and to whom, the default is a polite, slightly formal narrator.
  • No limits. Without rules, you get filler phrases, long sentences and the same structure every time.

There is also a policy reason to care. YouTube's Partner Program policies reject mass-produced, repetitive ("inauthentic") content. Scripts built from your own research, examples and opinions are easier to make distinct, and they give viewers a reason to watch your version instead of another one.

The five parts of a good script prompt

A prompt that produces natural scripts usually has five parts. Write them in this order.

  1. Role and audience. Who is narrating and who is listening. "You are writing narration for a calm, friendly history channel. The viewer is curious but not an expert."
  2. Your points. The facts, steps or story beats you have already checked, in order. This is the most important part and the one most people skip.
  3. Structure. Hook, sections, ending. Give approximate lengths so the pacing fits your video.
  4. Voice rules. Sentence length, words to avoid, how to address the viewer.
  5. Output format. Plain narration only, or narration plus on-screen text notes, or section headings that match your chapters.

Write your points first

Your points are what make the script yours. Spend most of your time here, not on clever prompt wording.

  • Write each point as one short sentence of fact or instruction.
  • Add one concrete example or detail per point (a date, a measurement, a step).
  • Mark anything you want said in a specific way: "Say clearly that this is a myth."
  • Check your facts before they go into the prompt. The AI will repeat mistakes confidently.

If your points are thin, the script will be thin, no matter how good the rest of the prompt is.

Voice rules that make scripts sound spoken

Scripts are heard, not read. These rules push the AI toward natural speech:

  • Use short sentences. Most under 15 words.
  • Use contractions: "it's", "you'll", "don't".
  • Talk to one person: "you", not "viewers" or "everyone".
  • No filler openers: ban "Have you ever wondered", "Let's dive in", "In today's video", "Buckle up".
  • No stacked adjectives or words like "fascinating", "incredible", "game-changing".
  • One idea per sentence. If a sentence needs a comma and an "and", split it.
  • Vary rhythm: follow a long sentence with a short one.
  • End sections with a clear handoff, not a summary.

Keep the banned-phrase list in your prompt and add to it whenever you notice a new habit in the output.

A full example prompt

Here is a complete prompt for a how-to video in a home cooking niche. Copy it, then replace the points with your own.

You are writing voiceover narration for a faceless home cooking channel.
The narrator is calm, practical and friendly, like a neighbor who cooks a lot.
The viewer is a beginner who wants simple weeknight meals.

TOPIC: How to cook fluffy rice on the stove without a rice cooker.
TARGET LENGTH: about 900 words (around 6 minutes spoken).

MY POINTS (use all of them, in this order, and do not add new facts):
1. Rinse the rice until the water is mostly clear. This removes surface starch that makes rice sticky.
2. Use about 1.5 cups of water for 1 cup of long-grain white rice. Other rice types need different amounts; check the package.
3. Bring the water to a boil, add rice and a pinch of salt, then turn the heat to low.
4. Cover with a tight lid and do not lift it for 15 minutes.
5. Turn off the heat and leave the lid on for 10 more minutes.
6. Fluff with a fork, not a spoon.
7. Common mistake: stirring during cooking breaks the grains and makes them gummy.

STRUCTURE:
- Hook (2-3 sentences): start with the problem (sticky or burnt rice), not with a question.
- One section per point, with a short heading that I can use as a chapter title.
- Ending (3-4 sentences): one practical tip for leftovers, then a single line inviting viewers to try it tonight.

VOICE RULES:
- Short sentences, mostly under 15 words. Use contractions.
- Speak to one person as "you".
- Do not use: "Have you ever wondered", "Let's dive in", "In today's video",
  "game-changer", "incredible", "fascinating", "Buckle up", "Without further ado".
- No exclamation marks.
- Do not summarize all the steps at the end.

OUTPUT FORMAT:
- Plain narration under each section heading.
- After each section, add one line starting with "ON SCREEN:" with 2-5 words of on-screen text.
- No stage directions, no music cues, no emojis.

End with exactly this line: "That's it. Simple rice, every time."

Notice what the prompt does: it supplies every fact, sets the voice in one sentence, lists banned phrases, and gives a fixed ending line so every video closes the same recognizable way. The section headings double as chapter titles, which makes the next steps easier.

Edit the script before you record

Even a good prompt gives you a draft, not a finished script. Run through this quick edit:

  1. Read it out loud. Anything you stumble on, rewrite.
  2. Cut the first sentence if it is a warm-up. The second sentence is often the real hook.
  3. Check every fact against your points. Delete anything the AI added.
  4. Add one personal touch per section: an opinion, a comparison, a "here's what I'd do".
  5. Time it. Read a section aloud and multiply to estimate the full length.

If you use an AI voice or avatar that looks or sounds realistic, remember that altered or synthetic realistic content must be disclosed in YouTube Studio when you upload.
