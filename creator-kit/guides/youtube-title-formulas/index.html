YouTube Title Formulas That Are Not Clickbait (With Examples)

Ten honest YouTube title formulas with example titles, plus a simple checklist for length, numbers, keywords and wording you can test for free.

A good YouTube title tells the right viewer, in a few seconds, what they will get and why it matters to them. It does not need tricks. This guide gives you ten title formulas you can reuse every week, example titles from everyday niches, and a short checklist you can run on any draft before you publish. None of it is a promise of views; it is a way to write clear, honest titles faster and more consistently.

What makes a title clickbait (and what doesn't)

Clickbait is not about being interesting. It is about a gap between the title and the video. If your title says "the secret banks hide from you" and the video is a general explainer on savings accounts, viewers feel tricked, leave early and are less likely to click your next upload.

A non-clickbait title can still be curious, emotional or specific. The test is simple:

  1. Does the video deliver exactly what the title says?
  2. Would a viewer who finishes the video agree the title was fair?
  3. Could you say the title out loud to a friend without feeling silly?

If all three answers are yes, you can make the title as compelling as you like.

10 YouTube title formulas with examples

Each formula below has a pattern and two example titles from neutral niches. Swap in your own topic and keyword.

1. Number + outcome

Pattern: [Number] [Things] That [Result]

  • 7 Kitchen Habits That Make Weeknight Cooking Easier
  • 5 Budget Rules That Keep Your Spending in Check

2. How to + specific result + constraint

Pattern: How to [Result] Without [Pain]

  • How to Cook Rice Perfectly Without a Rice Cooker
  • How to Start an Emergency Fund on a Small Income

3. The mistake

Pattern: [Number] [Topic] Mistakes Beginners Make (and Fixes)

  • 6 Telescope Mistakes Beginners Make (and Easy Fixes)
  • 4 Budgeting Mistakes That Quietly Drain Your Savings

4. Why question

Pattern: Why [Surprising Fact]?

  • Why Is the Night Sky Dark If There Are So Many Stars?
  • Why Did the Roman Empire Split in Two?

5. The explained/simple version

Pattern: [Big Topic] Explained Simply in [Time]

  • Black Holes Explained Simply in 10 Minutes
  • Index Funds Explained Simply for Complete Beginners

6. What happened / the story

Pattern: What Really Happened [Event]

  • What Really Happened to the Lost Colony of Roanoke
  • What Happened When the Library of Alexandria Burned

7. Comparison

Pattern: [Option A] vs [Option B]: Which Is Better for [Person]?

  • Cast Iron vs Nonstick: Which Pan Is Better for Beginners?
  • Debit vs Credit Card: Which Is Safer for Everyday Spending?

8. Before you / timing

Pattern: Watch This Before You [Action]

  • Watch This Before You Buy Your First Telescope
  • Read This Before You Open a High-Yield Savings Account

9. The step-by-step promise

Pattern: [Task] Step by Step: [Specific Detail]

  • Homemade Bread Step by Step: No Mixer Needed
  • Finding Saturn Step by Step With a Free Sky Map App

10. The timeline

Pattern: [Topic] in [Number] [Units]

  • The Entire History of Ancient Egypt in 15 Minutes
  • A Week of Cheap Dinners for Two in 30 Minutes Each

You don't need all ten. Pick three or four that suit your niche and rotate them so your channel page doesn't look repetitive.

The title checklist (simple rules, not ranking factors)

The free YouTube Title Scorer checks a title against a short list of transparent rules and explains every point. These are writing habits that make titles clearer and easier to scan. They are not proven ranking factors, and YouTube does not publish a formula for how titles affect reach. Treat them as a checklist, not a law.

Rule What the scorer looks for Why it helps
Length 40–65 characters Long titles get cut off in many views
A number Any digit, like 5 or 2026 Sets a clear expectation
Curiosity or pain word Words like mistake, why, avoid, easier Gives a reason to click
Strong start Not "In this video" or "Today I" The first words carry the most weight
Caps At most 2 ALL CAPS words Readable, not shouty
No clickbait phrases No "shocking", "you won't believe" Keeps the title honest
Keyword Contains your target phrase Matches what people search for

A few notes on using the rules well:

  • Length: YouTube allows up to 100 characters, but long titles are truncated in many places, especially on phones. Put the important words in the first 40 or so characters.
  • Numbers: only use one if it is true and meaningful. "7 Kitchen Habits" is fine if there are seven; padding a list to hit a number is not.
  • Keyword: write the phrase the way a person would type it into search, then fit it naturally into the title rather than stuffing it at the end.

How to write a title in five minutes

  1. Write the one-sentence promise of your video: "This video shows beginners how to find Saturn with a free app."
  2. Pick a formula that matches the promise (here, step by step or how to).
  3. Draft three versions, each with a different formula.
  4. Run each through the checklist. Fix length first, then the start, then the keyword.
  5. Read the winner next to your thumbnail text and make sure they say different things.

Example of three drafts for the same video:

  • How to Find Saturn Tonight With a Free App (Beginner Guide)
  • Finding Saturn Step by Step With a Free Sky Map App
  • 3 Easy Ways to Spot Saturn Without a Telescope

All three are honest. The choice depends on your keyword and which one pairs best with your thumbnail. If your thumbnail says "SATURN TONIGHT", the second or third title adds more information than the first.

Pair the title with the thumbnail

The title and thumbnail work as a team. The thumbnail grabs attention with an image and a few words; the title explains. If both say the same thing, you waste space. Common title mistakes to avoid

  • Starting slow: "In this video I will talk about budgeting" wastes the most visible words.
  • Vague topics: "Space Is Amazing" says nothing about what the viewer will learn.
  • Overpromising: "This Changes Everything About Cooking" sets an expectation few videos can meet.
  • All caps everywhere: one or two capitalized words can add emphasis; a whole title in caps is hard to read.
  • Bait phrases: "shocking", "you won't believe" and similar wording tends to make viewers wary.
  • Same formula every time: if every title is "7 Things...", your channel page blurs together.

Keep a small title log

Titles get better when you notice patterns in your own channel. Each week, write down the title, the formula you used and the click-through rate from YouTube Studio. After a couple of months you will see which formulas suit your audience. The weekly content review guide shows a simple way to do this, and planning a faceless YouTube channel explains where title writing fits in a weekly workflow.

Remember that titles are only one part of the picture. A clear, honest title helps the right people find your video; the video itself still has to deliver.
