Best Free Tools for a Faceless YouTube Channel (Honest List)

An honest list of free tools for faceless YouTube channels: editing, audio, images, stock media, research, planning and Pinterest. No affiliate links.

You can run a faceless YouTube channel with free software. Not forever, and not without trade-offs, but well enough to learn the workflow and publish good videos before you spend anything. This list covers free tools for each step: writing, recording, editing, images, stock media, research, planning and Pinterest.

A few ground rules for this list:

  • No affiliate links. Nothing here earns this site a commission.
  • Free means a free version exists at the time of writing. Free plans change, so check each tool's current terms.
  • Check licenses for commercial use. If your channel is monetized or promotes products, make sure each tool's free plan and any media you download allow that use.

Writing and planning

  • Google Docs or any notes app for scripts. Version history is useful when you rewrite.
  • Google Sheets or another spreadsheet for a simple content plan: idea, title, status, publish date. It's enough for many one-person channels. The weekly content calendar guide shows a layout.
  • The free YouTube Title Scorer on this site checks up to three title drafts for length, a number, a strong start, clickbait words and your keyword. It runs in your browser.

If you use AI to help draft scripts, the free tiers of the major chat assistants are enough to try the workflow. The AI script prompts guide shows how to get drafts that don't sound robotic.

Voice and audio

  • Audacity is a free, open-source audio editor. It handles recording, noise reduction, cutting and normalizing voiceovers.
  • Your phone's voice memo app plus a quiet room with soft furnishings can produce clean narration. A closet full of clothes is a classic home booth for a reason.
  • AI voice tools often have free tiers with limits on minutes, voices or commercial use. Read the terms before you publish, and check whether the free plan allows monetized videos.

If you use realistic AI voices or avatars, read AI voice and avatar channels: YouTube policy basics for the disclosure settings and the monetization policies to know.

Video editing

  • DaVinci Resolve has a free version that is a full professional editor. It's demanding on older computers and has a learning curve, but it covers cutting, color, audio and titles.
  • Shotcut and Kdenlive are free, open-source editors that run on Windows, Mac and Linux. They're lighter than Resolve and fine for voiceover-plus-visuals videos.
  • CapCut has a free tier and is popular for Shorts. Some features and export options are paid, and its terms have changed over time, so check what the free version includes when you start.

Pick one editor and learn it well. Switching editors every month costs more time than any feature saves.

Recording your screen

  • OBS Studio is free and open-source, and it's the standard for screen recording. It's useful for tutorial channels that show software, websites or games.
  • Built-in recorders on Windows and macOS are fine for quick captures.

Images and thumbnails

  • Canva's free plan has templates for YouTube thumbnails and Pinterest pins. Some elements are paid-only, so watch for the crown icons.
  • GIMP and Krita are free, open-source image editors for more control.
  • Photopea is a free browser-based editor that opens layered files.

Thumbnails are mostly about readable text and a clear image, not fancy software. The thumbnail text rules apply whichever tool you use.

Stock footage, images and music

  • Pexels and Pixabay offer free stock photos and videos under their own licenses. Read the license page: it generally allows use in videos, but not reselling the media unchanged or implying a person in the footage endorses you.
  • The YouTube Audio Library in YouTube Studio has free music and sound effects for use in YouTube videos. Some tracks require attribution in your description; the library tells you which.
  • Public domain and Creative Commons sources, such as government archives and Wikimedia Commons, can be useful for history and science channels. Check each file's license and attribution requirements.

For faceless channels, how you use stock media matters for monetization too. Combining clips with your own script, narration and editing is very different from re-uploading footage with little added. The policy guide linked above explains the difference.

Research

  • YouTube's own search bar shows suggestions as you type, which hint at what people search for.
  • Google Trends compares interest in topics over time, including a YouTube search filter.
  • YouTube Studio's Research tab (where available) shows what your viewers and others search for on YouTube.
  • Pinterest Trends shows what people search for on Pinterest, useful if you promote videos there.

Pinterest and hosting

  • A free Pinterest business account gives you analytics and bulk pin uploads.
  • GitHub Pages can host your pin images for free with direct links, which bulk upload needs. The GitHub Pages hosting guide walks through it on the github.com website.
  • The free Pinterest CSV Checker checks a bulk-upload CSV for header, length, link and date problems before you upload.

Selling and email

  • Payhip has a free plan that takes a fee per sale instead of a monthly price, which suits a first product. The selling digital products guide covers the basics honestly.
  • Most email platforms have free plans for small lists, enough for a lead magnet and occasional updates. The lead magnets guide explains what to offer.

When paying makes sense

Free tools are a good way to start. Consider paying when a tool saves you real time every week, when a free plan's limits block something you actually need, or when the license for commercial use requires it.
