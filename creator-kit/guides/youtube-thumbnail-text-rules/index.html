YouTube Thumbnail Text Rules: 2–5 Words That Stay Readable

Simple rules for YouTube thumbnail text: keep it to 2-5 words, don't repeat the title, use two short lines, strong contrast and test at small size.

Thumbnail text has one job: add a quick, readable idea to the image so the right viewer understands the video at a glance. Most thumbnail text problems come from doing too much, such as too many words, words that repeat the title, or letters that disappear on a phone screen. This guide gives you a short set of rules you can apply to every thumbnail, with examples and a checklist. They are practical design habits, not a promise of more clicks.

Rule 1: Use 2–5 words

Thumbnails are seen small and fast. A viewer scrolling a feed won't read a sentence, but they will catch a few bold words.

  • Two words work well when the image does most of the explaining: "TOO SALTY?"
  • Three or four words fit most videos: "SATURN WITHOUT A TELESCOPE".
  • Five words is the upper end. Past that, the text gets small and the thumbnail looks crowded.

If you can't get your idea under five words, the idea is probably the title's job, not the thumbnail's.

Some thumbnails need no text at all, for example a clear before-and-after image. That is fine. These rules are for when you do use text.

Rule 2: Don't repeat the title

The title and thumbnail are shown together, so repeating the same words wastes half your space. Let them split the work:

Title Weak thumbnail text Better thumbnail text
How to Cook Rice Without a Rice Cooker COOK RICE WITHOUT COOKER NO MORE GUMMY RICE
Why Did the Roman Empire Split in Two? ROMAN EMPIRE SPLIT ONE EMPIRE, TWO RULERS
5 Budget Rules That Keep Spending in Check 5 BUDGET RULES WHERE IT ALL GOES
Black Holes Explained Simply in 10 Minutes BLACK HOLES EXPLAINED NOT ACTUALLY A HOLE

A useful pattern: the title says what the video is; the thumbnail text gives the feeling, the problem or the surprise. For ways to write the title side, see YouTube title formulas that are not clickbait.

Keep the thumbnail honest too. If the text promises a surprise, the video needs to deliver it.

Rule 3: Two lines, about 20 characters each

Long single lines shrink the font. Break your text into at most two lines of roughly 20 characters or fewer each.

Good:

  • Line 1: "NOT ACTUALLY"
  • Line 2: "A HOLE"

Too long:

  • "THE TRUTH ABOUT WHAT BLACK HOLES REALLY ARE"

Tips for line breaks:

  1. Break at a natural pause in speech, not in the middle of a phrase.
  2. Put the most important word on the line where you can make it biggest.
  3. Keep both lines left-aligned or both centered; mixed alignment looks messy at small size.

Rule 4: High contrast and a thick font

The text must stay readable over your image, on light and dark backgrounds, on a phone in bright light.

  • Contrast: light text on a dark area, or dark text on a light area. If the background is busy, add a solid shape or a thick outline behind the letters.
  • Font: a bold, simple sans-serif. Thin, script and decorative fonts break down when the thumbnail is small.
  • Color: one or two text colors at most. Use a second color to highlight one key word.
  • Caps: all caps is common for short thumbnail text because it reads as a block; for longer phrases, mixed case can be easier to read. Test both.

Rule 5: Keep text away from the corners

YouTube places some overlays on thumbnails, most notably the video length in the bottom-right corner. Keep important text out of that area. A safe habit is to keep text in the top half or left two-thirds of the frame, leaving room for the main image.

Design at YouTube's recommended thumbnail size of 1280×720 so your text stays sharp when it is scaled down.

Rule 6: Test at small size

This is the rule that catches most problems. Before you upload:

  1. Shrink the thumbnail to about the width of your thumb on a phone screen, or zoom out in your editor until it's tiny.
  2. Look at it for one second, then look away.
  3. Ask yourself: could you read every word? Did you understand the idea?
  4. Check it on a light background and a dark background, since viewers use both themes.
  5. Put it next to two or three other thumbnails from your niche and see if it still stands out.

If any word is hard to read at that size, cut it or make it bigger.

Common problems this test reveals:

  • A thin outline that vanishes, leaving pale text on a pale sky.
  • A second line set so small it turns into a gray smudge.
  • A key word hidden behind the video length overlay.
  • Text and a busy image fighting for attention, so neither reads clearly.

A quick thumbnail text checklist

Run through this list for every thumbnail:

  • 2–5 words (or none, if the image works alone)
  • Doesn't repeat the title
  • Maximum two lines, about 20 characters each
  • Strong contrast against the background
  • Bold, simple font
  • Nothing important in the bottom-right corner
  • Readable when shrunk to phone size
  • Honest about what the video shows

Build it into your weekly routine

Thumbnails are easier when they are a fixed step rather than a last-minute job. Write the thumbnail text at the same time as the title, right after the script is done, then design the image. If you plan videos on a board with a thumbnail stage, as described in how to plan a faceless YouTube channel, you'll always know which videos still need one.

Shorts use a similar idea for on-screen text: a few bold words that add to the hook. The guide on turning chapters into YouTube Shorts covers that step.

Over time, note which thumbnail styles you used and compare click-through rates in YouTube Studio. Your own channel's numbers are a better guide than any general rule, and these six rules are simply a reliable place to start.
