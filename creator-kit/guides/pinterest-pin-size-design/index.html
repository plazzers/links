Pinterest Pin Size and Design Rules for 2026

The right Pinterest pin size (1000x1500, 2:3), plus practical design rules for headlines, fonts, contrast, branding and alt text that read well on a phone.

Pinterest is a visual search engine, and most of its feed is seen on phones, in narrow columns, while people scroll fast. A pin that looks great on your laptop can turn into an unreadable smudge in that feed. This guide covers the pin size Pinterest recommends and a set of practical design rules for creators who make pins for videos, guides and products.

Pinterest updates its specs from time to time, so check the Pinterest help center if anything here looks different in your account.

The size: 1000 × 1500 pixels (2:3)

Pinterest recommends a 2:3 aspect ratio for standard image pins, and 1000 × 1500 pixels is the usual size to design at. It's tall enough to stand out in the feed and matches how Pinterest lays out its columns.

A few related points:

  • Taller than 2:3 can get cut off. Pinterest notes that pins with a taller ratio may be cropped in the feed. Very long "infographic" pins are a common example.
  • Square (1:1) works but is smaller. A square pin takes up less space in a column, so it's easier to scroll past.
  • Wide images look small. A 16:9 image, like a YouTube thumbnail, becomes a thin strip in the feed. The Pinterest for YouTubers guide explains why reusing thumbnails as pins rarely works.
  • File type. JPEG and PNG are the common choices. JPEG keeps photos and gradients small; PNG suits flat graphics with sharp text.

If you upload in bulk, every image also needs a public, direct link. The GitHub Pages hosting guide shows a free way to do that.

Headline text: short, big, and high on the pin

Most pins for creators carry a text headline, because it tells people what they'll get before they tap. Keep it readable at feed size:

  • Aim for a short headline, roughly 3 to 8 words. If it needs a full sentence, it probably belongs in the pin title or description.
  • Make it big. At 1000 pixels wide, headline text often needs to be 70 pixels tall or more to stay readable on a phone.
  • Put it in the top half. Viewers' eyes land at the top of a pin first, and the lower part is closer to where Pinterest places buttons and overlays.
  • Highlight one or two words in a second color instead of making everything bold and colorful.
  • Keep away from the very edges. Leave a margin around text so it doesn't feel cramped or get trimmed in some views.

Fonts, contrast and color

The same rules that make thumbnail text readable apply here (see the thumbnail text rules):

  • Use one or two fonts. A bold sans-serif for the headline and a simple font for any smaller text is enough. Thin, script and decorative fonts break down at small sizes.
  • Strong contrast. Light text on a dark background or dark text on a light one. If you place text over a photo, add a solid shape or a dark overlay behind it.
  • Limit small text. Lines under about 30 pixels tall are hard to read in the feed. Use them for minor details only, or leave them out.
  • Keep a consistent palette. Using the same two or three colors across your pins helps people recognize your pins over time, without needing a big logo.

Branding without clutter

A small, consistent brand mark helps, but it shouldn't compete with the headline:

  • Put a small logo or your site name at the top or bottom, in the same place on every pin.
  • Use your website address in plain text rather than a large watermark across the image.
  • Keep the brand area simple: one line of text, one small icon.

Make several designs per page

Pinterest tends to favor fresh images over the same image pinned again and again. Rather than repinning one design, make a few different pins for the same video or guide:

  • Different headlines that highlight different angles ("5 checks before you upload", "Why your CSV fails")
  • Different layouts: a numbered list, a single tip, a screenshot or a before-and-after
  • Different color treatments within your palette

Space them out over days or weeks. The scheduling guide covers how to do that with Pinterest's own scheduler or a bulk CSV.

Title, description and alt text

The image gets attention, but the text fields help Pinterest understand and show your pin:

  • Title: up to 100 characters. Use the phrase people would search for, written naturally.
  • Description: up to 500 characters. Explain what the viewer will get, include a couple of relevant keywords and end with a soft call to action.
  • Alt text: describe the image for people using screen readers, such as "Pin with the headline 'Keep thumbnail text to 2–5 words' on a dark background". It's an accessibility feature first, so write it for people.
  • Link: point to the specific page the pin is about, not just your home page.

Check your pin at feed size

Before you publish a batch, shrink one pin to about the width of a phone column (roughly a third of your screen width on a laptop, or smaller) and check:

  1. Can you read the headline in one glance?
  2. Is the most important word the biggest?
  3. Does it still make sense without the description?
  4. Does any text sit close to the edges?
  5. Does it look different enough from your last few pins?

If any answer is no, simplify. Removing a line of text usually helps more than adding another color.

Quick checklist

  • 1000 × 1500 pixels, 2:3 ratio
  • JPEG for photos, PNG for flat graphics
  • Short headline, large, in the top half
  • One or two fonts, strong contrast
  • Small, consistent brand mark
  • Several designs per page, spaced out
  • Title, description and alt text filled in
  • Linked to the exact page the pin is about
