How to Track What Works: A Simple 20-Minute Weekly Content Review

A 20-minute weekly review for YouTube and Pinterest: which numbers to note from YouTube Studio and Pinterest analytics, how to compare, one change a week.

Analytics dashboards can swallow an evening and leave you with nothing but worry. A weekly review is the opposite: a short, fixed routine where you write down a handful of numbers, compare them with your own past weeks and pick one thing to try next. This guide shows you which numbers to note from YouTube Studio and Pinterest analytics, how to compare them fairly and how to turn them into a single change.

Why weekly, and why only 20 minutes

Daily checking mostly shows noise. A video can look slow on day two and pick up later, or the other way round. Monthly reviews are too slow when you publish weekly, because you make four decisions before you learn anything.

Weekly fits the rhythm of a one-person channel. Twenty minutes is enough if you decide in advance what to write down, and short enough that you will keep doing it. Put it on your calendar on the same day each week, ideally before you choose next week's topic.

Set up a simple review sheet

Before your first review, make a spreadsheet or a note with one row per week. Keep the columns few. A layout like this works:

Week Video Views (first 7 days) Avg view duration
Oct 6 Example: 5 budgeting myths (your number) (your number)
Oct 13 Example: How credit scores work (your number) (your number)

Add a second small table for click-through rate, subscribers and Pinterest numbers, plus a "notes" column for what you changed that week. The Faceless Creator Kit's Channel Planner has a weekly review where you type these in and see small trend lines, but a spreadsheet does the same job.

The numbers to note from YouTube Studio

Open YouTube Studio, go to Analytics and look at your newest video and the channel overview. Names and placements in Studio change from time to time, so look for the closest equivalent if a label differs.

  1. Views in the first 7 days of the newest long video. Using the same window for every video makes them comparable.
  2. Impressions click-through rate for that video. This tells you how often people who saw the thumbnail and title chose to click.
  3. Average view duration and, if shown, average percentage viewed. This tells you whether the video kept the promise of the title.
  4. The retention graph's shape. You do not need to copy numbers here. Note in words where the big drop happens: in the first 30 seconds, at a particular chapter or near the end.
  5. Subscribers gained from that video, if Studio shows it.
  6. Top traffic sources in a few words, for example "mostly browse features" or "mostly search".
  7. Shorts: views and, if shown, the share of viewers who chose to keep watching rather than swiping away.

That is it for YouTube. Resist adding more columns until these feel routine.

The numbers to note from Pinterest analytics

If you promote videos or products on Pinterest, open Pinterest analytics in your business account. Again, labels may differ over time.

  1. Impressions for the week across your pins.
  2. Outbound clicks, the clicks that go to your link. For a YouTuber this is usually the number that matters most.
  3. Saves, which suggest a pin is useful enough to keep.
  4. Your top pin by outbound clicks, with a short note on its template, headline and board.

Pinterest often takes longer than YouTube to show results for a new pin, so judge pins over several weeks rather than one.

How to compare without fooling yourself

The goal is to compare like with like, using only your own numbers. There are no outside benchmarks in this routine, because averages from other channels in other niches tell you very little about yours.

  • Compare the same time window. First 7 days against first 7 days. A video that has been live for a month will always have more views than one live for a week.
  • Look at the last four to six videos, not just last week. One video above or below the rest is often just that topic.
  • Separate packaging from content. Low click-through rate with good watch time suggests the title and thumbnail are underselling a good video. Good click-through rate with a sharp early drop suggests the opening does not match the promise.
  • Write down what was different. New format, new thumbnail style, a different upload day, a holiday week. Without notes, you cannot connect a change to a result.
  • Treat small numbers gently. When a video has a few hundred views, small shifts in percentages can be chance. Look for patterns over several weeks.

Pick one change for next week

The last five minutes are the point of the whole review. Choose exactly one thing to change, write it in the notes column and keep everything else the same so you can see its effect.

Examples of single changes:

  1. Rewrite the first 20 seconds so the hook states the payoff faster.
  2. Shorten thumbnail text to three words and make it larger. See thumbnail text rules.
  3. Try a more specific title. Draft three options and compare them in the free YouTube Title Scorer; our guide to title formulas that are not clickbait has patterns to try.
  4. Move the strongest chapter earlier in the video.
  5. Make pins with a different template for the same video and compare outbound clicks over the next few weeks.

If nothing stands out, the change can be "keep going". Consistency is a valid choice when there is not enough data yet.

A 20-minute review, step by step

Use a timer and follow the same order every week:

  1. Minutes 0 to 5: copy the YouTube Studio numbers into your sheet.
  2. Minutes 5 to 8: copy the Pinterest numbers.
  3. Minutes 8 to 13: compare with the last four to six weeks and write one or two sentences about what you see.
  4. Minutes 13 to 17: choose one change and write it down.
  5. Minutes 17 to 20: choose next week's topic and put the change on your calendar.

If you need a calendar to hang this on, a weekly content calendar for one-person channels puts the review on Sunday.

What this review will not do

A weekly review will not tell you exactly why a video did well, and it carries no promise of views. Platforms change how they recommend content, and topics rise and fall for reasons you cannot see. What the routine does give you is a record of your own channel, a calm way to make decisions and, after a couple of months, a clear picture of which topics, formats and packaging tend to work for your audience.
