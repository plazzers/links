How Many Shorts Should You Cut From One Long Video?

There's no magic number of Shorts per video. Here's a practical way to decide how many to cut, which moments to pick and how to space them out.

Once you start making Shorts from your long videos, a practical question comes up quickly: how many should you cut from each one? Some creators post one, others slice a single video into a dozen clips. There isn't a correct number that YouTube rewards, and anyone quoting one is guessing. What you can do is decide based on the video in front of you and the time you actually have.

This guide gives you a simple way to make that decision, the signs that a moment works as a Short, and a way to space them out so they support the long video instead of repeating it.

Start from ideas, not minutes

The length of the long video tells you very little. A 20-minute video with one big argument might have only one or two moments that stand alone. A 7-minute list video might have five.

A better starting point is the number of self-contained ideas in the video. A Short works when a viewer who has never seen your channel can understand it from the first second to the last. So count the moments that:

  • Make one clear point, tip or answer
  • Don't depend on something explained earlier in the video
  • Can be told in under a minute or so, or up to three minutes if the idea really needs it (YouTube allows Shorts up to three minutes long)
  • Have a natural first line that works as a hook

If your video has chapters, start there. Each chapter is usually one idea, which makes it a candidate. The chapters to Shorts guide explains how to turn a chapter into a vertical clip, step by step.

A practical way to decide the number

Go through your chapter list (or your script's sections) and sort each one into three groups:

  1. Strong. Stands alone, has a clear hook, ends with a satisfying point. Make these.
  2. Possible. Interesting, but needs context or a rewritten opening line. Make these only if you have time this week.
  3. Skip. Intros, setups, recaps, sponsor sections and anything that only makes sense mid-video.

Your number of Shorts is the number of strong moments, capped by the time you have. A typical explainer might give you two to four strong candidates, but count your own rather than aiming for a figure.

Then apply one more filter: can you finish them properly? Each Short still needs a vertical edit, readable on-screen text, captions and a title. Two finished Shorts are more useful than six rushed ones with tiny text and abrupt endings.

Signs a moment makes a good Short

When you're unsure about a candidate, check it against these:

  • The first sentence works on its own. "Most people store coffee in the wrong place" works. "So, as I said earlier" doesn't.
  • It has a visible payoff. A before-and-after, a final answer, a number on screen or a clear "do this, not that".
  • It fits the vertical frame. If the key visual is a wide chart or a full-screen map, check it still reads when cropped to 9:16. If it doesn't, you may need a different visual or on-screen text.
  • It doesn't spoil the whole long video. A Short can give away one tip. If it gives away the entire answer, viewers have less reason to watch the long version.
  • You'd watch it from someone else. Scroll through a few Shorts in your niche, then watch your draft. Be honest about whether it holds up.

Each Short needs its own hook and title

A Short cut straight from the long video often starts mid-sentence. Before you publish, give each one:

  • A rewritten first line, recorded or generated fresh if needed, that states the point immediately.
  • On-screen text of a few bold words that adds to the hook, following the same idea as the thumbnail text rules.
  • Its own title, short and specific to that one idea, not the long video's title. You can test drafts in the free YouTube Title Scorer, which has a Shorts mode.
  • A related video link to the long video, set in the Short's details, so interested viewers can watch the full version. Links in Shorts descriptions aren't clickable, so the related video feature is the way to connect them.

Space them out

Posting all your Shorts from one video on the same day means they compete with each other and your channel shows the same topic over and over. Spreading them out usually makes more sense:

  • Post the first Short after the long video is live, so its related video link has somewhere to go.
  • Space the rest across the following days or weeks, mixed with Shorts from other videos.
  • Keep a small queue. If you make Shorts in batches, schedule them in YouTube Studio so you aren't posting at the last minute.

A simple weekly rhythm for a one-person channel: one long video, plus the strong Shorts from it, spread across the next week. The weekly content calendar guide shows where Shorts editing fits in a solo schedule.

Avoid near-duplicate Shorts

It can be tempting to cut many slightly different versions of the same moment, or to post the same clip with a new caption. That tends to be a poor use of time, and it can also run into YouTube's monetization policies on inauthentic content, which cover mass-produced or repetitive uploads. Each Short should be a distinct idea with its own edit. The AI voice and avatar channels guide links to YouTube's official policy pages if you want the details.

Learn your own number

After a few weeks, look at your Shorts in YouTube Studio and note:

  • Which Shorts were cut from which long video
  • How each one did compared with your other Shorts
  • Whether the long video got views from the Shorts (check the traffic sources for the long video)

Look for patterns rather than single results. Maybe your "common mistakes" chapters make better Shorts than your "how to" steps, or maybe the second Short from a video rarely does as well as the first. That tells you how many to cut next time, based on your channel instead of a general rule. The weekly content review shows a simple way to keep these notes without spending hours in analytics.

Quick checklist

  • Count self-contained ideas, not minutes
  • Sort chapters into strong, possible and skip
  • Make only the strong ones you can finish properly
  • Rewrite the first line and give each Short its own title
  • Set the related video link to the long video
  • Spread them out over the following days
  • Review which kinds of moments work for your channel
