Loaded Baked Potato Soup: The Restaurant Copycat

Loaded baked potato soup like the grill chains serve: the mash-half trick, ingredients, mistakes to avoid, toppings, and what a bowl costs at home.

Loaded baked potato soup is the bowl that shows up on every casual grill chain menu once the weather turns. Thick, creamy, smoky from bacon, with cheese and green onions on top. Basically a baked potato with everything on it, in a bowl, with a spoon.

You can make it at home in about 45 minutes, for roughly $1.50 a bowl. One pot. No blender. And with one simple trick, it comes out better than the one you get at the restaurant.

Here's what makes it work, what goes in it, and the mistakes that turn it into wallpaper paste.

What makes the restaurant version work

A good potato soup has two jobs. It has to be creamy, and it has to taste like a real baked potato. Most bad versions do one or the other. Either it's smooth and bland like a cream sauce, or it's a watery broth with potato cubes floating around.

The restaurant-style bowl gets both right because of a few things:

  • Bacon fat as the base. The bacon is cooked first, and the onion cooks in what it leaves behind. That smoky flavor goes into every spoonful, not just the topping.
  • A light roux. A little flour cooked in that fat gives the soup body, so it's thick without needing a ton of cream.
  • Russet potatoes. Starchy russets break down and thicken the soup on their own. That's the baked-potato taste.
  • Sour cream and cheddar stirred in at the end. That's the "loaded" part, and it's what makes it taste like a stuffed potato instead of plain potato soup.

Sal's key trick: mash half, leave half

This is the thing that makes it. When the potatoes are tender, mash about half of them right in the pot and leave the rest in chunks.

Why? Because a real baked potato is never one texture. It's fluffy and soft in the middle, with bigger bites here and there. Mash half and you get a creamy, thick base. Leave half and you still have pieces to chew. Smooth and chunky at the same time.

A plain potato masher does it. No blender, no immersion blender, nothing to clean but the masher. If you blend the whole pot, you get a heavy, gluey purée. If you don't mash any, you get thin broth with cubes in it. Half and half is the sweet spot.

The ingredients (names only)

Simple, cheap, and probably mostly in your kitchen already:

  • Bacon, chopped
  • Onion, diced
  • Flour
  • Chicken broth
  • Milk
  • Russet potatoes, peeled and cubed
  • Sour cream
  • Shredded cheddar
  • Green onions to finish

That's the whole list. Salt and pepper to taste, of course.

A few notes on choosing them:

  • Bacon: regular thick-cut is great. You want enough fat to cook the onions, and pieces big enough to stay crisp on top.
  • Potatoes: russets, please. Waxy potatoes like reds or golds hold their shape too well and the soup won't thicken the same way.
  • Cheddar: sharp, from a block, grated by you. It melts smoother than the pre-shredded kind.
  • Sour cream: full-fat. The light versions can turn grainy in a hot soup.

Common mistakes to avoid

Boiling after the dairy goes in

Once the sour cream and cheese go in, keep the heat low and stir. If the soup boils hard, the dairy can split and turn grainy. Take the pot off the heat, stir in the sour cream, then the cheese a handful at a time.

Not cooking the flour

The flour needs a minute or two in the fat before the liquid goes in. Skip it and the soup tastes a little raw and pasty.

Dumping the liquid in all at once

Add the broth gradually and whisk as you go. That's how you avoid lumps. Then the milk.

Cutting the potatoes uneven

Some pieces turn to mush while others are still hard. Cut them roughly the same size so they're all tender at the same time.

Putting all the bacon in the pot

Save some crispy bacon for the top. Bacon that sits in soup gets soft. On top, it stays crunchy. That's the "loaded" experience.

Under-salting

Potatoes soak up salt like a sponge. Taste at the end and season again. A potato soup that tastes flat almost always just needs more salt.

How to top it and what to serve with it

Toppings are where you make it yours. Set them out and let everybody load their own bowl:

  • Extra shredded cheddar
  • Crispy bacon bits
  • Sliced green onions or chives
  • A spoon of sour cream
  • Cracked black pepper
  • A few drops of hot sauce if you like heat

For sides, keep it simple. This soup is rich, so it wants something crunchy or bready next to it:

  • Warm biscuits. My cheddar garlic biscuits were made for dunking in this.
  • A crisp green salad with a sharp vinaigrette to cut through the cream.
  • Crusty bread or grilled cheese for a full "soup and sandwich" night.

Make-ahead and leftovers

Potato soup is one of those dishes that's even better the next day. Two tips:

  • It will thicken in the fridge. The potatoes keep soaking up liquid. When you reheat, add a splash of milk or broth and warm it gently, stirring.
  • Reheat low and slow. Same rule as before: no hard boil, or the dairy can split.

I'd skip freezing this one. Potato and dairy soups often turn grainy once frozen and thawed. Make a pot, eat it over a few days.

What it costs at home

About 45 minutes, roughly $1.50 a bowl, and a pot that feeds about six.

At the restaurant, a bowl of soup is one of the highest-markup things on the menu. It's cheap to make in a big batch and easy to hold hot all day. That's why it's always pushed as a starter or a "soup and salad" combo. For more about how menus steer you toward those items, read my guide to menu psychology tricks.

At home you get the same comfort, a bigger bowl, and leftovers for lunch.

The short version

  • Cook the bacon first and build everything on its fat.
  • Use starchy russets, and mash half while leaving half in chunks.
  • Keep it below a boil once the sour cream and cheese go in.
  • Save crispy bacon for the top and let everybody load their own bowl.
