Smash Burger Recipe Guide: Crispy Edges and Special Sauce

Thin patties with dark, crispy, lacy edges. Melted American cheese. A soft, buttery bun. Pickles, onion, lettuce, and that pink special sauce dripping down your wrist. That's the smash burger, the style the famous burger chains on both coasts built their names on.

You can make it at home in about 20 minutes, for roughly $2.50 a burger. And that's a double. With cheese.

There's one rule that matters more than everything else. Let's get into it.

At home, at a glance

Time
20 min
Cost
about $2.50 per burger
Makes
4 double burgers

What makes a smash burger different

A regular burger is a thick patty cooked slowly so it stays pink and juicy in the middle. A smash burger is the opposite idea. You press a loose ball of beef flat onto a very hot surface, so as much meat as possible touches the metal.

That contact is everything. It creates a deep brown crust across the whole patty, with crispy, lacy edges. That browned crust is where the big beefy flavor comes from. Two thin patties stacked together give you more crust than one thick one, which is why the double is the classic.

Other things that make it work:

  • Fatty beef. The fat renders out and basically fries the edges.
  • American cheese that melts into every corner.
  • A soft, squishy bun, toasted with butter so it doesn't go soggy.
  • The special sauce, creamy, tangy and a little sweet.

Sal's key trick: smash once, in the first ten seconds

Smash it in the first ten seconds, and never again. That crispy edge is the whole point.

Put the cold ball of beef on the hot pan, lay a little square of parchment on top so the spatula doesn't stick, and press down hard and fast with a stiff, sturdy spatula. Thin. Then take the spatula away and leave it alone.

Why only once, and why so early? In those first seconds the meat is cold and the pan is screaming hot, so it flattens easily and the whole surface sears. Press it again later and you're just squeezing the juice out onto the pan. Now you have a dry, grey disc. One smash. Then hands off.

The ingredients (names only)

For the burgers: - 80/20 ground beef, shaped into loose balls (don't pack them) - Salt and black pepper - American cheese slices - Soft potato buns - Butter, for toasting the buns - Thin-sliced onion - Pickles - Shredded lettuce

For the special sauce: - Mayonnaise - Ketchup - Sweet relish - Yellow mustard - White vinegar - A pinch of sugar - Salt and pepper

That sauce is the one every burger place acts so secretive about. It's probably in your fridge already. Mix it and chill it while you cook; it tastes better after a short rest.

The equipment

  • Cast iron or a flat griddle. It needs to get very hot and hold that heat. Nonstick pans aren't made for this kind of heat.
  • A stiff metal spatula. A flimsy one bends when you press. Some people use a second spatula on top for leverage.
  • Parchment squares. So the beef doesn't stick to the spatula when you smash.
  • Good ventilation. A real smash burger makes smoke. Fan on, window open.

Common mistakes

A pan that isn't hot enough

Without real heat, the patty steams instead of searing. No crust, no lacy edges. Heat the pan longer than you think.

Lean beef

Lean ground beef makes dry, crumbly patties without much crust. You want the fat; fat is flavor.

Packing the balls tight

Loose balls smash into tender patties. Packed balls turn dense and tough. Handle the beef as little as possible.

Smashing more than once

Covered above. The second smash only pushes out the juice.

Flipping too early

Wait until the edges are dark brown and crispy before you flip. Then scrape under the patty with the spatula so you keep all that crust.

Skipping the toasted bun

An untoasted bun soaks up sauce and juice and falls apart. Toast it cut-side down in butter until golden.

Crowding the pan

Too many patties at once cools the pan. Cook in batches and keep the finished burgers warm.

Building the burger

Order matters a little. Here's how I stack it:

  1. Toasted bottom bun, then a spread of special sauce.
  2. Shredded lettuce and pickles. The lettuce acts like a little shield, so the bottom bun doesn't get soggy.
  3. First patty with its cheese, then the second patty with cheese, stacked straight from the pan so they melt together.
  4. Thin-sliced onion. Raw for bite, or cooked on the griddle for sweetness.
  5. More sauce on the top bun. Close it, press gently, eat right away.

What to serve with it

  • Fries, of course. Oven fries work fine.
  • A crisp coleslaw or a simple green salad to cut the richness.
  • Cheddar garlic biscuits if you want something different. Strange combo, great combo.
  • Extra sauce for dipping. Make plenty; people always want more.

What it costs at home

About 20 minutes, roughly $2.50 a burger for a double cheeseburger.

A burger night out for two, with fries and drinks, adds up fast these days. At home you make four doubles for about what one restaurant burger costs, and they come straight off the pan to your plate. The short version

  • Fatty beef, loose balls, very hot cast iron.
  • Smash once, hard, in the first ten seconds. Then never again.
  • Wait for dark, crispy edges before flipping. Cheese right after.
  • Toasted buttery bun, special sauce on both sides.

That crispy edge is the whole point. Mangia.
