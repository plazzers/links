Homemade Marinara Sauce: Sal's Sunday Marinara

A homemade marinara sauce that beats the jar: olive oil, onion, garlic, good canned tomatoes and basil. About 45 minutes, about $4 for the whole pot.

If you learn one recipe from me, make it this one. Sunday marinara is the base for half the Italian food you love: chicken parm, spaghetti, meatballs, lasagna, a dip for breadsticks, a quick pizza. Once you make it, the jar on the supermarket shelf starts to look a little silly.

It's simple. Olive oil, onion, garlic, good canned tomatoes, a little oregano, fresh basil at the end. About 45 minutes, most of it gentle simmering while you set the table. The whole pot costs about $4, and a jar of the fancy stuff often costs about twice that.

This one is free. The full recipe card is right below, straight from Sal's Restaurant Copycat Cookbook. Around it, I'll tell you how to get it right, how to change it up, and what to do with it.

Why homemade marinara beats the jar

A jar sauce has to sit on a shelf for months. To me, a lot of them taste flat and sweet, like tomato paste with a hat on. A fresh pot tastes bright, a little sweet from the slow-cooked onion, with real tomato flavor and a clean finish of basil.

And you control everything:

  • The salt. Season it the way your family likes it.
  • The sweetness. Good tomatoes rarely need sugar. You only add a little if the can is sour.
  • The texture. Chunky, smooth, or somewhere in between.
  • The heat. A pinch of red pepper flakes, or a lot. Your kitchen, your rules.

Sunday Marinara: the full recipe

Makes
about 6 cups
Time
45 min
Cost
about $4 for the whole pot

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup olive oil
  • 1 small onion, finely diced
  • 5 cloves garlic, sliced
  • Two 28 oz cans whole San Marzano- style tomatoes, crushed by hand
  • 1 tsp salt, pinch of red pepper flakes, 1 tsp sugar (only if the tomatoes are sour)
  • 1 tsp dried oregano
  • A handful of fresh basil

Method

  1. Cook the onion in the oil over medium-low heat 8 minutes until soft and sweet.
  2. Add the garlic for 1 minute without browning.
  3. Add the tomatoes, salt, pepper flakes and oregano.
  4. Simmer gently 30 to 40 minutes, stirring now and then.
  5. Tear in the basil at the end. Taste for salt.

Sal's tip: Low heat, patience. A burnt garlic sauce is a sauce you pour down the drain.

Technique: what makes it work

Low heat, patience

This is the rule. Low heat, patience. A burnt garlic sauce is a sauce you pour down the drain. Garlic goes from golden to bitter in seconds, and that bitterness spreads through the whole pot. You can't fix it with more tomatoes. So keep the heat gentle, and give the garlic just a minute until it smells wonderful.

Cook the onion slowly

The onion goes in first and takes its time over medium-low heat until it's soft and sweet, not browned. This is where the sauce gets its gentle sweetness, without sugar. If you rush it on high heat, you get browned edges and a sharper flavor.

Crush the tomatoes by hand

Whole canned tomatoes, crushed with your hands right into the pot (or into a bowl first, if you don't want to wear the sauce). Whole tomatoes are usually better quality than the ones already crushed or diced, and hand-crushing gives you a rustic, restaurant texture. Watch out: they squirt. Wear an apron.

Simmer gently, stir now and then

A lazy bubble, not a rolling boil. A hard boil splatters all over your stove and can scorch the bottom. Stir every few minutes, scraping the bottom of the pot. As it simmers, the sauce thickens and the raw, tinny taste of the can cooks away.

Basil at the end

Fresh basil is delicate. Cook it for half an hour and it turns dark and loses its perfume. Tear it in at the very end, off the heat, and taste for salt one more time.

Which tomatoes to buy

Good canned tomatoes make good sauce. San Marzano-style whole peeled tomatoes are a great choice: sweet, low in acid, meaty. But plenty of everyday store brands are good too. Taste one straight from the can. If it's sweet and bright, you're set. If it's sour or tinny, that's when a small pinch of sugar earns its place.

Fresh tomatoes? In the height of summer, yes, wonderful. The rest of the year, canned tomatoes are picked and packed ripe, and they often taste better than pale winter tomatoes from the store.

Variations

  • Smooth sauce. Blend it with a stick blender at the end for a silky, pizza-parlor texture.
  • Arrabbiata-style. More red pepper flakes, and let them sizzle in the oil with the garlic. Spicy and angry, the good way.
  • Meat sauce. Brown Italian sausage or ground beef first, then build the sauce in the same pot.
  • Vodka-style. Stir in a splash of cream and a little grated parmesan at the end for a rosy, creamy sauce.
  • Puttanesca-style. Add olives, capers and a few anchovies with the garlic. Salty and bold.
  • Pizza sauce. Simmer it longer until it's thick, then blend smooth. Thick sauce keeps the crust from going soggy.

Serving ideas

  • Chicken parm. Spoon it on top, never underneath. Here's my take on restaurant-style chicken parmigiana.
  • Spaghetti or rigatoni with a shower of grated Parmigiano.
  • A dip for breadsticks. Warm a bowl of it next to a basket of copycat breadsticks.
  • Eggs in purgatory. Simmer eggs right in the sauce in a skillet, and serve with crusty bread for breakfast or a cheap dinner.
  • Meatball subs, lasagna, baked ziti. It's the base for all of them.

Storing and freezing

Let it cool, then keep it in the fridge in a covered container for a few days. It freezes very well, so I always make a big pot. Freeze it in portions, in containers or flat in freezer bags, so you can thaw just what you need for a weeknight. A good sauce in the freezer is like money in the bank.

And here's my honest opinion about what the restaurants are doing with sauce. The places I respect make it fresh that day. Why does that matter to you? Because if you can make this at home for a few dollars, you can see why a plate of pasta with red sauce is one of the most marked-up things on a menu. I talk about that in what chefs never order. The short version

  • Soften the onion slowly; give the garlic just a minute. Never brown it.
  • Good whole canned tomatoes, crushed by hand.
  • A gentle simmer, stirring now and then.
  • Fresh basil at the end, taste for salt, and you're done.
