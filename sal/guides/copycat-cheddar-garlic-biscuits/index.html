Copycat Cheddar Garlic Biscuits (Like the Seafood Chain's)

Cheddar garlic biscuits like the seafood chain's basket: why they work, the cold-butter trick, ingredients, mistakes to avoid and what they cost at home.

You know the ones. Warm, cheesy, garlicky biscuits that land on the table in a basket at the seafood chain before you've even opened the menu. People go there for the shrimp. They leave talking about the biscuits.

Good news: these are some of the easiest restaurant breads you can make at home. No yeast, no rising, no stand mixer. About 25 minutes from cold oven to warm basket, and roughly 20 cents a biscuit. A dozen costs you about the same as a cup of coffee.

The catch? There's one trick, and if you skip it you get hockey pucks. Let's talk about it.

What makes the restaurant biscuits so good

These are drop biscuits. You don't roll them, you don't cut them, you don't fold them into layers. You stir a shaggy dough and spoon it onto a sheet. That's why they look a little rough and craggy on top, and those craggy bits are exactly where they get crisp and golden.

Three things make them taste like the real thing:

  • Sharp cheddar all the way through. Not a sprinkle on top. Cheese in every bite, with little pockets where it melted.
  • Garlic in two places. A little garlic powder in the dough, and more in the butter you brush on at the end.
  • That shiny buttery top. The brush of melted garlic-parsley butter right out of the oven is what makes your fingers shine. That's the part people remember.

Restaurants make them all day because they're fast, cheap and addictive. Same reasons you should make them at home.

Sal's key trick: cold butter, quick hands

Here's the whole secret in four words. Cold butter, quick hands.

The butter goes in cubed, straight from the fridge. You rub it into the flour with your fingertips, fast, until the mix looks like coarse crumbs and little pebbles. Then you stop.

Why fast? Because your hands are warm. Warm hands melt the butter into the flour, and melted butter gives you dense, flat biscuits. Cold butter stays in little pieces. In a hot oven those pieces turn to steam, the steam makes pockets, and pockets make fluffy.

So no squeezing, no rolling the butter around like it's modeling clay. In, rub, out. If your kitchen is hot, put the cubed butter in the freezer for a few minutes first. Use cold milk too. Everything cold, oven hot.

The ingredients (names only)

Nothing fancy here. Most of it is probably in your pantry right now.

For the biscuits: - All-purpose flour - Baking powder - A little sugar - Garlic powder - Salt - Cold butter, cubed - Shredded sharp cheddar - Cold milk

For the topping: - Melted butter - Garlic powder - Dried parsley

The most expensive thing on the list is the cheese, so buy a block and grate it yourself. Block cheese melts better than the pre-shredded bags, which are coated so the shreds don't stick together. It's usually cheaper by the pound too.

Sharp cheddar matters. Mild cheddar disappears into the dough and you lose that tang. If you like a little heat, a pinch of cayenne in the dough is my favorite extra.

The exact amounts, oven temperature and timing are in Sal's Restaurant Copycat Cookbook and in Sal's Kitchen App. I keep the full recipe there, but everything in this guide will get you most of the way.

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

Overmixing the dough

Once the milk goes in, stir just until the flour disappears. Lumpy is fine. Lumpy is good. The more you stir, the more gluten you build, and the tougher the biscuit gets. If it looks a little messy, you did it right.

Warm butter

Covered above, but it's the number one reason biscuits come out flat. If the butter is soft before it even hits the bowl, you've lost before you started.

A lukewarm oven

Biscuits want a hot oven. A hot oven turns those butter pieces into steam fast, which is what lifts the dough. A timid oven melts the butter slowly, and it just leaks out.

Skipping the second brush of butter

The garlic butter goes on the moment they come out of the oven, while they're still hot enough to drink it up. Don't wait until they cool. Cold biscuits won't soak it in; it just sits there.

Using pre-shredded cheese

It works in a pinch, but the anti-caking coating means it doesn't melt as smoothly. Grate the block. It takes one minute.

Making them too big

Restaurant biscuits are small enough to eat in a few bites. Giant spoonfuls stay raw in the middle while the outside over-browns. Keep them modest and even, so they bake at the same rate.

The freezer trick

This is my favorite thing about these biscuits. Mix the dough, drop the spoonfuls onto a lined sheet, and freeze them until they're hard. Then tip them into a freezer bag.

Next time you want biscuits, bake them straight from the freezer and add a couple of extra minutes. No thawing. Warm, fresh biscuits on a random Tuesday night in about 20 minutes.

That's frozen done right: you freeze the raw dough and bake it fresh. Not baked yesterday and reheated today. If you want to know why I care about that difference, read my guide on how to tell if restaurant food is frozen.

What to serve them with

Honestly? Everything. But here are the pairings I like best:

  • Soup night. They're made for dunking. Try them next to a big bowl of loaded baked potato soup or a sausage-and-kale soup.
  • Chili. Crumble one on top or tear it and drag it through the bowl.
  • Burger night. Skip the fries and put two biscuits next to a smash burger. Sounds strange, tastes right.
  • Seafood. Garlic shrimp, fried fish, a pan of scampi. This is the combination the seafood chain built a whole meal around.
  • Breakfast. Split one, add a fried egg and a slice of ham.
  • Late at night, standing at the counter. No judgment from me.

What they cost at home vs. the restaurant

At home, figure about 20 cents a biscuit, and about 25 minutes start to finish. A full batch makes a dozen.

At the restaurant they feel free because they come with the meal. But nothing in a restaurant is free. You're paying for that basket inside the price of the entrée. At home, a whole batch costs less than a single appetizer, and you get leftovers for tomorrow.

Bottom line

  • Drop biscuits: no yeast, no rolling, about 25 minutes.
  • Cold butter, quick hands. Warm butter makes hockey pucks.
  • Grate a block of sharp cheddar and don't overmix.
  • Brush the garlic butter on the second they leave the oven.
  • Freeze the raw dough and you're never more than 20 minutes from a warm basket.
