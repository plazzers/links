Restaurant Upselling Tricks (and How to Tip Fairly)

Restaurant upselling explained: "Can I start you off with...", the little yeses, side upgrades, and how to tip fairly on the bill you agreed to.

Your server is not your enemy. Let's start there. Most servers are hard-working people on their feet all night, and many depend heavily on tips. They're also trained, very often, to sell. That's the job the restaurant gave them.

Restaurant upselling is simply the art of getting you to add a little more to your order: a starter, a bigger size, an add-on, a second drink. It's polite, it's friendly, and it works on smart people too.

Once you can hear it, you get to decide each "yes" on purpose. And you can still be a lovely guest and a fair tipper. Let me show you how.

"Can I start you off with...?"

Listen to how the question is shaped. A server rarely asks, "Would you like an appetizer?" That question has an easy answer: no thanks.

Instead you'll hear, "Can I start you off with the spinach dip or the calamari?"

Hear the difference? Now your brain is busy choosing which one, instead of asking do I even want one. In the trade, this is often called suggestive selling, and it's taught everywhere because it works.

And appetizers matter to the house. After drinks, they're often among the most profitable things on the menu. Many are fried, quick and made from inexpensive ingredients, with a nice price on top. Some come straight from the freezer, too.

Your move: smile and say, "We're good, thanks. We'll go straight to the mains." That's it. No explanation needed.

The little yeses

Then come the small questions. They sound like service. They're arithmetic.

  • "Make it a double?"
  • "Add chicken to that salad?"
  • "Want avocado on that?"
  • "Would you like to add shrimp?"
  • "Large or regular?"

Each one feels tiny. A couple of dollars here, a few dollars there. Who says no to a couple of dollars? Almost nobody. That's the beauty of it, from the restaurant's side.

But add it up. Four people, two little yeses each, and you've bought something close to an extra main course without anyone ordering one. And the add-ons themselves are often priced generously compared to what they cost the kitchen. A few slices of avocado or a piece of grilled chicken that was bought by the case.

Your move: say the price out loud before you answer. "Seven dollars for chicken on the salad?" Half the time you'll laugh and say no. The other half, you'll say yes because you really want it. Either way, you decided.

Sides and upgrades

"Upgrade your side for just a little more?" Swap fries for a loaded baked potato, a side salad for soup, regular for truffle fries.

Potatoes and greens cost very little. The upgrade price is often almost pure margin. If you want the upgrade, enjoy it. Just know the "just" in "just a little more" is doing a lot of work.

Same with "Would you like to make that a combo?" or "add a side for the table?" Before you nod, glance at the plate size. Many mains already come with plenty. I talk about plates and portions in restaurant atmosphere tricks.

Dessert, coffee and "one more round"

The final upsells come when you're full and relaxed, which is exactly when they work best. The dessert menu "just to look." Coffee. One more glass of wine. Each sounds harmless at that hour.

If you want dessert, I say share one. If the desserts aren't made in house, you might enjoy a cookie at home just as much. I explain how to tell in restaurant dessert tricks.

When an upsell is actually good advice

Let me be fair to the servers again. Not every suggestion is a sales trick. Sometimes it's the best tip you'll get all night.

  • "The short rib is what the kitchen's proudest of." That's information. Use it.
  • "That portion is small, you might want a side." A server who warns you before you're hungry is looking after you.
  • "Honestly, one starter is plenty for the two of you." When a server talks you down, remember their name. That's a keeper.
  • "The halibut came in this morning." That's not an upsell. That's a gift. See restaurant specials and market price.

The way to tell the difference is simple. Good advice comes with a reason about the food. A pure upsell comes with a question that's easy to say yes to.

How to say no without feeling awkward

Saying no to a nice person feels rude. It isn't. Servers hear "no thanks" all night long. What they remember is whether you were warm about it.

A few easy lines:

  • "We're good for now, thanks!"
  • "Just the mains tonight."
  • "No upgrade, but thank you."
  • "Let us think about dessert in a bit."

Say it with a smile and eye contact. That's all.

Tipping: be fair, be kind

Here's the part I care about most. Knowing the upselling tricks is about controlling your order, not about being stingy with the person bringing it.

In places where tipping is customary, like the US, servers often earn a big share of their income from tips. So a few simple guidelines:

  • Tip on the bill you agreed to. You chose what you ordered. Tip on the total for that, the normal way, at whatever rate is customary where you live.
  • Don't punish the server for the menu. They didn't set the prices or the drink markups. Being careful with your order is smart. Taking it out on the tip isn't fair.
  • Saying no to upsells is not a reason to tip less, and saying yes isn't a reason you must tip more. Good service is what the tip is for.
  • If something went wrong, tell the server or the manager kindly while you're still there. Most places want to fix it.
  • Be extra generous when you've taken extra. Lingered for hours on a busy night, a big group, lots of special requests? A little more is a nice thank-you.

Being a smart guest and a generous one go together just fine.

The short version

Upselling is built on friendly questions: "Can I start you off with...", little add-ons, side upgrades and late-night extras. Say the price out loud, decide each yes on purpose, and feel free to say "no thanks" with a smile. Then tip fairly on the bill you agreed to, because your server isn't the enemy.
