Restaurant Specials and "Market Price": What to Ask First

Restaurant specials can be the best plate in the house or the fridge clear-out. How to tell the difference, and why you should always ask the market price.

The server leans in. "Tonight we have a few specials." And then comes a lovely little speech: a fish, a pasta, a steak with a sauce whose name you didn't quite catch. No menu. No printed price. Just words.

Restaurant specials can be the very best thing a kitchen makes all week. They can also be a clever way to use up food before it goes past its best. Same word, two very different plates.

The good news is that two short questions sort them out every time. Let me show you.

Why restaurants run specials

Let me be fair first, because I love a real special. There are honest, wonderful reasons a kitchen puts one up:

  • Something beautiful came in. The fish supplier had gorgeous scallops this morning, or the farmer dropped off the first ripe tomatoes. The chef can't wait to cook them.
  • It's seasonal. Some things only make sense for a few weeks, like soft-shell crab or summer corn. Not worth reprinting the menu for.
  • The kitchen is trying something. A special is a test run. If it sells, it may join the menu.

These are the specials you want. They're the kitchen at its happiest.

The other kind of special

Now the other side. Every restaurant ends up with product that needs to move. Vegetables that didn't sell over the weekend. A tray of sausage from Friday. Cream getting close to its date. Throwing it away is throwing money in the garbage, so a smart kitchen turns it into something.

A pasta. A soup. A "chef's creation." Give it a nice name, write it on the chalkboard in pretty letters, and it's gone by nine o'clock.

Is that wrong? Not really. It's not illegal, it's usually perfectly good food, and every kitchen does it a little. It's just business. But it's not the same as the scallops that came in this morning, and you deserve to know which one you're ordering.

Question one: "What came in fresh today?"

This is the question that separates the real special from the clean-out-the-fridge special.

Ask it with a smile. Then watch the answer.

  • The good answer comes fast and with a little excitement. "The halibut came in this morning, chef's doing it with brown butter." That's a kitchen showing off. Order it.
  • The not-so-good answer is a pause, a look toward the kitchen, and something vague like "everything's fresh." That's your hint.

Another trick I use: count the ingredients and see if they belong together. A special with three or four things that obviously go together, like fish, lemon, capers and butter, is the chef talking. A special with six things that wandered in from different dishes is the fridge talking. I talk more about that in what chefs never order.

Question two: "How much is the special?"

Here's the part that catches people. Notice that the special almost never has a price next to it. The server describes it out loud, beautifully, and the number just... doesn't come up.

And you're too polite to ask. Then the bill arrives, and that special was noticeably more than anything on the printed menu.

So ask. "How much is the special?" Nobody thinks you're cheap. They think you're paying attention. A good server will tell you happily, and many are glad you asked, because a surprised guest at the end of the night is nobody's idea of fun.

"Market price" decoded

Then there's the famous one. "Lobster: market price." "Whole fish: MP."

Sometimes it's completely honest. Lobster, crab and some fish really do change price from week to week, and printing a fixed number would be silly. That's what the phrase was made for.

But think about who orders without asking. Someone on a date. Someone taking the boss to dinner. Someone on an anniversary who doesn't want to look careful with money. Restaurants know these situations very well. When nobody asks, "market price" can quietly drift toward whatever the table will pay.

And the market, my friend, is you.

Always ask the price, out loud. "What's the market price on the lobster tonight?" It's a normal question. Restaurants hear it all the time. If someone at the table would judge you for asking, that's their problem, not yours.

Specials by day of the week

The day you eat out changes the odds a little. This isn't a law, just how a lot of kitchens tend to run:

  • Thursday and Friday are often when the big deliveries arrive, ready for the weekend rush. Specials built around fresh fish and meat are more likely to be, well, fresh.
  • Saturday is the busiest night. The kitchen is cooking flat out, and specials tend to be things it can fire quickly and consistently.
  • Sunday night and Monday are when the weekend's leftovers need a home. That's exactly when a "chef's creation" pasta or soup is most likely to be a tidy-up of the walk-in cooler.

So a Monday special deserves one more question than a Friday special does. Same rule as always: ask what came in, and when.

Specials and the "soup of the day"

The soup of the day is the original special. At a proud kitchen it's made from good stock and whatever's in season, and it can be a little gem. At another place it's a bag that gets heated up each morning. Same words on the chalkboard.

Ask, "Is the soup made here?" You'll usually get an honest answer.

When a special is worth it

I don't want you to walk away thinking "never order the special." That would be a shame. Here's when I go for it:

  • The server tells you, quickly and happily, what came in today.
  • The ingredients clearly belong together.
  • You've heard the price and it feels fair for what's on the plate.
  • It's something hard to make at home. A braise, a whole fish, fresh pasta.

Tick most of those boxes and the special might be the best plate in the building tonight. For more on choosing well, see what to order at a restaurant, and for the price games on the printed menu, see menu pricing tricks.

A little script you can steal

If asking feels awkward, have the words ready. Something like:

"Those sound great. What came in fresh today? And how much are the specials?"

That's it. Two questions, one breath. You sound like someone who knows food, because now you do.

The short version

Specials can be the kitchen's proudest dish or a smart way to use up leftovers. Ask "What came in fresh today?" and "How much is the special?" every time, and always ask the market price out loud. Then order the special that makes the server's eyes light up.
