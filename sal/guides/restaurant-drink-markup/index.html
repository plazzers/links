Restaurant Drink Markup: Where the Real Money Is

Restaurant drink markup explained: why soda, coffee, iced tea, wine and cocktails earn more than the food, and simple ways to drink well for less.

Want to know where a restaurant really makes its money? It's often not the food. It's the glass next to the food.

Food is expensive to buy, slow to prep, and a lot of it ends up in the bin. Drinks are the opposite. Many cost the restaurant very little, take seconds to pour and keep for ages. That's why your server asks about drinks before anything else, and why the bread is salty. More on that in a minute.

None of this means you shouldn't order a drink. Enjoy one! Just know what you're paying for, so you can choose the ones that are worth it.

Why drinks carry the biggest margins

Think about what goes into a plate of food: ingredients that spoil, a cook's time, the stove, the dishwasher. Now think about a soda. Syrup, bubbles, ice, a cup. Almost no labor and almost nothing to throw away.

So restaurants lean on drinks to help pay the rent. In the business, drink sales are commonly described as some of the most profitable items on the whole check. A table of four that orders drinks with dinner can add a lot to the bill without the kitchen doing any extra work at all.

Soda, iced tea and the "free" refill

A fountain soda costs the restaurant very little per glass. You pay a few dollars. And free refills? Of course they're free. The refills cost them almost nothing too.

Iced tea is an even better deal for the house. Brewed tea and water, in a big batch. It may well be one of the highest-margin things in the whole building.

Nothing wrong with any of that. A cold drink is nice. But if you're just thirsty, think about how many sodas you'd buy at full price somewhere else. At a restaurant, the answer is usually "a lot fewer."

Coffee and the after-dinner cup

Coffee is the same story. A cup brewed from a big pot costs the restaurant pennies, and it's offered at the exact moment you're full, relaxed and lingering over dessert. An espresso or cappuccino is a bit more work, but the margin is still very comfortable.

I love a coffee after dinner. Often, though, the coffee I make at home tastes just as good, for a tiny fraction of the price.

Wine: the bottle and the glass

Wine is where the numbers get big. It's common for a bottle on a restaurant list to cost several times what the bottle cost the restaurant. Not every place, and not every bottle, but that range is very normal in the business.

A few things to know:

  • Wine by the glass is often marked up even more than by the bottle. Some places aim to cover the cost of the whole bottle with the first glass or two they pour.
  • The second-cheapest bottle trick. People rarely want to order the cheapest bottle, so they pick the next one up. Some lists put a bottle with an extra-big markup right in that spot. I cover more price games like this in menu pricing tricks.
  • Pricier bottles sometimes carry a smaller percentage markup. That doesn't make them cheap, but the very cheapest wines are not always the best value per dollar.

My move: ask the server, "What's the best value on this list?" A good server knows exactly where the deal is. And if the place allows it, ask whether they have a corkage policy. Bringing your own bottle and paying a corkage fee can be a nice way to enjoy something special.

Cocktails and the pour

A cocktail feels like a treat, and the price looks like one too. The spirit in a single cocktail usually costs the bar far less than the menu price. The rest pays for the bartender's skill, the fancy glass, the garnish and, yes, the margin.

A well-made cocktail by someone who cares is worth paying for once in a while. A sugary drink from a pre-made mix is a lot of money for not a lot of craft.

A few tips:

  • Happy hour is when drinks are often priced closer to fair. If you want one, that's a smart time.
  • Ask what's in it. If the answer is "it's our house mix," you're paying cocktail prices for something from a bottle.
  • One great drink beats three average ones. Your wallet agrees.

Juice, bottled water and the "specialty" drinks

A few more drinks to keep an eye on:

  • Bottled water. "Still or sparkling?" sounds like a simple question, but it skips right over the free option. Tap water is a perfectly normal answer. If you love sparkling water, enjoy it, just know you're ordering a paid drink.
  • Fresh-squeezed juice and lemonade. Real fresh juice takes work, so a higher price can be fair. "House lemonade" from a mix is another story. Ask which it is.
  • Mocktails and specialty sodas. Lovely for a non-drinker, and often priced close to a cocktail. A splash of syrup and soda water can cost a surprising amount.
  • Milkshakes. A treat, and usually priced like one. Think of it as dessert in a glass, and budget for it that way.

None of these are bad choices. They just add up faster than people expect.

The salty bread connection

Free bread and chips are lovely, and I'd never tell you to skip them. But notice: they're usually salty. Salty food makes you thirsty, and thirsty people order another drink. I explain the rest of this game in restaurant atmosphere tricks.

So eat the bread. Enjoy it. Just notice if you're on your third soda and you only came in for a plate of pasta.

How to drink well for less

Here's my simple drink plan when I eat out:

  1. Water first. Ask for tap water. It's free, it's cold, and it goes with everything.
  2. Pick one drink you'll really enjoy. One good glass of wine or one nice cocktail, instead of several you barely notice.
  3. Ask for the best value on the wine list. Let the server help you.
  4. Skip the after-dinner coffee if you're heading home anyway. Make one there.
  5. Watch the refills of paid drinks. Some places refill soda for free, some charge per glass. Ask if you're not sure.

Do this and the bill can drop by a surprising amount. Want more ways to trim the check? See how to eat out cheaper.

Bottom line

Drinks are often where a restaurant earns its best margins: soda, iced tea and coffee cost very little to serve, and wine is often priced at several times what the bottle cost. Drink water first, choose one drink you really want, and ask the server for the best value. You'll enjoy it more and spend less.
