Copycat Cilantro Lime Rice (Like the Burrito Chain's)

Copycat cilantro lime rice like the fast-casual burrito chain makes: the bay leaf secret, ingredients, mistakes that make it mushy, and the cost at home.

Cilantro lime rice is the quiet hero of the burrito bowl. You line up at the fast-casual burrito chain, they scoop it in first, and everything else sits on top of it. Fluffy, separate grains, bright with lime, flecked with green.

At home it takes about 25 minutes and costs roughly 40 cents a serving. That's one of the cheapest copycats in the whole book. And there's one ingredient nobody tells you about that makes all the difference.

Here's how to get it right, and how to stop it from turning into a sticky, gummy mess.

What makes the restaurant rice so good

Restaurant cilantro lime rice looks simple. Rice, lime, cilantro. But a few details give it that clean, fluffy, restaurant texture and flavor:

  • Long-grain white rice. Long-grain cooks up separate and fluffy, not sticky like short-grain.
  • Rinsed rice. Washing off the loose surface starch is what keeps the grains from gluing together.
  • A little oil in the pot. It coats the grains and helps them stay separate.
  • A bay leaf while it cooks. The secret. We'll get there.
  • Lime juice, lime zest and cilantro folded in at the end. Not cooked in. Added after, so they stay fresh and bright.

Sal's key trick: the bay leaf

The bay leaf is the thing nobody tells you about. Drop one in the pot with the rice and water, then pull it out when the rice is done.

You won't taste "bay leaf." What you'll taste is rice that has a little something behind it, a gentle savory, almost herbal background that makes it taste finished instead of plain. Without it, the lime and cilantro sit on top of bland rice. With it, the whole thing tastes like it belongs together.

Try it once without the bay leaf and once with. You'll see. It's a small leaf doing a big job.

The ingredients (names only)

  • Long-grain white rice, rinsed
  • Water
  • Oil (a neutral one)
  • Bay leaf
  • Salt
  • Fresh lime juice
  • Lime zest
  • Chopped fresh cilantro

That's the whole thing. No butter needed, no broth needed. Cheap, simple ingredients and good technique.

A few notes:

  • Rice: regular long-grain white or basmati both work. Basmati is a little more fragrant. Avoid short-grain or "sushi" rice, which is meant to be sticky.
  • Lime: fresh, and use the zest too. The zest carries a lot of the lime perfume. Zest before you juice; it's much easier.
  • Cilantro: chop the leaves and the tender stems. If cilantro tastes like soap to you, swap in flat-leaf parsley and a little extra lime. Not the same, but still good.

How to keep it fluffy

Mushy rice is the number one complaint. Here's how to avoid it:

  • Rinse until the water runs mostly clear. Swirl the rice in a bowl of water, drain, repeat a few times. Cloudy water is starch, and starch is glue.
  • Toast the rinsed rice in the oil for a minute. It helps the grains stay separate and adds a little nutty flavor.
  • Don't lift the lid. Once the pot is covered and the heat is low, leave it alone. Every peek lets the steam out.
  • Let it rest off the heat. After cooking, keep the lid on and let it sit. The grains firm up and finish steaming.
  • Fluff with a fork, not a spoon. A spoon mashes. A fork separates.

Common mistakes

Adding the lime while it cooks

Cooked lime juice turns dull and a little bitter. Fold it in after the rice is done and has rested.

Stirring like risotto

This is not risotto. Stirring rice while it cooks releases starch and makes it sticky. Lid on, hands off.

Too much water

Extra water doesn't make softer rice; it makes wet rice. Measure carefully, and if you use a rice cooker, follow its line.

Mixing in the cilantro too early

Hot rice wilts cilantro fast. Let the rice cool a few minutes before folding it in, and it stays green and fresh.

Forgetting the salt

Unsalted rice tastes flat no matter how much lime you add. Salt goes in the water.

How to serve it

This rice is a base. It goes under almost anything:

  • Burrito bowls. Rice, beans, grilled chicken, restaurant-style salsa, corn, cheese, sour cream. Your own bowl, your own rules.
  • Fajita night. Serve it next to sizzling chicken fajitas instead of plain rice.
  • Inside burritos. Spoon it into a warm flour tortilla with your fillings and roll it tight.
  • With grilled fish or shrimp. Lime and seafood are old friends.
  • With a bowl of queso on the side. I won't stop you.

Make-ahead and leftovers

Rice keeps well, which makes this perfect for meal prep:

  • Fridge: store it covered and use within a few days.
  • Reheating: sprinkle a little water over it, cover, and warm it in the microwave or a covered pan so it steams back to fluffy.
  • Freshen it up: add a squeeze of lime and a little fresh cilantro after reheating. It tastes freshly made again.
  • Freezer: cooked rice freezes well in flat bags. Thaw and reheat with a splash of water.

What it costs at home

About 25 minutes, roughly 40 cents a serving. A bag of rice, a lime and a bunch of cilantro make many meals' worth.

At the burrito chain, the rice is the cheapest thing in the bowl, and it's used to make the bowl look full. At home you can build a bigger bowl for a fraction of the price and choose exactly what goes on top. If you want to compare the numbers on a full meal, try the Restaurant or Home? calculator.

The short version

  • Long-grain rice, rinsed until the water runs clear.
  • A bay leaf in the pot. Nobody tells you, and it makes the whole thing.
  • Lid on, hands off, then rest and fluff with a fork.
  • Lime juice, zest and cilantro go in at the end, not during cooking.
