Copycat Breadsticks: Soft, Garlicky, Brushed Twice

Copycat breadsticks like the bottomless ones at the big Italian chain: soft, buttery and garlicky. About 2 hours, mostly rising, about 25 cents each.

The bottomless breadsticks at the big Italian chain. Soft, pillowy, shiny with garlic butter, a little salty. You told yourself you'd only have one, and then the basket was empty.

At the restaurant they're "free." But nothing is free, my friend. You're paying for the bread inside the price of the pasta. At home, a batch costs about 25 cents a breadstick, and they come out of your oven warm, smelling like garlic and butter.

They take about 2 hours, but almost all of that is the dough rising while you do something else. The hands-on part is short. And the trick that makes them taste like the real thing is something most home recipes skip.

At home, at a glance

Time
2 hr (mostly rising)
Cost

What makes the restaurant version work

These are not Italian grissini. Grissini, from Turin, are thin, dry and crunchy, like a pencil you can eat. The American restaurant breadstick is a different animal: soft, tender and buttery. Both are delicious. Here's what makes the soft kind work:

  • An enriched dough. A little butter and sugar in the dough keeps the crumb soft and tender.
  • A gentle bake. They come out pale golden, not deep brown. A brown breadstick is a crunchy breadstick, and that's not what we want here.
  • The topping. Melted butter, garlic powder, salt and a pinch of dried oregano.
  • Brushed twice. This is the big one.

Ingredients at a glance

By name only. The exact amounts and the full method are in Sal's Restaurant Copycat Cookbook and in Sal's Kitchen App.

For the dough: - Warm water – warm to the touch, like a baby's bath. Too hot and it hurts the yeast. - Yeast – instant or active dry. - Sugar – feeds the yeast and softens the crumb. - All-purpose flour – no special bread flour needed. - Softened butter – for tenderness. - Salt – for flavor.

For the topping: - Melted butter - Garlic powder – yes, powder, not fresh. It spreads evenly and won't burn. - Salt - Dried oregano – just a pinch.

That's it. Flour, water, yeast, butter, salt. That's the whole secret.

Sal's key technique tips

Brush them twice

This is the rule. The chains brush them twice, and that second brush is why you can't stop eating them. Brush the breadsticks with the garlic butter the second they come out of the oven. The first coat soaks into the hot bread. Wait a couple of minutes, then brush them again. The second coat sits on top and shines. Now you know.

Why the second brush matters so much

People think the restaurant has some special flour or a secret dough. They don't need one. What they have is a habit. When the bread comes out of the oven, it's hot and thirsty. The first coat of garlic butter disappears into it almost right away. You can watch it happen. That coat seasons the inside of the crust and keeps it soft.

But if you stop there, the outside looks dry and matte by the time it reaches the table. The second coat, a couple of minutes later, has nowhere to go. It sits on the surface. That's the shine you see in the basket, and it's the salty, garlicky hit you taste the second it touches your lips. Two thin coats beat one thick coat every time, and it's the cheapest trick in this whole cookbook.

Knead until it feels like a pillow

Knead the dough until it's smooth, soft and springy. By hand takes a few more minutes than a stand mixer, and it's good therapy. If it's still sticky and shaggy, keep going.

Give it time to rise

Let the dough rise somewhere warm until it's roughly doubled. Then shape the sticks and let them rise again on the tray. The second rise is what makes them light and fluffy instead of dense.

Shape them even

Divide the dough into equal pieces and roll each one into a log the same length and thickness. Even sticks bake evenly. A kitchen scale helps, but eyeballing works once you get the hang of it.

Pull them out pale

Bake until they're just lightly golden. Soft breadsticks need to come out before they brown deeply.

Common mistakes

  1. Water that's too hot. It can kill the yeast and the dough never rises. Warm, not hot.
  2. Rushing the rise. Under-risen dough bakes up dense and heavy.
  3. Over-baking. Dark breadsticks are crunchy breadsticks. Pull them early.
  4. Only one brush. You'll get good bread, not the restaurant breadstick.
  5. Fresh garlic in the topping. It can scorch on hot bread and tastes harsh raw. Garlic powder is the right call here.
  6. Too much flour while shaping. A light dusting only, or the sticks get dry.

Make ahead and storage

  • Freeze the shaped dough. Shape the sticks, freeze them on a tray, then bag them. Thaw and let them rise before baking.
  • Freeze baked breadsticks. Cool them completely, freeze, then reheat in a warm oven and brush again with fresh garlic butter.
  • Day-old breadsticks. Warm them in the oven for a few minutes. Don't microwave them; they turn chewy.

Serving ideas

  • With soup. A creamy sausage potato kale soup and a basket of these is the classic restaurant combo.
  • With pasta. Perfect for mopping up the sauce from copycat fettuccine alfredo.
  • For dipping. Warm marinara on the side. My Sunday marinara sauce recipe is free.
  • Cheesy version. Sprinkle grated parmesan over the second butter brush.

Time, cost and is it worth it?

At home: about 2 hours, mostly rising, at about 25 cents a breadstick. Flour, yeast, butter and salt are some of the cheapest things in your pantry.

The short version

  • Soft, enriched dough; let it rise twice.
  • Bake until just pale golden, not brown.
  • Garlic butter brush straight out of the oven.
  • Wait a couple of minutes and brush again. Twice. Always twice.
