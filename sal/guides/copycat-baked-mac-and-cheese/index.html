There's a side dish at the Southern-style country restaurants that people order instead of a vegetable and never feel bad about. Baked mac and cheese. Golden on top, creamy underneath, the kind you scoop out of the corner of the pan to get the crispy edge.

At the restaurant it comes in a little crock for about six dollars. At home you can make a whole pan that feeds eight for about a dollar and a half a serving, in about 50 minutes. And with the holidays coming, this is the side dish everybody asks you to bring.

Let me tell you what makes the restaurant version work, and the mistakes that turn homemade mac and cheese grainy, greasy or dry.

What makes restaurant mac and cheese different

Box mac and cheese and restaurant baked mac are two different animals. The restaurant kind has a few things going for it.

  • A real cheese sauce. Butter and flour cooked together, milk whisked in, then cheese melted into it. This is the base of almost every creamy mac and cheese you've loved.
  • Evaporated milk. This is the old diner trick. It makes the sauce extra creamy and helps it stay smooth even when you reheat it the next day.
  • Two cheeses. Sharp cheddar for the flavor, plus a softer melting cheese like Colby or Monterey Jack for the creamy pull.
  • A little mustard powder and paprika. You won't taste mustard. It just makes the cheese taste more like cheese.
  • A buttery crumb topping. Panko tossed with melted butter, baked until golden. That's the crunch on top.

None of it is complicated. It's all about the order and the heat.

The ingredients (names only)

Here's what goes into the pan. The exact amounts and the full method are in the cookbook.

  • Elbow macaroni
  • Butter and flour
  • Milk and evaporated milk
  • Mustard powder, paprika, salt and pepper
  • Sharp cheddar, shredded
  • Colby or Monterey Jack, shredded
  • Panko breadcrumbs and melted butter for the top

That's it. No fancy cheese, no truffle oil. Diner food.

Sal's key tips

Shred your own cheese. This is the big one. Bagged shredded cheese is coated with a little starch so it doesn't clump in the bag, and that coating can keep it from melting smoothly. A block and a box grater take five minutes and give you a much silkier sauce.

Undercook the pasta. Pull it a couple of minutes before the package says it's done. It keeps cooking in the oven, soaking up sauce. Fully cooked pasta turns soft and mushy after baking.

Cook the butter and flour for a minute. Melt the butter, whisk in the flour and let it bubble gently for about a minute, stirring. That takes away the raw flour taste. Then add the milk slowly, whisking, so you don't get lumps.

Take the pot off the heat before the cheese goes in. If the sauce is boiling when you add cheese, the cheese can split and turn grainy or oily. Off the heat, add it a handful at a time and stir until each one melts. Gentle heat is the whole secret.

Make the sauce a little looser than you think. It should look almost too saucy when you mix it with the pasta. The pasta drinks it up in the oven. A thick sauce going in means a dry pan coming out.

Season the sauce, not just the water. Salt the pasta water well, then taste the cheese sauce before it meets the pasta. Cheese is salty, so go easy, but a sauce that tastes flat on the spoon will taste flat on the plate.

Common mistakes

Grainy sauce. Usually the sauce was too hot when the cheese went in, or the cheese was pre-shredded. Lower the heat, shred your own, add it slowly.

Greasy sauce. Often the same cause: too much heat, so the fat separates out of the cheese. Some very aged cheeses do this more easily too. Mixing in the softer melting cheese helps.

Dry mac and cheese. Not enough sauce, pasta cooked too long, or baked too long. Bake it just until it bubbles at the edges and the top is golden.

Soggy topping. Toss the panko with melted butter before it goes on, and don't cover the pan in the oven. Butter is what makes the crumbs brown and crisp.

Cutting it straight from the oven. Give it about ten minutes to rest. The sauce settles and thickens a little, so it scoops instead of running.

Making it ahead for the holidays

This is a great make-ahead side, which is exactly what you want on a busy holiday cooking day.

You can mix the pasta and sauce, spread it in the baking dish, let it cool, cover it and keep it in the fridge until the next day. Add the buttered crumbs right before it goes in the oven, and expect it to need extra time because it starts cold. A splash of milk stirred in before baking helps if it looks stiff.

Leftovers reheat well too, thanks to the evaporated milk. Warm them gently, covered, with a little milk, in the oven or a pot on low. They freeze well in portions.

How to serve it

Mac and cheese at the country restaurants usually sits next to something smoky or fried. At home:

  • Next to a holiday ham or turkey
  • With crispy chicken Parmigiana or a pan-seared steak
  • On game day, with a pot of diner chili (spoon the chili on top, trust me)
  • As the main event with a simple salad and buttermilk ranch on the side

For a family dinner, serve it in the baking dish right on the table. Everybody fights over the corners.
