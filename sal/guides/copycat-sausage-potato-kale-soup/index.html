Sausage Potato Kale Soup Like the Italian Chain's

Creamy sausage potato kale soup like the Tuscan-style bowl at the big Italian chain. About 40 minutes and roughly $2 a bowl when you make it at home.

You know this soup. Creamy broth, spicy Italian sausage, tender slices of potato, dark green kale. It's the Tuscan-style soup at the big Italian chain that people order with "unlimited" refills and then can't stop thinking about.

Good news: it's one of the easiest copycats there is. One pot, about 40 minutes, and roughly $2 a bowl at home. It reheats beautifully, it feeds a crowd, and it tastes like you worked much harder than you did.

The trick that makes it taste like the one you remember isn't an ingredient. It's how you cut the potatoes. Let's get into it.

What makes the restaurant version work

This soup is a lesson in layering flavor in one pot:

  • Sausage and bacon first. They render fat and leave browned bits on the bottom of the pot. That's the flavor foundation for everything else.
  • Onion and garlic in that fat. Soft, sweet, savory.
  • Broth and potatoes simmered together. The potatoes release a little starch, which gives the soup body.
  • Cream at the end. Just enough to make it rich and silky without turning it into chowder.
  • Kale last. It wilts quickly and stays bright green with a bit of chew.

And personally, I'll say this: when I eat out, the soup is often where the kitchen actually cooks. It's one of the better orders on a chain menu. But it's even better at home, where you control the spice and the salt.

Ingredients at a glance

By name only. The exact amounts and the full method are in Sal's Restaurant Copycat Cookbook and in Sal's Kitchen App.

  • Italian sausage – mild or hot, casings removed. Hot gives you that gentle background kick.
  • Bacon – chopped. It adds smokiness and salt.
  • Onion – diced.
  • Garlic – minced.
  • Chicken broth – low-sodium if you can, since the sausage and bacon bring plenty of salt.
  • Russet potatoes – sliced thin. More on that below.
  • Heavy cream – for the creamy finish.
  • Kale – chopped, with the tough stems removed.
  • Red pepper flakes, salt and pepper – to taste.

Sal's key technique tips

Slice the potatoes thin and even

This is the rule. Thin, even slices, about the thickness of a coin or a bit more. Thick chunks make it a stew. Thin slices make it the soup you remember. They cook evenly, they're soft all the way through at the same time, and they sit nicely on a spoon. A mandoline makes this fast; a sharp knife and a little patience work too. Russets are the right choice because they get tender and release a bit of starch that thickens the broth naturally.

Brown the sausage properly

Don't just cook it until it's no longer pink. Let it sit in the pot without stirring for a minute so it browns. Those brown bits are flavor. Break it into small, bite-size crumbles as it cooks.

Drain some fat, not all of it

Sausage and bacon together make a lot of fat. Spoon off the excess so the soup isn't greasy, but keep a little to cook the onions in. That's where the flavor is.

Cream goes in gently

Once the cream is in, keep it at a gentle simmer, not a rolling boil. A hard boil can make the cream separate and the soup look broken.

Kale at the very end

Stir the kale in during the last few minutes. It should wilt and turn deep green, not grey and soft. If you're making the soup ahead, add the kale when you reheat.

Common mistakes

  1. Thick potato chunks. You'll get a stew. Thin slices, please.
  2. Salting too early. Sausage, bacon and broth are already salty. Taste at the end, then season.
  3. Boiling after the cream. Gentle simmer only.
  4. Leaving the kale stems in. They stay tough and stringy. Strip the leaves off the stems.
  5. Overcooking the potatoes. Thin slices cook quickly. Check them with a fork; they should be tender but not falling apart into mush.
  6. Kale too early. Grey, sad kale. Add it last.

Storing and variations

Storing and reheating

This soup is a great make-ahead meal, with one catch: potatoes keep drinking the broth as the soup sits. Leftovers will be thicker the next day. That's normal. When you reheat, add a splash of broth or water and warm it gently over medium-low heat, stirring now and then. Don't let it boil hard, for the same reason as before: the cream likes gentle heat.

If you want to freeze it, freeze the base before the cream and kale go in. Creamy soups with potatoes can turn a little grainy after freezing. Thaw, reheat, then finish with cream and fresh kale, and nobody will know.

Make it your way

  • Spinach instead of kale. Softer and milder. Stir it in right at the end, it wilts in seconds.
  • Turkey or chicken sausage. Lighter, with a little less fat to drain.
  • Spicier. Hot sausage plus extra red pepper flakes.
  • Less cream. Use half-and-half for a lighter broth; just keep it off a hard boil.
  • Make-ahead. The base (everything except the cream and kale) keeps well in the fridge. Reheat, add cream and kale, and serve.

Time, cost and is it worth it?

At home: about 40 minutes and roughly $2 a bowl. The sausage is the biggest cost, and a pot feeds a family with leftovers for lunch.

At the restaurant, soup is often bundled into a deal that looks generous. Whether it really is depends on what else you order. I break that down in how to eat out cheaper. For your own numbers, try the Restaurant or Home? calculator.

The short version

  • Brown the sausage and bacon well; keep a little of the fat.
  • Slice the potatoes thin and even. That's the whole secret.
  • Gentle simmer once the cream goes in.
  • Kale last, bright and green. Mangia.
