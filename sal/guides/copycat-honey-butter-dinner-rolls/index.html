Copycat Honey Butter Dinner Rolls (Like the Steakhouse Basket)

Soft honey butter dinner rolls like the steakhouse bread basket: why they work, Sal's dough tips, ingredients, common mistakes and make-ahead ideas.

You sit down at the steakhouse, and before anybody asks what you want to drink, a basket lands on the table. Warm, soft, slightly sweet rolls with a little dish of whipped honey butter. Half the table fills up before the steaks arrive. Nobody regrets it.

Here's the good news. Those rolls are one of the most forgiving yeast breads you can make at home. The dough is soft and rich, it rises happily in a warm kitchen, and the honey butter takes about two minutes. Start to finish it's around two and a half hours, but most of that is the dough sitting there doing its own thing while you do something else.

And at home they cost about 20 cents a roll. A whole pan of a dozen costs about what you would tip on one steak.

With the holidays coming, this is the bread basket I'd put on the table. Let's talk about how to get them right.

What makes the steakhouse rolls so good

Three things, and they all matter.

  • A soft, enriched dough. This isn't crusty bread. Milk, butter, egg and honey all go into the dough. That's what makes them tender and keeps the crust thin and soft instead of crunchy.
  • Just a little sweetness. Honey in the dough, honey in the butter. Not dessert-sweet. Just enough that you notice.
  • Whipped honey butter on the side. Soft butter, honey, a pinch of cinnamon and a pinch of salt, whipped until it's light. The salt is the part people forget, and it's the part that makes it taste like the restaurant.

Some steakhouses make a darker roll with a bit of cocoa or molasses. This one is the classic golden kind, the one most people picture when they think of a warm dinner roll.

The ingredients (names only)

Here's what goes in. The exact amounts and the full method are in the cookbook.

For the rolls: - Warm milk - Instant yeast - Honey - Egg - Softened butter - Bread flour (all-purpose works too) - Salt

For the honey butter: - Soft butter - Honey - A pinch of cinnamon - A pinch of salt

That's it. No special flour, no starter, no stand mixer required. A big bowl and your hands will do the job.

Sal's key tips

Warm, not hot. The milk should feel just warm on your wrist, like a baby's bottle. Too hot and you hurt the yeast. Too cold and the dough sits there sulking. This one thing decides whether your rolls rise in an hour or never really get going.

Bread flour if you have it. It has more protein, so the rolls get a little more chew and hold their shape better in the pan. All-purpose flour still makes very good rolls. Don't run to the store over it.

Knead until it's smooth and a little tacky. A soft dough is a good dough. People get nervous and keep adding flour because it sticks to their hands. That's how you get dense rolls. Use a light hand with the extra flour and give the dough time.

Shape them tight. Pull each piece of dough into a smooth ball by tucking the edges underneath. A tight surface means a nice round top and an even rise. Put them close together in the pan so they rise into each other. That's how you get those soft pull-apart sides.

Brush with butter the second they come out. It keeps the tops soft and shiny. Skip this and you get a drier crust that doesn't look like the basket.

Common mistakes

Adding too much flour. The number one reason homemade rolls turn out heavy. The dough should be soft. If it's stiff like play dough, it went too far.

Rushing the rise. Watch the dough, not the clock. It's ready when it has roughly doubled and a gentle poke leaves a dent that springs back slowly. A cold kitchen can take a lot longer. Put the bowl near a warm spot, not on top of the stove burner.

Overbaking. These rolls should be golden, not brown. Pull them when the tops are an even golden color and they sound hollow when you tap the bottom of one. A few extra minutes turns soft rolls into dinner rolls nobody asks for twice.

Cold, hard honey butter. Straight from the fridge it tears the bread. Let it sit out a bit before dinner so it spreads.

Make them ahead for a holiday dinner

On a big cooking day the oven is busy and so are you. Rolls are easy to move out of the way:

  • Bake ahead and rewarm. Bake them a day ahead, let them cool, and store them covered at room temperature. Before dinner, warm them wrapped in foil in a low oven for a few minutes, then brush with butter.
  • Freeze them baked. Cool completely, freeze in a bag, thaw on the counter, then warm in foil. Nobody will know.
  • Make the honey butter early. It keeps about two weeks in the fridge. Make a double batch. It goes on pancakes, cornbread and toast too.

If you're doing a whole holiday spread, these go nicely next to the garlic butter sauce for the potatoes or a steak, and the cheddar garlic biscuits if your family is a biscuit family. Some families are both. I don't judge.

How to serve them

Warm, in a towel-lined basket, with the honey butter in a little bowl. That's the whole restaurant trick: the warmth and the presentation do half the work.

They're right at home next to:

  • A steak dinner, like the one in eating out vs. cooking at home
  • Soup night, especially the loaded baked potato soup
  • The holiday table, between the turkey and the mashed potatoes

Leftover rolls make great little sandwiches the next day. Split, toasted, with leftover turkey and a smear of the honey butter. That might be the best part of the holiday.

Time and cost at home

  • Makes: 12 rolls
  • Time: about 2 hours 30 minutes, mostly rising time
  • Cost: about 20 cents a roll at home

At a steakhouse, the basket is "free," but it's built into what you pay for everything else. At home, a dozen warm rolls cost you a couple of dollars and an afternoon of mostly waiting.
