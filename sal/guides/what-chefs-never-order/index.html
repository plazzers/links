What a Chef Never Orders at a Restaurant

What chefs never order when they eat out: the well-done steak, Monday fish, the fridge-clearing special, late hollandaise, the pricey plain salad and more.

Ask a room full of cooks what they never order when they eat out, and you'll hear the same handful of answers again and again. Not because those dishes are dangerous. Relax. It's because cooks know how kitchens work, and they'd rather spend their money on the plate that shows a kitchen at its best.

So this is my list. It's personal taste and value, nothing more. If you love one of these dishes, eat it with joy. Your mouth, your rules.

And at the end, I'll tell you what I do order. That's the fun part.

Steak and fish: when the kitchen can hide things

The first two are about what a dish lets you see, and what it lets the kitchen hide.

The well-done steak at a cheap steakhouse

There's an old kitchen joke. What do you do with the worst steak in the cooler? Save it for the person who orders it well-done.

It's a joke. But like every good kitchen joke, there's a little truth in it. Medium-rare shows you everything: the color, the grain, the texture. Cook a steak all the way through and every steak turns the same grey. An odd-shaped cut with a bit too much gristle looks just like a beautiful one.

A great steakhouse will cook your steak any way you like and do it properly. At a budget place, I'd rather see what I'm paying for.

What I do instead: order medium. Or, if you love your meat cooked through, order something built for long cooking, like a burger or a braised short rib.

Fish on a Monday

You're in a lovely town, hours from the ocean. It's Monday night. And the special is sea bass.

Hmm.

Many restaurants get their big seafood deliveries toward the end of the week, because the weekend is when everybody eats out. Plenty of suppliers don't deliver on Sunday. So Monday's fish has very often been sitting since Thursday or Friday. A good kitchen stores it properly, but fish is a clock. Every day, it loses a little sweetness and that firm, clean flake.

What I do instead: ask, "When did the fish come in?" A good server answers in one second. "This morning, chef's excited about it." Order it. If they look at the ceiling and say "let me check"... have the chicken.

Dishes that have been waiting around

The next three have one thing in common: time. Food that sat a while before it reached you.

The Sunday night "chef's special" pasta

Sunday night. The weekend's over. On the chalkboard, in lovely handwriting: rigatoni with roasted vegetables, sausage and a touch of cream.

Sounds great. Often, it's the weekend in a bowl. The vegetables that didn't sell, the sausage from Friday's special, the cream that's close to its date. Pasta forgives everything. Cream forgives everything. Cheese on top? Forgiven twice.

It can taste perfectly fine. But nobody designed that dish. The fridge did.

My test: a special with six ingredients that don't really belong together is the fridge talking. Three ingredients that clearly belong together is the chef talking. Order the chef. More on this in restaurant specials and market price.

Hollandaise at the end of brunch

I love hollandaise. Egg yolks, butter, lemon. Fresh, it's silk. But it's a nightmare to hold. Too hot and it breaks; too cold and it sets up like pudding. So lots of kitchens make one big batch before brunch and keep it warm near the stove for hours.

At ten in the morning, it's gorgeous. By half past one, it's tired. Thick, a bit greasy, the lemon gone flat.

What I do instead: early brunch, get the Benedict and enjoy every bite. Late brunch, order something cooked when you order it. An omelet. Pancakes. French toast.

Buffet seafood

I love a buffet. The ambition! The trays! But I walk right past the shrimp and crab legs.

Seafood is one of the most expensive things a kitchen buys, and a low, all-you-can-eat price means the buffet has to buy it as cheaply as possible. Then it sits under heat. Shrimp turns rubbery fast. I just don't know how long it's been in that tray.

What I do instead: go for the carving station, where someone is slicing it fresh in front of you, plus the potatoes and the little desserts.

Big prices for simple plates

These two are about value, plain and simple.

The plain salad with a big price

Lettuce, a few tomatoes, some cucumber, croutons and oil and vinegar. It's one of the cheapest things a kitchen makes, with no stove and not much skill. And then it shows up on the menu at a main-course price. Add chicken or salmon and it climbs higher still.

A truly great salad, with peak summer tomatoes or real burrata, is worth paying for. A pile of lettuce? You can make that at home in your pajamas.

Plain marinara at the big Italian chain

This one hurts me. Pasta, tomatoes, garlic, olive oil, maybe basil. At home it costs very little per plate. At a big chain, it can cost about as much as a dish that took real work, and the sauce may well have been heated up rather than made that day.

My rule: order what you can't easily make at home. Make the marinara yourself. My Sunday marinara sauce takes about 45 minutes. Then order the osso buco when you go out.

Anything the server can't describe

The simplest rule I've got. You ask, "What's in the signature sauce?" and the server says, "Oh, it's like... a sauce? Everybody gets it."

That tells me nobody sat the staff down and fed them the food. Maybe nobody in the back knows either. When a server lights up and tells you the whole story, how long it braised and where the fish came from, that's a proud kitchen. Order whatever they're excited about.

What I order instead

Here's my "yes" list, and notice none of it is the most expensive thing on the menu:

  • A braise. Short ribs, lamb shank, osso buco. You can't fake time.
  • Fresh pasta made in house. Ask: "Is the pasta made here?" A proud yes means order it.
  • Whatever came in fresh today. The happy side of the fish question.
  • A dessert made in house, shared. Somebody got up early to make it.

The short version

Chefs skip dishes that hide what they are, sit around too long, or cost a lot for very little skill. Ask when the fish came in, judge the special by how well its ingredients belong together, and order what you can't easily make at home. Then enjoy it. Mangia bene.
