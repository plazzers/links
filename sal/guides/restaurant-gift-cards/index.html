Restaurant Gift Cards: What to Check Before You Buy or Use One

Giving restaurant gift cards this year? Bonus card fine print, expiration rules, fees, where the card works, scams to avoid and how to use every dollar.

Every December, the gift card rack by the register fills up. And every December, a lot of us grab a few restaurant cards for the mail carrier, the teacher, the brother-in-law who's impossible to shop for. A restaurant gift card is a good gift. It's a night out somebody doesn't have to pay for.

But restaurants love gift card season too, and not only because of the sales. Here's what most people don't think about: a good share of gift cards come back with a bigger bill attached, and some never come back at all. The "buy $50, get $10 free" deal at the front door has rules most people never read.

None of this is a scam. It's just how the business works. So here's what to check before you buy one, give one, or finally use the one in your kitchen drawer.

Why restaurants push gift cards in December

Think about it from the restaurant's side. A gift card is money in the register today, in their slowest planning month, for a meal that happens later. Maybe in January, when the dining room is quiet. Maybe never.

And when that card does come back, the person holding it usually spends more than the card is worth. A fifty-dollar card turns into a sixty or seventy-dollar dinner, because nobody wants to order carefully when it feels like free money. They add the appetizer. They add the dessert. That's not a trick, it's human nature, and the restaurant knows it.

Knowing that doesn't mean don't buy them. It means use them on purpose.

The bonus card fine print

This is the big one. "Buy a $50 gift card, get a $10 bonus card." Sounds like free money. Sometimes it nearly is. But read the back of that bonus card.

Bonus cards, also called promotional cards, are often treated differently from the gift card you paid for. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains that cards given away for free as part of a promotion can come with shorter expiration dates and other limits, as long as those terms are disclosed. Common rules on a bonus card:

  • A short window. Often only valid in January, February or March, and expired after that.
  • Not valid on holidays, or on certain days of the week.
  • A minimum purchase before you can use it.
  • One per table or per visit.
  • Not good with other offers, or not on alcohol, tax or tip.
  • Dine-in only, or not for takeout and delivery.

None of these are bad, as long as you know them. The problem is the bonus card that sits in a drawer until April and is worth nothing. If you get one, write the dates on your calendar the day you get it.

The rules for the card you paid for

The gift card you buy with your own money has more protection. Under federal rules in the United States, as the CFPB explains, a gift card generally can't expire for at least five years from the date it was bought or last loaded with money. Inactivity or dormancy fees are generally only allowed after a year of no use, no more than one per month, and only if they're clearly disclosed. Some states give even more protection.

That said, read the back of the card and the receipt. The rules have exceptions, the terms should be printed on or with the card, and if anything is confusing, the CFPB's gift card pages are a good place to start.

Where does the card work?

This one surprises people. Not every location with the same name on the sign is owned by the same company. Many restaurant groups have a mix of company-owned places and franchises, and gift card rules can be different between them.

Before you buy or give a card, ask:

  • Does it work at every location, or only this one?
  • Can it be used online for takeout or delivery orders?
  • Does it work at the group's other restaurants? Some restaurant groups own several different concepts, and their cards work at all of them. That can make a card a lot more useful.

For a local independent restaurant, the card usually works only there. That's fine. Just make sure the person you're giving it to actually lives near it.

Use it, don't save it

Here's a little kitchen truth: the best time to use a restaurant gift card is soon.

Restaurants close. It happens to good places all the time. When a restaurant shuts its doors or a company goes out of business, an unused gift card can be very hard to get any value from. That card in the drawer isn't getting more valuable. It's just getting older.

A good rule: use a gift card within a few months. If you get one in December, plan the dinner for January or February, when tables are easier to get anyway.

How to get the most out of a gift card

When you finally sit down with that card, here's how to make it count:

  • Use it on a weeknight or at lunch. Many restaurants have lunch portions and lunch prices on similar dishes. Your card goes further.
  • Decide your total before you order. Card value plus what you're happy to add, including tip. Then order inside that number.
  • Tip on the full bill, not just what's left after the card. Your server did the same work either way.
  • Watch the extras. Drinks and desserts are where the bill runs past the card fastest. Our guide to restaurant drink markups explains why.
  • Ask if you can split it across two visits if the card is large. Many cards keep the balance, but check.
  • Keep the receipt with the remaining balance, or snap a photo of it.

Gift card scams to watch for

A few warnings, because these happen every season:

  • Check the package. At the store, look for scratched-off PIN areas, torn packaging or a card that looks handled. Thieves sometimes tamper with cards on the rack and drain them once they're activated. If something looks off, take a different card.
  • Keep the receipt. If there's a problem with activation or the balance, the receipt is your proof.
  • Buy direct. Buying a card from the restaurant's own website or at the restaurant is the safest. Discount resale sites and strangers online carry more risk.
  • Nobody legitimate asks for payment in gift cards. The Federal Trade Commission is very clear on this: anyone who tells you to pay a bill, a fine or a "problem" with gift cards is a scammer. Hang up.

A better gift idea, if you want one

A restaurant gift card is fine. But you can also give the restaurant night itself. A homemade pasta dinner. A jar of your own Sunday marinara with a box of good pasta and a note that says "dinner's on me." Some people like that kind of gift even more, and it costs a lot less.

The short version

Restaurant gift cards make good gifts, but read the fine print. Bonus cards often expire fast and have limits, so put the dates in your calendar. The card you paid for generally can't expire for years under federal rules, but use it soon anyway, because restaurants close. Ask where the card works, set your total before you order, tip on the full bill, check packages for tampering and never pay anyone with a gift card. Then go enjoy that dinner.
