Copycat Spinach Artichoke Dip (Restaurant-Style, Hot and Bubbly)

Copycat spinach artichoke dip like the grill chains serve: why it works, the dry-spinach trick, ingredients, common mistakes, and what it costs at home.

Hot spinach artichoke dip is the appetizer every grill chain sells you before you've even looked at the entrées. Bubbly, cheesy, creamy, with a basket of chips on the side. And somehow it always costs more than you expect.

At home it takes about 30 minutes and costs roughly $1 a serving. It's one bowl, one baking dish, and one very important squeeze.

Here's what makes the restaurant version so good, the ingredients by name, and the mistakes that turn it into a soupy mess.

At home, at a glance

Time
30 min
Cost
about $1 per serving
Makes

Why the restaurant version works

Most people think spinach dip is just "cheese and spinach." The good ones are more careful than that. What makes the restaurant dip so scoopable comes down to balance:

  • A thick, creamy base. Cream cheese gives it body, sour cream and mayonnaise loosen it and add tang. Together they hold up in the oven without going greasy.
  • Two cheeses doing two jobs. Parmesan brings the salty, nutty flavor. Mozzarella brings the stretch and the golden top.
  • Real chunks of artichoke. Chopped, but not puréed. You want to bite into them.
  • Spinach that's dry. More on that in a second, because it's the whole secret.
  • A hot oven at the end. That's where the edges bubble and the top browns.

Sal's key trick: squeeze the spinach until your hands hurt

I mean it. Frozen spinach holds an amazing amount of water. If you thaw it and toss it straight into the dip, that water comes out in the oven and you get a watery puddle around the edges. Wet spinach equals watery dip.

So: thaw the spinach, put it in a clean kitchen towel, twist the towel, and squeeze. Then squeeze again. Keep going until almost nothing more drips out and the spinach is a tight, dry little ball. Your forearms should feel it.

Do the same with the artichoke hearts. Drain them well, chop them, and press them between paper towels. A dry dip stays thick and creamy. A wet one separates.

The ingredients (names only)

  • Cream cheese, softened
  • Sour cream
  • Mayonnaise
  • Grated parmesan
  • Shredded mozzarella (some in the dip, some on top)
  • Frozen spinach, thawed and squeezed very dry
  • Artichoke hearts, drained and chopped
  • Garlic, minced
  • Red pepper flakes

A few shopping notes:

  • Cream cheese: the full-fat brick, not the whipped tub. Let it sit out until it's soft so it mixes smooth. Cold cream cheese makes lumpy dip.
  • Parmesan: grate it from a wedge if you can. The powdery shelf-stable kind won't melt into the base the same way.
  • Artichokes: canned or jarred in water or brine. Marinated ones work too, but they bring oil and extra flavor, so drain them very well.
  • Frozen spinach: chopped frozen spinach is the easiest. Fresh works, but you'll need a mountain of it and you still have to wilt and squeeze it.

Common mistakes

Wet spinach

Covered it, but it's worth saying twice. Squeeze it. Then squeeze it again.

Cold cream cheese

If the cream cheese is straight from the fridge, you'll be chasing white lumps around the bowl forever. Soft cream cheese blends smooth in a minute.

All the mozzarella in the mix

Save some for the top. That's what gives you the golden, bubbly crust you see at the restaurant. If it's all inside, the top stays pale.

Too much garlic, raw

Mince it fine so it melts into the base. Big chunks of raw garlic stay sharp, even after baking.

Pulling it too early

You want the edges bubbling and the top spotted golden. If the middle isn't hot yet, give it a few more minutes. A lukewarm center is a sad dip.

Under-seasoning

Taste the base before it goes in the oven. Cheese is salty, but artichokes and spinach are not. A pinch of salt, some black pepper, and the red pepper flakes wake it up.

What to dip and how to serve it

The restaurant gives you tortilla chips. You can do better:

  • Tortilla chips for the classic. Thick ones, so they don't snap.
  • Toasted baguette slices brushed with olive oil.
  • Pita chips or warm pita wedges.
  • Crackers, the sturdier the better.
  • Raw vegetables: bell pepper strips, carrots, celery, cucumber.
  • Leftover breadsticks, sliced and toasted. My garlic butter breadsticks are excellent for this.

Serve it right out of the baking dish, still bubbling, on a board with a towel under it. If you're putting out a full dip spread for a party, pair it with a bowl of white queso and some restaurant-style salsa. Nobody leaves hungry.

Make it ahead

This is a great party dish because you can do the work early:

  • Mix it the day before. Put the dip in the baking dish, cover, and keep it in the fridge. Top with the mozzarella just before baking.
  • Bake from cold. It'll take a few extra minutes in the oven since it starts cold. Watch for the bubbling edges.
  • Leftovers reheat well in the oven or in short bursts in the microwave, stirring between them.

Why I make it at home instead of ordering it

Spinach dip is one of the classic upsell appetizers. The server suggests it before you've even asked, and it's priced like a meal. I wrote about how that works in my guide to restaurant upselling tricks.

At home: about 30 minutes, roughly $1 a serving, and a dish big enough to share with the whole table. You choose the cheese. You choose the dippers. And it comes out of your oven bubbling, not out of a holding drawer.

The short version

  • Cream cheese, sour cream and mayo for the base; parmesan for flavor, mozzarella for stretch.
  • Squeeze the spinach until your hands hurt. Wet spinach equals watery dip.
  • Keep some mozzarella for the top and bake until the edges bubble.
  • Make it the day before and bake it when the guests arrive.

That's it. Grab a chip.
