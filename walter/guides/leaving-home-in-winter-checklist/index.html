Leaving Home in Winter: A Checklist Before You Travel

Going away for the holidays? A winter checklist for your house: heat, water, pipes, outside, safety and a neighbor who can check in while you're gone.

The bags are packed, the car is loaded, and somebody is asking whether you remembered the charger. In all of that, the house gets a quick glance and a locked door.

In summer, that's usually fine. In winter, an empty house has a few extra ways to get into trouble. The heat can go off. A pipe on an outside wall can freeze. A small drip can run for a week with nobody there to hear it. And the longer you're gone, the longer a small problem has to grow.

The good news is that most of it is prevented by a short walk through the house before you leave. Here's the checklist, in the order that makes sense.

A few days before you go

Some things are easier to do before the last-minute rush.

  • Line up someone to check on the house. A neighbor, friend or family member who can stop by every day or two, especially during a cold snap. Give them a key, your phone number, and a short note: where the main water shutoff is, where the breaker panel is, and who your plumber and heating company are.
  • Check the heating system. If the furnace or boiler hasn't been serviced this season, now is a good time. At the very least, run the heat for a day and make sure it cycles normally. Put in a fresh filter.
  • Check the thermostat batteries. Many thermostats run on batteries, and some will stop controlling the heat when they die. Replace them now rather than hoping.
  • Look for drips. Check under sinks, around toilets and near the water heater. EPA WaterSense points out that small household leaks can waste a lot of water over time, and a week away is a lot of time.
  • Stop the mail and packages, or ask your neighbor to pick them up. A pile on the porch tells everyone the house is empty.

Heat: don't turn it off

It's tempting to turn the heat way down to save energy while nobody's home. Don't turn it off, and don't set it too low.

The American Red Cross recommends that if you're going away during cold weather, you leave the heat on and set no lower than 55 degrees Fahrenheit. That keeps warmer air around the pipes inside the walls and under the floors.

  • Set the thermostat to a steady temperature, at least that level, and turn off any schedule that would drop it lower at night.
  • If you have a smart thermostat, make sure it's connected so you can see the temperature from your phone, and turn on alerts if it offers them.
  • Leave interior doors open so warm air can move through the house.
  • Open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, so room air reaches those pipes.

If you live where it gets very cold and you'll be gone for a long time, talk to a plumber about whether draining the water lines makes sense for your house. It isn't something to guess at.

Water: the main shutoff decision

A burst pipe or a failed washing machine hose with nobody home is one of the worst things that can happen to an empty house. That's why many people turn off the main water valve before a trip.

  • Find the main water shutoff and make sure it turns. It's usually where the water line comes into the house, often in the basement, crawlspace or utility room. Our frozen pipes guide explains how to find it.
  • If you turn the water off, open a faucet briefly to let pressure out of the lines, then close it again.
  • Check before you shut it off whether anything in your house needs water while you're away. Some heating systems, such as hot water boilers, may need a water supply, and some homes have fire sprinklers. If you're not sure, ask your heating company or a plumber.
  • Water heater: many have a "vacation" setting that keeps the water warm but uses less energy. Check your manual. If you're not sure how to adjust a gas water heater safely, leave it as it is.
  • Turn off the supply to the washing machine at the two valves behind it, even if you leave the main on.

Outside the house

A quick walk around the outside before you leave:

  • Garden hoses disconnected from outside faucets, and those faucets shut off or covered if that's how your house is set up.
  • Furnace, water heater and dryer vents clear of snow and leaves, so they can vent properly while you're gone. Ask your neighbor to check them after a storm.
  • Gutters and downspouts clear and pointing away from the foundation.
  • Snow removal. If you're gone during a snowstorm, an unshoveled walk is another sign nobody's home. Some people arrange for a neighbor or a service to clear it.
  • Outdoor lights on a timer or a dusk-to-dawn sensor.

Safety before you lock up

These take a few minutes and matter a lot:

  • Test the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms. Press the test button on each one. If you have alarms that can send alerts to your phone, make sure they're connected.
  • Unplug what doesn't need to stay on: small kitchen appliances, chargers, electric blankets, space heaters, and holiday lights and decorations, inside and out. Never leave a space heater running in an empty house. The U.S. Fire Administration lists heating equipment among the leading causes of home fires.
  • Real Christmas tree? Water it before you go, make sure the lights are unplugged, and ask your neighbor to top up the water if you'll be gone more than a day or two.
  • Candles out. All of them. Check twice.
  • Fireplace: if you used it in the last day or so, make sure the fire is completely out and the ashes are cold. Ashes can stay hot for days, so they belong in a metal container with a lid, outside and away from the house.

Locks and light

You don't need a security system to make a house look lived-in.

  • Lamps on timers in a couple of rooms, set to come on in the evening.
  • Every door and window locked, including the door from the garage into the house and basement windows.
  • The garage door closed, and if you have an opener with a vacation or lock mode, turn it on.
  • Deadbolts all the way in. If you have to push or lift a door to lock it, the bolt may not be fully seated.
  • Be quiet online about the trip until you're home.

What your neighbor should check

Give the person checking on the house a short list. It makes their job easier and yours safer.

  • Is the house warm? Look at the thermostat.
  • Any water on floors, under sinks, near the water heater or in the basement?
  • Any water stains on ceilings?
  • Are the outside vents clear of snow?
  • Is the mail and any package picked up?

And tell them what to do if something's wrong: where the main water shutoff is, and who to call. A phone number on the fridge is worth a lot at the wrong moment.

When you get home

Walk through the house before you unpack:

  • Check that the heat is on and the house is warm.
  • Turn the main water back on slowly, then look under sinks and around the water heater for drips. Run each faucet for a moment.
  • If water won't flow from a faucet and it's very cold, a pipe may be frozen. Don't use an open flame to thaw it. The Red Cross guide in the sources below explains safe ways to thaw a pipe, and when to call a plumber.
  • Look at ceilings and floors for new stains.

The short version

Before a winter trip: have someone check on the house, keep the heat on and no lower than 55 degrees, put fresh batteries in the thermostat, and decide about the main water valve. Disconnect hoses, clear outside vents, unplug decorations and heaters, test your alarms, put lamps on timers and lock everything. When you get home, turn the water back on slowly and look for leaks.
