How Often to Change Your Furnace Filter (and How to Do It)

How often to change a furnace filter, how to find the right size, which way the arrow points, what MERV means, and the signs it's time for a new one.

Of all the jobs on a house, changing the furnace filter is one of the smallest. It takes about five minutes, needs no tools, and you can do it in your socks. It's also one of the jobs people forget the longest.

A filter that's been in there since last winter makes your heating system work harder to pull air through it. That can mean rooms that don't warm up as well, a system that runs longer than it should, and more dust in the house. It's also one of the first things a heating technician will look at when something seems off.

So let's answer the question properly: how often should you change it, how do you pick the right one, and how do you put it in the right way around?

How often: the short answer

There isn't one number that fits every house, because filters and homes are different. But the general guidance is simple.

  • Check it every month during heating season, and during cooling season too if you have central air on the same system. The U.S. Department of Energy recommends checking filters regularly and cleaning or replacing them when they're dirty.
  • Thin filters (about one inch thick) are usually changed every one to three months. Many filter makers print a suggested interval on the package. Treat it as a maximum, not a promise.
  • Thicker media filters (four or five inches) usually last longer. Follow the interval from the maker of your system or filter.

Then adjust for your house. You'll likely need to change it more often if:

  • You have pets that shed.
  • Someone in the house is bothered by dust.
  • You're doing renovation work, sanding or sawing indoors.
  • The system runs a lot, like during a cold snap.
  • You live on a dusty road or near construction.

The honest rule is: when it looks dirty, change it. The calendar just reminds you to look.

The light test

Here's the easiest way to tell. Slide the filter out and hold it up to a bright light or a window.

  • You can see light through it: it's probably fine for now. Slide it back in the same way.
  • You can barely see light, or it's gray and furry: time for a new one.

A new filter is usually white or a light color. Compare the old one to a new one and the difference is often obvious. If it's caked with dust after just a few weeks, that's worth knowing too. It may mean you need to check it more often, or that there's a lot of dust coming from somewhere.

Where to find the filter

Most homes have the filter in one of these places:

  • In a slot on the furnace or air handler, usually where the big return duct meets the unit. There's often a small cover or door.
  • Behind a return air grille in a wall, ceiling or floor. These are the larger vents that pull air in, not the ones that blow warm air out. The grille may have clips or a couple of screws.
  • In both places. Some systems have more than one filter. If you find one, look around for another.

If you can't find it, look in the furnace manual, or ask your heating technician to show you at the next service visit. It's a good question to ask when you buy a house, too.

How to find the right size

The size is printed on the edge of the filter frame, something like 16 x 25 x 1. The three numbers are width, height and thickness in inches.

  • Write the size down somewhere you'll find it: on the furnace itself with a marker, on a note in your phone, or in your home maintenance notes.
  • Match it exactly. A filter that's too small leaves gaps, and air takes the easy way around it. Then the filter isn't filtering much at all.
  • Buy a few at a time. If you have spares in the closet, the job gets done. If you have to go to the store, it waits another month.

Which way the arrow points

Every filter has an arrow printed on the frame. It shows the direction the air flows.

The arrow points toward the furnace (or air handler), away from the return duct the air comes from. Think of it as pointing in the direction the air is going.

Put in backwards, a filter may not work as well as it should. If you're not sure, look at how the old one came out before you remove it, and take a quick photo.

What MERV means, and which to pick

Many filters have a MERV rating on the package. MERV stands for Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value. According to the EPA, it runs from 1 to 16, and a higher number means the filter catches more of the small particles in the air.

That sounds like higher is always better. It isn't always. A filter that catches more can also be harder for air to pass through, and not every home system is designed for that. The EPA suggests checking with your system's manual or an HVAC professional to find out the highest efficiency filter your system can handle.

A sensible approach:

  • Start with what the manual or your technician recommends.
  • If you want better filtering, ask your technician whether your system can handle a higher-rated filter before you switch.
  • Whatever you choose, change it on time. A clogged filter of any rating isn't helping.

Washable or reusable filters exist too. If you have one, follow the maker's cleaning instructions and let it dry completely before it goes back in.

How to change it, step by step

  1. Turn the system off at the thermostat. You don't want the blower pulling dust in while the filter is out.
  2. Open the cover or grille and slide the old filter out slowly, so the dust stays on it.
  3. Note the size and the arrow direction on the old one.
  4. Slide the new filter in with the arrow pointing toward the furnace. Make sure it sits flat and fills the slot.
  5. Close the cover. Some furnaces won't run if the door isn't closed properly.
  6. Write today's date on the edge of the new filter with a marker. Next month, you'll know exactly how long it's been in.
  7. Turn the system back on.

Put the old filter in a bag before you carry it through the house. It's dustier than it looks.

Signs the filter is overdue

Besides the light test, the house sometimes tells you:

  • Rooms take longer to warm up than they used to.
  • Weaker airflow from the vents.
  • More dust on furniture than usual.
  • The system runs longer or turns on and off often.

A dirty filter isn't the only cause of these, but it's the easiest one to check, so check it first. If a new filter doesn't help, our guide to furnace warning signs explains what to look at next and when to call a pro.

Filter vs. service visit

Changing the filter is your job. Most other work on the furnace isn't. The Department of Energy recommends having heating systems checked by a professional, and many people schedule that once a year, before the heating season.

  • You: check and change the filter, keep the area around the furnace clear, keep vents and returns open and unblocked.
  • A heating technician: everything inside the cabinet, burners, gas connections, the flue and venting, electrical parts, and any strange noises or smells.

If you ever smell gas, leave the house and call your gas company or 911 from outside. If a carbon monoxide alarm goes off, get everyone out and call 911. Our smoke and CO alarm guide covers both.

The short version

Check the furnace filter every month during heating season and change it when it looks dirty. For a thin filter, that's often every one to three months; thicker filters last longer. Match the size exactly, point the arrow toward the furnace, write the date on the frame, and keep a few spares at home. Ask your manual or technician before switching to a higher MERV rating. And leave everything inside the furnace to a pro.
