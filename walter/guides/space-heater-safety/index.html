Space Heater Safety: A Plain Checklist Before You Plug One In

Space heater safety in plain English: the three-foot rule, where to plug it in, what to look for when you buy one, and the habits that matter most.

Every fall the same thing happens. The first cold night shows up, one room never quite warms up, and a space heater comes out of the closet. That's fine. A space heater is a handy tool. But it's also one of the few things in a house that gets very hot, sits on the floor next to blankets and curtains, and often runs while nobody is watching it.

NFPA's heating safety guidance boils down to a few rules, and they're easy to remember. Keep anything that can burn at least three feet away. Plug it straight into a wall outlet. Turn it off when you leave the room or go to bed. If you do just those three things, you've covered most of it.

Here's the plain version, plus what to check before you plug one in this year.

The three-foot rule

Draw an imaginary circle around the heater, three feet in every direction. Nothing that can burn goes inside that circle. That means:

  • Curtains and drapes
  • Bedding, blankets and throws on the back of a couch
  • Clothes drying on a chair
  • Paper, magazines, boxes and holiday decorations
  • Rugs that can curl up toward the heater

Kids and pets count too. NFPA recommends a three-foot "kid-free zone" around space heaters and other heat sources. A heater in a busy hallway or right next to a play area is in the wrong spot.

Before you turn it on, stand where the heater will sit and look around slowly. Most problems are obvious once you actually look.

Where and how to plug it in

This is the rule people break the most, usually without knowing it.

  • Plug it directly into a wall outlet. NFPA's guidance is clear on this: not an extension cord, not a power strip. Space heaters pull a lot of power for a long time, and cords and strips aren't always built for that.
  • One heater per outlet, and think about the circuit. If the same circuit also runs a hair dryer, a microwave or another heater, breakers may trip. A breaker that trips often is telling you something; have an electrician look at it rather than just resetting it again and again.
  • Feel the plug and outlet after the heater has run for a while. Warm can be normal for some heaters. Hot, discolored, melted or smelling of burning plastic is not. Turn it off, unplug it and have the outlet checked.
  • Don't run the cord under a rug. You can't see damage there, and heat can't escape.

If the house is older, it's worth a moment more thought. Older wiring wasn't designed for a house full of modern appliances. If you're not sure what's in your walls, our guides on aluminum wiring and knob-and-tube wiring explain what to ask about.

What to look for when you buy one

You don't need the fanciest model. You do want a few safety features:

  • Automatic shut-off if it tips over. A tip-over switch turns the heater off if it gets knocked down.
  • Overheat protection. It shuts the heater off if it gets too hot inside.
  • A label from a recognized testing laboratory. That shows the design was tested to a safety standard.
  • A cord in good shape. Check older heaters every season. A frayed, cracked or kinked cord means it's time to stop using it.

Read the instructions that come with it. They'll tell you where it's meant to be used and how close it can sit to walls and furniture, and some heaters aren't meant for bathrooms or damp spaces.

Habits that matter more than the heater

A good heater used carelessly is still a risk. These habits are where most of the safety comes from:

  1. Turn it off when you leave the room. Even for a few minutes. "I'll be right back" is how heaters get forgotten.
  2. Turn it off when you go to bed. If a bedroom is too cold to sleep in without a heater running all night, that's a sign to look at the furnace, the ducts or the drafts instead. Our nightly home safety routine includes this check.
  3. Set it on a hard, level floor. Not on a table, a bed or a stack of books, where it can tip.
  4. Keep it out of walkways where people trip over the cord at night.
  5. Use it for spot heating, not as the house's heating system. If you need space heaters in several rooms just to stay warm, something bigger needs attention. Start with our guide on furnace warning signs.

Fuel-burning space heaters and carbon monoxide

Electric heaters don't make carbon monoxide. Heaters that burn fuel, like kerosene or propane, can. That changes the rules.

  • Use only heaters that are meant for indoor use, exactly as the instructions say, and only with the fuel they're made for.
  • Never use an outdoor or camping heater inside, and never use a grill, a camp stove or a generator indoors or in a garage. CPSC warns about carbon monoxide from these again and again.
  • Have working CO alarms outside sleeping areas and on every level. Our smoke and CO alarm guide covers where they go.
  • Refuel outside, with the heater off and cooled down.

If a CO alarm goes off, get everyone outside to fresh air and call 911 or the fire department from outside. Don't go back in to look for the cause.

The oven is not a heater

It sounds obvious, but cold snaps and power outages make people try it. NFPA and the U.S. Fire Administration both say not to use an oven or stovetop to heat your home. It wasn't designed for that, and with a gas range it can also add carbon monoxide to the air.

If the power goes out in winter, Ready.gov's winter weather advice is a good place to start: layers of clothing, blankets, closing off rooms you don't need, and going somewhere warm if the house gets too cold.

Before you put it away in spring

A short end-of-season check saves you a surprise next fall:

  • Unplug it and let it cool completely.
  • Wipe off dust from the grille, where the maker's instructions allow. Dust that sits on heating parts can smell when you turn it on again.
  • Look over the cord and plug one more time.
  • Store it somewhere dry, standing upright, with the cord loosely wrapped, not kinked.

Next fall, give it the same look before the first cold night.

When to call someone

You can handle most space heater safety yourself. Call a licensed electrician if:

  • A breaker trips over and over when the heater runs.
  • An outlet or plug gets hot, discolored or smells like burning.
  • Lights dim or flicker a lot when the heater kicks on.

And if you're relying on space heaters because the furnace can't keep up, have a heating professional check the system. That's usually the real fix.

The short version

Three feet of clear space all around. Plug it straight into the wall. Off when you leave the room, off when you go to bed. A tip-over switch and a testing-lab label when you buy one. CO alarms if anything in the house burns fuel. That's space heater safety, and it takes about a minute to check.
