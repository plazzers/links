Buying a House in Winter: What the Snow Can Hide

Buying a house in winter? What snow and cold can hide, what winter shows you better than summer, and the questions to ask before you make an offer.

House hunting in winter has a reputation for being hard. Short days, cold showings, fewer houses on the market. But there's a good side too: there are often fewer buyers, and sellers who list in winter usually want to sell.

The catch is that a house in winter shows you some things very clearly and hides others completely. Snow covers the roof and the yard. Frozen ground hides drainage problems. A furnace running full time tells you a lot, and an air conditioner that can't be tested tells you nothing.

Here's what to look for, what to ask about, and how to plan for the things you can't see until spring.

What snow and cold can hide

Write these down before your first showing. These are the things you'll likely need to ask about, look at in photos, or leave room for in your plans.

  • The roof. Under snow, nobody can see missing shingles, worn spots or flashing problems. Your inspector may not be able to walk it or see much of it, and the report will usually say so. Ask the seller how old the roof is and whether there are records.
  • Grading and drainage. In summer you can see whether the soil slopes away from the house. Under snow, you can't. Frozen ground also means water may not be soaking in or pooling the way it does in a spring rain.
  • The foundation from the outside. Snow piled against the house hides the lower walls, cracks and the spot where soil meets siding.
  • The yard and outbuildings. Fences, a shed, the driveway surface, trees close to the house. Snow makes all of it look tidy.
  • The air conditioner. Many inspectors won't run central air when it's cold outside, because it can damage the unit. Your report may say the AC wasn't tested.
  • Outdoor faucets, irrigation and pools. Often winterized and shut off, so they can't be checked.

None of this is a reason to skip a winter house. It's a reason to ask more questions and read the report's "not inspected" section carefully.

What winter shows you better

Winter is also the best time of year to learn some things about a house.

  • How the heating works. The furnace or boiler is running hard. Listen to it. Feel whether every room gets warm, including the bedrooms over the garage and the rooms on the north side. Ask the age of the system and when it was last serviced. Energy.gov's furnace and boiler guide explains the main types you'll see.
  • Drafts and cold rooms. Hold the back of your hand near windows, outlets on outside walls and exterior doors. A cold room in January is a cold room every January. Our guide to finding drafts in your house shows what to feel for.
  • Condensation and moisture. Look for water droplets or frost on the inside of windows, damp corners and musty smells. A little condensation on very cold days is common. A lot, plus stains or mold, points to moisture worth asking about. The EPA's mold and moisture guide explains why moisture control matters.
  • Ice dams and icicles. Big icicles and thick ridges of ice at the edge of the roof can point to heat escaping into the attic. Look from the ground and ask about it.
  • Snow melt patterns. On a snowy roof, bare patches while neighbors' roofs are still white can be a sign of heat loss through the attic. It's a clue, not a verdict.
  • Frozen pipe history. Ask the seller directly: have any pipes ever frozen? Where? What was done about it?

Questions to ask the seller

You can ask these through your agent. Write down the answers.

  1. How old are the roof, furnace or boiler, water heater and AC? Are there service records?
  2. Have any pipes frozen or burst? Where, and what was repaired?
  3. Has water ever come into the basement or crawlspace? When does it happen, if it does?
  4. Are there ice dams on the roof in a typical winter?
  5. Do you have summer photos of the house, the yard and the roof?
  6. What are the typical winter utility bills? Can you share a year of them?
  7. Was any work done with permits, and can you share the paperwork?

Summer photos are underrated. Many sellers have them on their phone, and listing photos from a previous sale can show the yard and the roof without snow. Our list of questions to ask the seller has more.

Make the inspection count

A general home inspection looks at the parts of the house that are visible and accessible on the day. The InterNACHI standards of practice, which many inspectors follow, describe what an inspector is and isn't expected to do. In winter, that means some things may be listed as not inspected because of snow, ice or the weather.

  • Go to the inspection if you can, and ask what the inspector couldn't see or test because of the season.
  • Read the limitations section of the report as carefully as the problems section.
  • Ask about specialists for the things that matter most for this house: a roofer, an HVAC technician, a sewer camera inspection, or a structural engineer for foundation concerns.
  • Test for radon if it's common in your area. The EPA's home buyer's guide to radon explains testing during a sale. Winter, with windows closed, is a normal time to test.

For a fuller picture of what inspections cover, see what a home inspection covers.

Planning for what you can't see yet

Some things simply can't be checked until spring. You have a few ways to handle that, and your agent or a real estate attorney can tell you what's normal where you live:

  • Ask for records and photos to fill the gaps.
  • Ask for a second look at a specific item once the snow melts, if the timing of the sale allows it.
  • Talk to your agent about options in the contract for items that couldn't be inspected. What's possible depends on your state and the deal.
  • Plan for the roof and the AC in your budget if they're old and couldn't be checked. Older systems are fine to buy. Surprises are what hurt.

A winter showing checklist

Take this on your phone:

  • Every room warm? Any cold rooms or cold floors?
  • Furnace or boiler age and service sticker?
  • Condensation, frost or stains on the inside of windows?
  • Musty smell in the basement or crawlspace?
  • Big icicles or ice ridges at the roof edge?
  • Water stains on ceilings, especially top floor and under bathrooms?
  • Snow piled against the foundation? Ask what's under it.
  • Summer photos available?

The short version

Buying a house in winter is a good way to learn how warm, dry and well heated it really is. It's a bad time to see the roof, the yard and the drainage. Ask more questions, get summer photos, read what the inspector couldn't check, and plan for those items instead of hoping.

Calm, careful buyers do well in winter. The house is telling you a lot. You just have to listen to the right parts.
