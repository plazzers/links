Water Heater Warning Signs (and How to Tell Its Age)

Water heater warning signs to watch for, how to read its age from the label, and what to look at on the relief valve before you call a plumber.

The most common water heater warning signs are rust at the bottom of the tank, water on the floor around it, rusty or cloudy hot water, popping and rumbling sounds, and hot water that runs out much faster than it used to. Any one of these is worth a closer look. Two or three together on an older tank usually mean it's time to start planning a replacement.

The other big clue is age. A standard tank water heater commonly lasts somewhere around 8 to 12 years. Tankless units often last longer. Age alone doesn't mean failure, but an old tank with warning signs is one you don't want to ignore, because the way they tend to fail is by leaking, sometimes a lot, sometimes all at once.

Here's how to read the signs and the label safely, and when to hand it to a plumber.

How to figure out your water heater's age

Start with the label on the side of the tank. It's usually a sticker with the brand, model number, capacity and serial number.

  • Look for a printed date first. Many labels show a manufacture date in plain words.
  • If there's no date, look at the serial number. Most makers hide the date in it, often as a letter or digits for the month and two digits for the year. The pattern is different from brand to brand, so don't guess. Look up the brand's serial number guide on the manufacturer's website, or ask a plumber, who usually reads them at a glance.
  • Check paperwork. Receipts, the original manual or a permit record can give you the install date, which is the one that really matters.

Take a phone photo of the label so you don't have to squeeze behind the tank again. Keep the label clear when you can; a faded or painted-over label makes this harder later.

If you're buying a house, ask the seller how old the water heater, furnace and AC are. Those three ages tell you a lot about what's coming. Our questions to ask the seller guide has the full list.

Warning signs you can spot yourself

You can check all of these by looking and listening. Keep your hands off hot pipes and the burner area.

  • Rust at the base of the tank. Rust on the bottom or around the seams is usually the beginning of the end for a tank. Corrosion on the pipe connections on top is also worth noting.
  • Water on the floor. A small puddle can come from a fitting, the relief valve or the tank itself. If it's the tank, it won't fix itself.
  • Rusty or discolored hot water. If only the hot side is rusty, the tank may be corroding inside.
  • Popping, rumbling or banging. Usually sediment cooking on the bottom of the tank. It's a maintenance issue at first, but it makes the heater work harder and can shorten its life.
  • Less hot water than before. Running out fast, or water that never gets as hot, can mean sediment, a failing part or a tank near the end.
  • A soot mark or scorching near the burner on a gas unit, or a vent pipe that's loose or disconnected on top. Treat these as urgent, because a bad vent can let exhaust gases into the house.

The relief valve: look, don't test if you're unsure

Every tank water heater has a temperature and pressure relief valve, usually called the T&P valve. It's a safety device that opens if the tank gets too hot or the pressure gets too high. You'll find it on the top or upper side of the tank, with a lever and a pipe running from it.

What you can safely check by looking:

  • There is a discharge pipe connected to the valve.
  • The pipe points down, running toward the floor or a drain, not sideways at someone's face.
  • The end of the pipe is open, not capped, plugged or threaded into something.
  • No dripping, crusty white or green deposits, or rust at the valve or the end of the pipe. Those suggest it has been leaking.

Many manufacturers recommend testing the valve now and then by lifting the lever briefly with a bucket under the pipe. But the water that comes out is scalding, and on an older heater the valve sometimes won't close properly afterward and keeps dripping. If you're not sure about any of it, leave the lever alone and have a plumber test it during a service visit. A valve that drips, or has no discharge pipe, is a plumber call.

Maintenance that helps it last

  • Have it flushed. Sediment settles in the bottom of the tank. A yearly flush, especially with hard water, can keep it quieter and more efficient. I'd let a plumber do it on an older tank, since old drain valves can break or fail to close.
  • Keep the area clear. No boxes, paint cans or laundry piled near a gas water heater. It needs air to burn safely.
  • Watch the temperature. Around 120°F at the tap is a common target that saves energy and lowers the risk of scalding, which matters most with kids or older adults in the house. Energy.gov's water heating pages cover this and more.
  • Glance at it regularly. A quick look for rust or drips every few months is part of a good monthly house walk.
  • In earthquake areas, check that the tank is strapped to the wall. Many areas require it.

Tankless heaters have their own needs. Mineral buildup slowly chokes them, so they usually need descaling, more often with hard water. That's typically a plumber's job.

When to call a plumber

Call a licensed plumber when you see:

  • Water coming from the tank itself, or rust at the base.
  • A T&P valve that drips, leaks, or has no proper discharge pipe.
  • A gas vent pipe that's loose, disconnected or rusted through. Have a plumber or HVAC technician check it soon.
  • Rusty hot water, loud banging, or hot water that has dropped off sharply.
  • A tank in the second half of its expected life, so you can plan a replacement before it fails.

If you ever smell gas near the water heater, don't flip switches or try to find the source. Leave the house and call your gas company from outside.

Bottom line

  • Tank water heaters commonly last around 8 to 12 years; find the date on the label or decode the serial number with the manufacturer's guide.
  • Rust at the base, water on the floor and rusty hot water are the warning signs that matter most.
  • Look at the relief valve and its pipe, but leave testing to a plumber if you're unsure.
  • An old tank with warning signs is easier to replace on your schedule than after a flood.
