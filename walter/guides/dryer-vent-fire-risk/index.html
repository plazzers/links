Dryer Vent Cleaning: Why It Matters and Signs You're Overdue

Dryer vent cleaning explained: why lint buildup is a fire risk, signs your vent is clogged, what you can check yourself, and when to call a pro.

Dryer vent cleaning means clearing the lint that builds up inside the duct that runs from the back of your dryer to the outside of the house. The lint screen catches most of it, but not all. Over time the rest packs into the duct, and lint burns easily. Fire safety groups like NFPA point to failure to clean as a leading cause of home dryer fires.

The usual advice is to have the vent duct cleaned about once a year, and sooner if your clothes start taking longer to dry. On top of that, clean the lint screen every single load. It's one of the cheapest, simplest fire-prevention habits in the house.

Here's how to tell if your vent is overdue, what you can safely check yourself, and when to bring in a dryer vent cleaning service.

Why lint in the vent is a fire risk

A dryer makes heat and pushes hot, damp air out through the duct. When lint narrows that duct:

  • Hot air can't escape as easily, so heat builds up inside the dryer and the duct.
  • The dryer runs longer to get clothes dry, which means more hours of heat.
  • Lint itself is light, dry and quick to catch fire.

Put those together and you have a real risk, and it's one that builds slowly where you can't see it. Gas dryers add one more concern: a blocked vent can push exhaust, including carbon monoxide, back into the house.

Signs your dryer vent needs cleaning

Watch for:

  • Clothes take longer to dry, or need a second cycle.
  • Clothes or the dryer top feel very hot at the end of a cycle.
  • The laundry room feels hot and humid while the dryer runs.
  • A burning smell when the dryer is on. Stop using it and get it checked.
  • The outside flap doesn't open when the dryer is running, or barely any air comes out.
  • Lint around the outside vent opening, or lint collecting behind the dryer.
  • It's been more than a year, or you don't know when it was last done.

What you can check yourself

These are safe, simple checks. Unplug an electric dryer before moving it; for a gas dryer, be very careful not to bend or strain the gas line when pulling it out, or leave this part to a pro.

  • Clean the lint screen every load. Before or after each load. If it looks clogged even when clean, dryer sheets can leave a film. Wash it with warm soapy water, rinse and let it dry fully before putting it back.
  • Check the outside vent. With the dryer running, go outside. The flap should open and you should feel warm air flowing out. If the flap is stuck, or air is weak, the duct may be clogged or crushed.
  • Clear the outside opening. Remove lint, leaves, nests or snow you can reach from the ground. Don't put a screen over the dryer vent; it traps lint.
  • Look behind the dryer. The duct should be short, as straight as possible, and not crushed or kinked behind the machine.
  • Check what the duct is made of. NFPA recommends rigid or flexible metal venting. White plastic or thin foil accordion-style duct is a red flag; it crushes easily and traps lint.
  • Keep the area clear. No piles of clothes, boxes or cleaning products around the dryer.

DIY cleaning vs. a dryer vent cleaning service

Some homeowners clean a short, simple vent themselves with a brush kit made for dryer ducts. If your duct is a few feet long, runs straight out a wall at ground level, and you're comfortable doing it, that can be reasonable.

I'd call a dryer vent cleaning service when:

  • The duct is long or has several bends.
  • It runs through the attic, through a closet or up to the roof. Don't climb on the roof to clean a roof vent.
  • You find a crushed, disconnected or plastic duct inside a wall or ceiling.
  • You've cleaned it and drying is still slow.
  • You have a gas dryer and anything about the vent looks wrong.

A good service will clean the full length of the duct, check the outside termination, and tell you if the duct itself should be replaced. If the duct inside a wall needs replacing, a contractor or HVAC technician may be the right call.

Safe dryer habits

A few habits from fire safety groups that are worth adopting:

  • Don't run the dryer when you're asleep or away from home. If something goes wrong, you want to be there to notice.
  • Don't dry items with oil, gasoline or cleaning solvents on them, even after washing. Follow the label, and line-dry them if in doubt.
  • Don't overload the dryer.
  • Have a working smoke alarm nearby, and if you have a gas dryer, a CO alarm in the home. Our smoke and CO alarm guide covers placement.
  • Have gas dryers checked by a qualified technician to make sure the gas line and connection are tight and not leaking.

Make it part of your routine

The easiest way to keep up with this is to tie it to the calendar.

  • Every load: lint screen.
  • Every few months: check the outside flap while the dryer runs.
  • Once a year: full vent duct cleaning, sooner if drying slows down.

The lint screen fits nicely into a nightly home safety routine, and the annual cleaning belongs on your home maintenance calendar. Early fall is a good time, before the heavy-sweater season.

When to call a pro

Call a dryer vent cleaning service, or an appliance or HVAC technician, when:

  • You smell burning or see scorch marks.
  • The dryer shuts itself off from overheating.
  • The vent runs to the roof, through an attic or is very long.
  • The duct is plastic, foil, crushed or disconnected.
  • You have a gas dryer and smell gas. In that case, leave the house and call the gas company from outside.

The short version

  • Lint that builds up in the vent duct is a fire risk, and you usually can't see it.
  • Clean the lint screen every load; have the duct cleaned about once a year.
  • Slow drying, hot clothes and a flap that doesn't open are the warning signs.
  • Short, simple vents can be a DIY job; long, roof or attic runs are for a pro.
