Winterize Your House Checklist: What to Do Before the First Freeze

A plain-English winterize your house checklist: pipes, faucets, heating, roof, gutters and alarms to check before the first freeze, and when to call a pro.

Winterizing a house mostly comes down to three things: keep water out, keep pipes from freezing, and make sure the heat runs safely. You don't need special tools or a free weekend. Most of this list is looking, listening and a few simple jobs you can finish in an afternoon.

The best time to do it is a few weeks before the first hard freeze, not the night the forecast drops below 32°F. Once it's cold and dark at 5 p.m., nobody wants to be outside disconnecting hoses or poking around in a crawlspace.

Below is the checklist I'd work through, in the order I'd do it. For each item you'll see what you can safely check yourself and when it's time to call a licensed pro.

Outside water: hoses, faucets and sprinklers

Outdoor plumbing is the number one thing that bites people in the first cold snap.

  • Disconnect every garden hose. A hose left attached holds water in the faucet. When it freezes, it can split the pipe inside the wall — and you often don't find out until spring, when you turn it on and water runs into the basement.
  • Shut the indoor valve for each outdoor faucet if your house has one (often in the basement, near where the pipe goes through the wall). Then open the outside faucet so any trapped water can drain out.
  • Frost-free faucets still need the hose removed to work as designed.
  • Sprinkler systems usually need to be blown out with compressed air. That's a job for an irrigation company — too much pressure can damage the lines.

If an outdoor faucet drips after you shut it off, or you can't find an indoor shut-off at all, a plumber can sort it out before the freeze.

Pipes in cold spots and the main shut-off

Pipes freeze where they run through unheated space: crawlspaces, garages, attics and outside walls.

  1. Walk the cold spots with a flashlight. Look for exposed pipes and note anything without insulation.
  2. Add foam pipe sleeves to exposed pipes you can reach easily. This is a simple, low-risk job.
  3. Seal obvious drafts near pipes, like a gap where a pipe goes through a rim joist or a crawlspace vent that won't close.
  4. Find the main water shut-off and make sure you can turn it. If a pipe ever bursts, this is the valve that stops the flood. If it's stiff or crusted, don't force it — ask a plumber to look at it.

On the coldest nights, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls so warm room air can reach the pipes, and let a faucet drip slightly. Ready.gov's winter weather guidance covers this, along with what to do if you're away from home.

Call a plumber if you see heat tape that looks damaged or old, pipes with green crust or active drips, or if a pipe has frozen before. Never thaw a frozen pipe with an open flame.

Heating: the most important safety check

Before you need the heat every day, turn the thermostat up and walk through the house. Every room should warm up, and the system shouldn't make new banging, squealing or rattling noises.

What you can do yourself:

  • Check the furnace filter and replace it if it's dirty. A clogged filter makes the whole system work harder.
  • Clear the area around the furnace or boiler. No boxes, paint cans or laundry piled near it.
  • Look, don't touch. Soot, rust, a burning smell or a yellow, lazy flame on a gas appliance are all reasons to call someone.
  • Keep vents and returns clear of furniture and rugs.

What belongs to a pro: a yearly heating tune-up by an HVAC technician. They check the parts you shouldn't open yourself, like the burners and heat exchanger. Book it early in the fall, before the first cold week when everyone calls at once. If you use a fireplace or wood stove, have the chimney inspected and swept by a certified chimney sweep.

NFPA's heating safety guidance also recommends keeping anything that can burn at least three feet away from space heaters and other heat sources.

If you ever smell gas, don't flip switches. Leave the house and call the gas company from outside.

Smoke and CO alarms

Winter is when furnaces, fireplaces and space heaters all run at once, so this matters more now than any other time of year.

  • Press the test button on every smoke alarm and every carbon monoxide alarm.
  • Check the date printed on the back. NFPA says smoke alarms should be replaced every 10 years. CO alarms have their own replacement timeline printed on the unit.
  • Make sure you have CO alarms near sleeping areas if you have any gas appliances, a fireplace or an attached garage.

For more on placement and testing, see the smoke and CO alarm guide.

Roof, gutters and drainage

Winter rain and snowmelt need somewhere to go, and that somewhere should be away from your foundation.

  • Look at the roof from the ground, with binoculars if needed. You're looking for missing, curled or lifted shingles and loose flashing around the chimney and vents. Don't climb up to check — a roofer can do that safely.
  • Clean the gutters after the leaves are down. If you're comfortable on a ladder on level ground, it's a reasonable DIY job. If the roof is high or steep, hire a gutter service. There's more on this in the fall gutter and drainage guide.
  • Check downspouts empty several feet from the house, not right at the foundation.
  • Clear basement window wells of leaves so they don't hold water against the glass.

In cold climates, watch for thick ice building along the roof edge once winter arrives. That's an ice dam, and it can push meltwater under the shingles. Use a roof rake from the ground or call a roofer — stay off a snowy roof.

Drafts, attic and the outside shell

Small gaps let in cold air, water and mice. Energy.gov's air-sealing guidance points to the usual leak spots: around windows and doors, where pipes and wires go through walls, and at attic openings.

  • Look for daylight around closed doors. If you see it, the weatherstripping is worn.
  • Check caulk around windows and trim. Cracked or missing caulk is an easy fix when it's still mild enough for caulk to set.
  • Peek into the attic with a flashlight. Insulation should look even, and the vents shouldn't be blocked. Frost on the underside of the roof boards later in winter means warm, damp air from the house is getting up there.
  • Seal small gaps where mice could get in. They're looking for a warm place right now.

If you see old fluffy gray-brown or pebbly vermiculite insulation in an older home, don't disturb it. Some of it can contain asbestos; have it tested by a professional before anyone works up there.

Be ready for a power outage

Winter storms knock out power. A little prep goes a long way:

  • Flashlights and fresh batteries where you can find them in the dark.
  • Water, a few days of food, and any medicines you need.
  • Shovels and ice melt before the first storm.
  • If you own a generator, run it only outdoors, well away from doors and windows. Never in a garage, even with the door open. Exhaust contains carbon monoxide.

The short version

  • Disconnect hoses and drain outdoor faucets before the first freeze.
  • Insulate pipes in cold spots and know where your main shut-off is.
  • Change the furnace filter and book a yearly heating tune-up with an HVAC pro.
  • Test every smoke and CO alarm.
  • Keep water moving away from the house, and leave roof work to a roofer.

Once winter is behind you, the spring home maintenance checklist picks up where this one leaves off.
