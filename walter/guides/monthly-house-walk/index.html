Monthly Home Maintenance Checklist: The 10-Minute House Walk

A monthly home maintenance checklist you can finish in 10 minutes: a simple house walk that catches leaks, cracks and safety problems early.

Most expensive house problems start small. A drip under the sink. A hairline crack that gets a little wider. A gutter that overflows at one corner. A musty smell in the basement you stopped noticing. None of them are emergencies on day one, but leave them alone for a year and they turn into rotted floors, wet basements and big repair bills.

The fix isn't a weekend project. It's a 10-minute walk through your house once a month, looking at the same spots each time. You're not repairing anything. You're just noticing — so you catch the small stuff while it's still small.

Here's the route I'd take. Pick a day you'll remember, like the first Saturday of the month, and keep the walk the same every time. Familiar is good: you'll notice changes faster.

Why a monthly walk works

The trick is repetition. When you look at the same pipe, the same ceiling corner and the same patch of foundation every month, a new stain or a new drip jumps out at you. If you only look once a year, you have nothing to compare against.

A monthly walk also catches the problems that get expensive fast:

  • Slow leaks that rot cabinets and subfloors.
  • Moisture in the basement that leads to mold. The EPA's mold guidance is clear that fixing the water source is the key.
  • Clogged gutters spilling water at the foundation.
  • Worn caulk around tubs and showers letting water behind the tile.
  • Dead smoke alarms.

Minutes 1–3: the outside

Take a slow lap around the house.

  • Gutters and downspouts: anything sagging, overflowing or disconnected? Do downspouts still empty away from the house?
  • Foundation: any new cracks, or old ones that look wider? Any thin mud tubes running up the wall? (Those can mean termites — call a pest control company.)
  • Ground: puddles or soggy spots next to the house after rain?
  • Roof, from the ground: missing shingles or anything hanging loose? Don't climb up — a roofer can check it.
  • Siding and trim: new peeling paint, soft wood or holes.
  • Outdoor AC unit: clear of leaves and weeds.

Minutes 4–6: kitchen, bathrooms and laundry

This is where water lives, so this is where leaks start.

  • Open the cabinets under every sink with a flashlight. Look for drips, and touch the cabinet floor. Soft, swollen or stained wood means a slow leak.
  • Toilets: does any run on its own between flushes? Does one rock when you sit on it? A rocking toilet can leak at the base. The EPA's WaterSense program notes that a running toilet is one of the most common household leaks.
  • Tub and shower caulk: cracked or missing caulk lets water behind the tile.
  • Ceilings under bathrooms: any new stains?
  • Washing machine hoses: any bulges, cracks or damp spots?
  • Dryer: clean the lint screen (you should do this every load anyway), and make sure the area behind the dryer isn't full of lint.

Minutes 7–8: basement, crawlspace and mechanicals

Head down with a flashlight.

  • Smell first. Musty means moisture.
  • Floor and walls: look for damp spots, puddles, or white chalky deposits on the walls.
  • Water heater: any water around the base or rust on the bottom of the tank? That's an early warning worth a call to a plumber. The water heater warning signs guide covers what to look for.
  • Furnace or air handler: check the filter. If it's gray and clogged, change it.
  • Electrical panel: look and listen only. Scorch marks, buzzing, a warm panel or a burning smell mean call an electrician right away. Never take the cover off.
  • Sump pump: is the pit dry or does the pump look ready? Test it every few months — see how to test a sump pump.
  • Crawlspace: look in from the hatch for standing water. Don't crawl in if you see hanging wires or animals.

Minutes 9–10: safety checks

Finish with the two jobs that matter most for your family.

  1. Press the test button on each smoke and CO alarm. NFPA recommends testing smoke alarms at least once a month. If one doesn't beep, replace the battery or the unit.
  2. Press TEST and RESET on the GFCI outlets in the kitchen, bathrooms, garage, laundry and outside. The CPSC's GFCI fact sheet recommends testing them monthly. If one won't trip or won't reset, call an electrician.

While you're at it, glance at the fire extinguisher gauge — the needle should be in the green.

Keep a simple log

Write down what you find, even when it's nothing. A note like "small stain on basement ceiling, under upstairs bath — dry" is worth a lot six months later when you're trying to figure out if it's growing.

  • Take a photo of anything you're watching, from the same angle each time.
  • Note the date you changed the furnace filter.
  • Keep a short "call someone" list for things that need a pro.

When to stop watching and call a pro

The monthly walk is for looking, not fixing. Call a professional when you see:

  • Water where it shouldn't be that you can't trace to an easy source — a plumber or waterproofing contractor.
  • Cracks that grow, horizontal foundation cracks, or doors that suddenly stick — a structural engineer.
  • Anything electrical that's warm, buzzing, sparking or smells hot — a licensed electrician.
  • Rust or water at the base of the water heater — a plumber.
  • A gas smell — leave the house and call the gas company from outside.

Make it stick

The monthly walk only works if you actually do it. Tie it to something you already do, set a phone reminder, and keep the route the same. After two or three months it stops feeling like a chore and starts feeling like checking the mail.

Want the bigger picture? Pair this with the home maintenance calendar by month, which spreads the seasonal jobs across the year, and the nightly home safety routine for the two-minute version before bed.

The short version

  • Once a month, walk the same route: outside, wet rooms, basement, safety.
  • Look under every sink and around every toilet.
  • Change the filter when it's dirty.
  • Test smoke alarms, CO alarms and GFCIs.
  • Write down what you see, and call a pro when something changes.
