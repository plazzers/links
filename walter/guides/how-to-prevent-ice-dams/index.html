How to Prevent Ice Dams: Causes, Warning Signs and Safe Fixes

How to prevent ice dams: why they form, the warning signs inside and out, what you can safely do from the ground, and long-term fixes to ask a pro about.

An ice dam is a ridge of ice that builds up along the bottom edge of a roof, right above the gutters. Behind it, melting snow has nowhere to go. The water backs up under the shingles, and shingles are made to shed water running downhill, not to hold back a puddle. That's when you get a brown stain on the ceiling near an outside wall, wet insulation in the attic, or paint bubbling high on a wall in the middle of winter.

The ice on the edge of the roof is the symptom. The cause is almost always inside the house. If you want to know how to prevent ice dams, the short answer is to keep the roof deck cold, and that mostly comes down to stopping warm air from getting into the attic.

Why ice dams form

You need three things at once:

  1. Snow on the roof.
  2. A warm spot on the roof deck, warm enough to melt the bottom of that snow even when it's below freezing outside.
  3. A cold roof edge, the eaves, where the roof hangs out past the warm walls of the house.

Snow melts on the warm part of the roof, the water trickles down under the snow, and when it reaches the cold overhang it freezes again. Day after day, the ice gets thicker. Eventually it's tall enough to hold back a pool of water, and that water finds its way under the shingles.

So where does the warm roof come from? Usually from the house itself:

  • Warm air leaking into the attic through gaps around light fixtures, attic hatches, plumbing and wiring holes, chimneys and the tops of interior walls. Warm air rises, and it will find every gap.
  • Not enough insulation on the attic floor, or insulation that's thin in spots, so heat moves straight up.
  • Ventilation that isn't working, often because insulation is pushed against the soffit vents, so the attic can't stay close to the outdoor temperature.
  • Heat sources in the attic, like ducts that leak or aren't insulated, or a bathroom fan that dumps warm, damp air into the attic instead of outside.

Our guide to attic ventilation basics explains how intake and exhaust vents are supposed to work together.

A clogged gutter can make the ice build up faster and heavier at the edge, but clean gutters alone won't stop ice dams. The heat is the root of it.

The warning signs

Outside, from the ground

  • Big icicles hanging from the gutters or roof edge. A few small ones are normal. A thick row of large icicles, especially on a house where they keep coming back, is a hint that heat is reaching the roof.
  • A ridge of ice along the eaves, sometimes with a band of bare, melted roof just above it.
  • Patchy snow on the roof. After a snowfall, look at the roof from across the street. Bare or melted spots while the neighbors' roofs are still white often show where heat is escaping.

Inside the house

  • Stains or wet spots on the ceiling near exterior walls, especially on the top floor.
  • Peeling paint or bubbling high on outside walls, or water around window frames upstairs.
  • Damp or matted insulation if you look in the attic from the hatch, or frost or water drops on the underside of the roof boards.
  • A musty smell in the attic or upstairs closets. Moisture that sticks around can lead to mold, and EPA's moisture guide says the key is to find and fix the water source and dry things quickly.

If you see stains during a thaw, take photos and note where they are for the roofer or contractor.

What you can safely do this winter

This is where people get hurt, so let's be clear about the line.

Do not climb onto an icy or snowy roof. Not to shovel, not to check, not to chip. A roof in winter is one of the most dangerous places around a house, and a ladder on ice isn't much better.

Do not chip at ice dams with a hammer, axe, ice pick or shovel. You'll damage the shingles and the gutters, and a chunk of ice coming off a roof edge is heavy enough to hurt someone. Don't use a torch or open flame on ice either.

What you can do from the ground:

  • Use a roof rake, carefully. A roof rake is a long-handled tool that lets you pull the bottom few feet of snow off the roof while you stand on the ground. Less snow at the edge means less meltwater feeding the dam. Pull gently, work from the side so snow doesn't land on you, and leave a thin layer rather than scraping down to the shingles.
  • Look up before you rake. Check for power lines, including the service drop that runs to your house. Metal poles and wires don't mix. If there are lines anywhere near where you'd be working, skip that part of the roof.
  • Keep paths clear under the eaves. Ice and snow can come off the roof without warning. Keep people, pets and parked cars out from under big icicles, and clear walkways where water drips and refreezes.
  • Keep downspout outlets open so meltwater can drain away from the house instead of freezing at the foundation.
  • Watch the ceilings near exterior walls during thaws, and put a towel or bucket under any drip while you call for help.

Ready.gov's winter weather guidance is a good general reminder of cold-weather safety, including avoiding overexertion when clearing snow. Roof raking is hard work; if you aren't up to it, hire it out.

Long-term fixes to talk over with a pro

The real prevention happens in the attic, usually in the warmer months when it's safe and comfortable to work up there. These are the fixes to discuss with an insulation contractor, an energy auditor or a roofer:

  1. Air sealing the attic floor. Energy.gov's air sealing guidance explains how sealing the gaps between the living space and the attic keeps warm air where it belongs. Common spots include around recessed lights, the attic hatch, plumbing vents, wiring holes and the chimney. Sealing near chimneys and flues needs the right fire-safe materials, which is one reason to have someone experienced do it.
  2. Adding or evening out insulation. Energy.gov's insulation pages explain how insulation slows heat moving up into the attic. Air sealing usually comes first, then insulation on top.
  3. Fixing ventilation. Making sure soffit vents aren't blocked and there's a path for air to flow from the eaves to the peak helps keep the roof deck cold. Baffles at the eaves can hold insulation back from the vents.
  4. Venting bath and kitchen fans outdoors, not into the attic, and sealing and insulating any ductwork that runs through the attic.
  5. Roof-edge protection at the next reroof. When a roof is replaced, a roofer can explain what local code calls for along the eaves to resist water backing up. That's a decision for the roofer and your local building rules.

Heat cables along the roof edge can help in specific trouble spots, but they don't fix the cause and must be installed per the manufacturer's instructions. Ask an electrician or roofer whether they make sense for your roof.

If your house is older, it may have less insulation or more air leaks than a newer one. Our What to Check in a House Built in… tool lists items to look at for your home's era.

When to call a roofer

Call a roofer, or a roofing company that handles ice dam removal, when:

  • Water is coming into the house. That's the moment to get help, not to wait for spring.
  • The ice dam is large and you can't reach the snow safely from the ground.
  • Gutters are pulling away from the house under the weight of ice.
  • You see stains in the same spots every winter. A repeating leak means the cause hasn't been fixed.
  • Shingles look damaged, lifted or missing after the ice melts.

Ask how they'll remove the ice before they start, and avoid anyone who plans to chip it off with hand tools.

After a leak, have wet insulation and drywall looked at. Insulation that stays wet doesn't work well, and wet materials that don't dry out can grow mold.

A simple plan for the season

  • In fall: clean the gutters and check the downspouts (our fall gutter and drainage guide walks through it), peek into the attic from the hatch for blocked vents or thin insulation, and work through the winterize your house checklist.
  • After each big snow: look at the roof from the ground for bare patches and growing icicles. Rake the bottom edge if you can do it safely.
  • In spring or summer: if you had ice dams, book an energy audit or have an insulation contractor look at air sealing and insulation while the attic is comfortable to work in.

The short version

Ice dams form when heat from the house melts snow on the roof and the water refreezes at the cold edge. The lasting fix is to keep the roof deck cold: seal the air leaks into the attic, insulate well, and let the attic breathe. This winter, stay off the roof, don't chip the ice, rake from the ground if it's safe, and watch for power lines. If water comes inside, call a roofer, and make a note to fix the cause before next winter.
