Sewer Scope Inspection: What It Is and When to Get One

A sewer scope inspection puts a camera down the line from house to street. What it finds, when to get one before buying, and how to read the results.

The sewer line is the pipe that carries everything from your drains out to the city sewer or a septic tank. It's buried, it's out of sight, and on most houses nobody has looked at it in years. A sewer scope inspection fixes that: a trained plumber or inspector feeds a small waterproof camera down the line and records what's inside — cracks, roots, sags, breaks and blockages.

It's usually not part of a standard home inspection, which is visual and doesn't cover underground pipes. That's why a sewer scope is one of the most useful add-ons for buyers, especially with an older house or big trees in the yard. A sewer line repair is one of the bigger surprises a new owner can face, and a camera is one of the few ways to see it coming.

How a sewer scope works

The process is simple and non-destructive:

  1. The technician finds an access point — usually a cleanout, a capped pipe that sticks up in the basement, crawlspace, yard or near the foundation. Sometimes they go in through a roof vent or by pulling a toilet.
  2. They push a flexible cable with a camera and light on the end down the line toward the street or septic tank.
  3. They watch a live feed and record video, noting the distance to anything worth mentioning.
  4. Afterward, you get a report and, ideally, the video file.

Ask to watch the screen while it runs, and ask the technician to talk you through what you're seeing. Always ask for a copy of the video — another plumber can review it later if you need a second opinion.

What the camera can find

  • Tree roots that have worked into joints, from small hair-like roots to heavy masses that block the line
  • Cracks and breaks in the pipe wall
  • Offset joints, where one pipe section has shifted relative to the next
  • Bellies or sags, low spots where the line has settled and water and waste collect
  • Collapsed sections in older pipe
  • Heavy buildup of grease, scale or debris
  • Pipe material changes — for example, older clay or cast iron connected to newer plastic, which tells you whether part of the line was already replaced
  • Corrosion or flaking inside older cast-iron pipe

Not every finding is urgent. A small amount of buildup or a minor root intrusion may just need cleaning and monitoring. A collapse or a major break is a different conversation. The technician's report should say which is which.

When a sewer scope makes the most sense

I'd strongly consider one when:

  • The house is older. Many older homes have clay, cast-iron or other older-material sewer lines that crack, separate at joints and let roots in over decades. The What to Check in a House Built in… tool flags this for older homes.
  • There are large trees between the house and the street.
  • You see warning signs — slow drains throughout the house, gurgling toilets, sewage smells, a wet or unusually green patch in the yard, or water backing up into a floor drain.
  • The house has been vacant for a while, so problems may not have shown up yet.
  • The seller mentions past backups, root cleaning or partial repairs.

Even on a newer house, it can be worth asking about. Construction debris, a poorly connected joint or a damaged line can show up at any age.

What you can check yourself

You can't see inside the sewer line, but you can look for hints at a showing or in your own home:

  • Run water in several fixtures and listen for gurgling in nearby drains or toilets.
  • Watch how sinks and tubs drain. One slow drain is usually a local clog. Several slow drains on the lowest level is more interesting.
  • Look at the basement floor drain for staining or debris around it.
  • Notice smells near floor drains or outside near the cleanout.
  • Walk the yard between the house and the street for soggy or sunken spots.
  • Find the cleanout, so you know where access is.

Don't open a cleanout that might be under pressure from a backup, and don't run rented drain machines into a line you don't own. If you notice backups, stop using water and call a plumber.

Reading the results

When the report comes back, ask:

  • What material is the pipe, and how old does it appear?
  • Where exactly are the problems, and how far from the house? This matters because, in many areas, the homeowner is responsible for the line up to a certain point, and the city for the rest. Your local utility can tell you where that line falls.
  • What does the technician recommend — cleaning and monitoring, a spot repair, lining, or replacement?
  • Is this urgent, or something to plan for?

If the scope shows serious problems, talk to your agent about your options before your inspection contingency ends. Get a licensed plumber or sewer contractor to quote the fix, and consider a second opinion on major findings.

How it fits with the rest of your inspection

A general home inspection covers the plumbing that's visible inside the house — supply pipes, drains, fixtures, the water heater — but not the buried line to the street. My guide to what a home inspection covers explains the gaps. Some inspectors offer a sewer scope as an add-on; others will refer you to a plumber or a specialist.

If the house has a septic system rather than a city sewer, that needs its own septic inspection. A camera can still help on the line between the house and the tank, but it won't evaluate the tank or drain field.

Sewer backups also bring moisture problems with them. The EPA's mold guidance notes that water damage should be cleaned and dried quickly, and areas affected by sewage call for extra care. If a basement has been through a backup, it's worth asking how it was cleaned. See signs of water in a basement for what to look for.

Bottom line

A sewer scope inspection sends a camera through the one pipe nobody ever sees. It's not part of a standard home inspection, but it's worth adding for older homes, homes with big trees, and any house with slow drains or past backups. Watch the video, ask for a copy, and get a licensed plumber's opinion on anything serious before you close.
