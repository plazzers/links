Fireplace Safety Checklist: What to Check Before the First Fire

A plain fireplace safety checklist for fall: the chimney inspection, the damper, dry wood, screens, ashes, CO alarms and the habits that matter most.

The first cold evening of fall, somebody always says it: "Let's light a fire." It's one of the nicest things about owning a house with a fireplace. But a fireplace that sat unused all summer deserves a few minutes of attention before that first match.

A wood fire is a real fire inside your house. The chimney has to carry smoke, heat and gases safely out, and a lot can change in a year: birds build nests, mortar cracks, and creosote from last winter is still stuck to the inside of the flue.

Here's a plain checklist for wood-burning and gas fireplaces, split into what you can look at yourself and what belongs to a chimney professional.

Start with the chimney inspection

This is the one item you can't do yourself, and it matters most.

NFPA's heating safety guidance says chimneys and vents should be inspected every year by a qualified professional and cleaned as needed. Once a year, whether you used the fireplace a lot or hardly at all. A chimney that wasn't used can still have a nest, a cracked flue liner or loose bricks at the top.

Why it matters:

  • Creosote. Burning wood leaves a dark, tar-like residue inside the chimney. It builds up over time and it can burn. A chimney fire can start inside the flue where you can't see it.
  • Blockages. Nests, leaves and debris can block the flue, so smoke and gases come back into the room instead of going up.
  • Damage you can't see from below. Cracks in the flue liner or the mortar can let heat reach the wood framing around the chimney.

Book the inspection early. Chimney sweeps get busy once the weather turns, which is why our home maintenance calendar puts it in late summer. If you're reading this in October, call this week, and hold off on fires until it's done.

What you can check yourself

While you wait for the sweep, take a flashlight and a few minutes:

  • Open and close the damper. It should move freely and close fully. If it's stuck, rusted or won't close, mention it at the inspection. A damper that won't close is also a big winter draft, as our guide on how to find drafts in your house explains.
  • Look up the flue with the damper open, if you can do it safely. You're looking for daylight and obvious debris, not trying to judge the liner.
  • Look at the firebox. Cracked or missing firebricks, gaps in the mortar joints or crumbling pieces are worth pointing out to the pro.
  • Walk outside and look at the chimney top from the ground. Leaning, missing bricks, a missing cap, or heavy dark staining on the outside are all things to mention. Don't climb on the roof to look closer.
  • Check the hearth and the area around it. The hearth extension in front of the fireplace should be solid and uncracked.
  • Check the screen or glass doors. A sturdy metal screen or glass doors should be in place to keep sparks and rolling logs inside.

If anything looks off, write it down and show the inspector. Those notes help.

Burn the right wood

What you burn changes how much smoke and creosote you get. EPA's Burn Wise program has simple advice:

  • Burn only dry, seasoned wood. Wet or green wood smokes more, burns poorly and leaves more residue in the chimney. EPA suggests a moisture meter, which is an inexpensive tool, and splitting and stacking wood so it dries before you burn it.
  • Store firewood off the ground, covered on top, with the sides open so air can move through. Keep the stack away from the house walls, too, so it doesn't invite pests or hold moisture against the siding.
  • Never burn trash, cardboard, wrapping paper, plastic, painted or treated wood. They can burn too hot, throw sparks or give off harmful smoke.
  • Never use gasoline, lighter fluid or other flammable liquids to start or boost a fire. Use dry kindling or fire starters made for fireplaces.

Small, hot fires are better than one giant one. Add wood a little at a time.

The three-foot rule applies here too

Same as with a space heater, keep anything that can burn at least three feet away from the fireplace. That includes:

  • Furniture, rugs and pillows
  • Firewood waiting to be burned (keep just a small amount inside)
  • Holiday decorations, stockings, garland and paper
  • Newspapers and kindling bags

Kids and pets need a clear zone too. The glass doors and the screen get very hot, and so does the front of many gas fireplaces. A safety gate or barrier can help if little ones are in the house. Our space heater safety guide covers the same habits for portable heaters.

Ashes are hotter than they look

This one surprises people. Ashes can hold heat long after the fire looks dead, sometimes for days.

NFPA's guidance for ashes:

  1. Let them cool before you scoop them out.
  2. Put them in a metal container with a tight lid.
  3. Keep that container outside, at least 10 feet from the house and anything else that can burn.

Not in a paper bag. Not in a plastic bucket. Not on the deck or in the garage. A cardboard box of "cold" ashes in a garage is a classic way fires start.

Carbon monoxide: alarms and a working draft

Any fire that burns fuel makes carbon monoxide, which you can't see or smell. A fireplace is designed to send it up the chimney, but a blocked flue, a closed damper or a weak draft can push it back into the room.

  • Open the damper before lighting, and leave it open until the fire is completely out and the ashes are cold.
  • Have working CO alarms outside sleeping areas and on every level of the house. Our smoke and CO alarm guide covers where to put them and how to test them.
  • If smoke keeps coming into the room, put the fire out safely and have the chimney checked before you use it again.
  • If a CO alarm sounds, get everyone outside to fresh air and call 911 or the fire department from outside. CPSC is clear on this: don't go back in to find the cause.

Gas fireplaces need a check too

Gas fireplaces and gas logs are easier, but they're not maintenance-free.

  • Have them inspected and serviced by a qualified technician before the season, following the maker's instructions.
  • Glass fronts get very hot. Keep kids and pets back, and use the safety screen if your model came with one.
  • Never burn wood in a fireplace made only for gas, and don't add anything to gas logs that the maker didn't approve.
  • If you smell gas, don't light anything and don't flip switches. Get everyone out and call the gas company or 911 from outside.
  • Keep the vent outside clear of snow, leaves and plants, just like a furnace vent.

Habits every time you light a fire

Once the chimney is checked, the daily habits are where most of the safety comes from:

  1. Open the damper first.
  2. Screen or doors in place before the fire gets going.
  3. Never leave a fire burning when you leave the house or go to bed.
  4. Keep a fire extinguisher nearby and know how to use it.
  5. Keep the three-foot circle clear.
  6. Ashes cool, then into the metal can, then outside.

These six habits take less time than building the fire.

When to call a pro

Look yourself: the damper, the firebox, the screen, the chimney top from the ground, the wood and the ashes.

Call a qualified chimney professional or technician for: the yearly inspection and cleaning, any cracks in the flue or firebox, a damper that won't work, smoke coming into the room, and servicing any gas fireplace. If a fire ever seems to roar up inside the chimney, or you hear a loud rumbling or see sparks and flames shooting from the top, get everyone out and call 911.

The short version

Inspection once a year, before the first fire. Dry wood only, never trash or flammable liquids. Damper open, screen closed, three feet clear. Never leave it burning when you leave or go to bed. Ashes in a lidded metal can, outside, ten feet from the house. Working CO alarms on every level. That's a safe fireplace season.
