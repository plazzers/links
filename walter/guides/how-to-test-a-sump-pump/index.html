How to Test a Sump Pump (the Safe Bucket Test)

How to test a sump pump with a simple bucket of water, what a healthy pump should do, warning signs to watch for, and when to call a plumber.

The easiest way to test a sump pump is the bucket test. Slowly pour a bucket of water into the sump pit until the float rises. A healthy pump should switch on by itself, pump the water out, and shut off again when the level drops. The whole thing takes a few minutes.

If the pump doesn't start, hums without moving water, or runs and runs without shutting off, you've found a problem on a dry day instead of in the middle of a storm. That's the whole point.

I'd do this test at least twice a year: once in early spring, and once before your rainy season. Here's how to do it safely, what to watch for, and when to call a pro.

What a sump pump does

A sump pump sits in a pit (the sump) in the lowest part of a basement or crawlspace. Groundwater and water from drain tiles around the foundation collect in that pit. When the water rises, a float switch turns the pump on, and the pump pushes the water up through a discharge pipe and out of the house.

It's quiet, out of sight, and easy to forget. That's why it's often the last thing anyone checks until the basement is wet. If you've seen signs of water in the basement, the sump pump is one of the first things I'd look at.

Safety before you start

Water and electricity are a bad mix, so a few rules:

  • Keep your hands out of the pit while the pump is plugged in. If you need to touch the pump or float for any reason, unplug it first, and only if the plug and outlet are dry and easy to reach.
  • Don't stand in water to reach the outlet. If the floor around the pit is wet, call a plumber or electrician instead.
  • Don't try to pull the pump out of the pit or take it apart. That's a job for a pro.
  • Use a flashlight, not a lamp with a cord dangling near the water.

The bucket test, step by step

  1. Find the pump and its power. Note whether it's plugged into an outlet and that the cord isn't damaged. Some homes have two pumps (a main pump and a backup); test each.
  2. Look into the pit. It should be fairly free of mud, gravel and debris. Note anything that could jam the float.
  3. Pour slowly. Fill a bucket with water and pour it gently into the pit. Watch the float rise. You may need a second bucket if the pit is large.
  4. Watch it switch on. The pump should start on its own once the float reaches its set point.
  5. Watch it empty the pit. The water level should drop steadily, not barely move.
  6. Watch it shut off. When the water drops, the float lowers and the pump should stop. A short thump as it stops is common; that's often the check valve closing.
  7. Check outside. Have someone stand by the discharge outlet outside, or go check right after. You should see water coming out.

That's it. If all of that happened, your pump passed.

Warning signs to watch for

These are the signs that mean the pump needs attention:

  • It doesn't start at all. Could be power, a stuck float or a failed motor.
  • It hums but doesn't pump. Often a jam or a failed impeller.
  • It runs without stopping, or keeps running after the pit is empty. A stuck float switch is a common cause.
  • It cycles on and off very often even in dry weather, which can mean a check valve problem or a float set too tight.
  • Grinding, rattling or loud noises during the test.
  • Water flows back into the pit after the pump stops, which points to a failed check valve.
  • Rust, or water leaking from the pipe joints.
  • Age. Sump pumps commonly last somewhere around 7 to 10 years, sometimes less if they work hard. An older pump is one to plan to replace before it fails.

Don't forget the discharge pipe

A pump that works perfectly is still no use if the water comes right back.

  • The discharge should end well away from the house, so water drains away from the foundation instead of soaking back toward it.
  • The outlet should be clear. Check it for leaves, ice, dirt or crushed pipe.
  • In cold climates, the outside part of the line can freeze. Make sure it drains freely and isn't buried in snow.

Water that leaves the pit and then runs back along the foundation is a common reason basements stay damp. Our fall gutter and drainage guide covers the rest of the outside drainage picture.

Backup power and alarms

A sump pump usually needs power exactly when storms knock the power out. A few things to think about:

  • A high-water alarm is an inexpensive add-on that beeps if the water rises too high. Some plug in; some run on batteries.
  • A battery backup pump can keep pumping during an outage. It needs its own battery checks.
  • A water-powered backup uses city water pressure instead of electricity in some homes.
  • Generators must run outside, well away from doors and windows, never in a basement or garage.

A plumber or waterproofing contractor can tell you which backup makes sense for your house. Ready.gov's flood and power-outage pages are good reading before storm season.

When to call a plumber

Call a licensed plumber or a waterproofing contractor when:

  • The pump fails the bucket test in any way.
  • The float sticks, or the pump runs constantly.
  • You see water coming up through the floor or around the pit, or the pit overflows.
  • The pump is old and you want it replaced before the next wet season.
  • You want to add a backup pump or an alarm.
  • There's any electrical concern near the pit: a damaged cord, a wet outlet, or a breaker that keeps tripping. That's an electrician.

The short version

  • Pour a bucket of water into the pit slowly. The pump should start, empty the pit and shut off.
  • Test in early spring and before your rainy season, and check that the outside discharge is clear.
  • Keep hands out of the pit while the pump is plugged in, and don't work near wet outlets.
  • If it fails, runs nonstop or is getting old, call a plumber before the next storm does it for you.
