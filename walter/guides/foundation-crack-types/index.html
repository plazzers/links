Foundation Crack Types: Which Ones to Worry About

Foundation crack types explained in plain English: hairline, vertical, stair-step and horizontal cracks, which are normal, and when to call an engineer.

Almost every foundation has a crack somewhere. Concrete shrinks as it dries, houses settle a little in their first years, and soil moves with the seasons. So the question isn't "is there a crack?" — it's "what kind of crack is it, and is it changing?"

Here's the plain-English version. Thin vertical cracks are usually the least worrying. Stair-step cracks in block or brick, cracks where one side sticks out past the other, and especially horizontal cracks are the foundation crack types I'd want a structural engineer to see. And any crack that lets water in matters, whatever its shape.

This guide helps you sort what you're looking at. It can't tell you whether a specific wall is sound — only a qualified professional standing in front of it can do that.

Why foundations crack at all

A few common causes are behind most cracks:

  • Shrinkage. Poured concrete loses moisture as it dries and hardens, and it often cracks a little in the process.
  • Settlement. The soil under a new house compacts over time. Small, even settling is normal; uneven settling is where trouble starts.
  • Soil pressure. Wet or frozen soil pushes sideways on basement walls. Clay soils swell when wet and shrink when dry.
  • Water. Poor grading, overflowing gutters and short downspouts put water right against the foundation, which feeds both pressure and leaks.

That last cause is the one you can often influence yourself, which is good news.

Hairline and vertical cracks

What they look like: Thin, roughly straight up-and-down lines in a poured concrete wall, often running from the top of the wall partway down, or near the corners of basement windows.

What they usually mean: Shrinkage or minor settling. These are the most common foundation crack type and often aren't a structural concern.

When to pay closer attention:

  • The crack is wider than a credit card is thick
  • One side of the crack sits further out than the other
  • It's wider at the top than the bottom (or the reverse)
  • It leaks, has damp edges, or shows white chalky deposits around it
  • It's clearly getting longer or wider over time

Even a harmless vertical crack can let in water and soil gas. The EPA lists cracks in floors and walls among the ways radon can get into a house, so a cracked basement is one more reason to test.

Diagonal cracks

What they look like: Cracks running at an angle, often from a corner of the wall or from the corner of a window opening.

What they usually mean: One part of the foundation settling more than another. A small, old, stable diagonal crack may not be a big deal. A wide one, or one paired with sticking doors and sloping floors upstairs, suggests movement worth investigating.

Inside the house, look for the same pattern: diagonal cracks in drywall running from the corners of doors and windows. They can come from ordinary seasonal movement — or from a foundation that's still moving.

Stair-step cracks in block and brick

What they look like: A zig-zag that follows the mortar joints in a concrete block or brick wall, like a little staircase.

What they usually mean: Settlement or pressure. Mortar is the weakest part of the wall, so movement shows up there first.

A short, thin stair-step crack in an old house may have been there for decades. But stair-step cracks that are wide, that run a long way, that have bricks or blocks shifted out of line, or that show up alongside a bowing wall are a call for a structural engineer. Check the outside brick too — stair-step cracks on an exterior wall deserve the same attention.

Horizontal cracks and bowing walls

What they look like: A crack running sideways along a basement wall, often somewhere around the middle height or near the frost line. In block walls, it often follows a mortar joint. Sometimes the wall above or below the crack leans or bows inward.

What they usually mean: Soil on the outside pushing harder than the wall was built to resist. This is the foundation crack type I'd worry about most.

Stand at one end of the wall and sight down its length. It should look straight. If it bulges into the room, if there are horizontal cracks, or if you see braces, steel beams or wall anchors that suggest a past repair, have a structural engineer look before you buy. If there was a repair, ask for the paperwork — who did it, when, and whether it came with an engineer's report.

Quick comparison

Crack type Often caused by Concern level Who to ask
Hairline vertical Shrinkage as concrete dries Usually low Watch it; seal if it leaks
Wider vertical, offset Settling Medium Structural engineer
Diagonal Uneven settling Low to medium Structural engineer if wide or active
Stair-step (block/brick) Settling or pressure Medium to high Structural engineer
Horizontal / bowing Soil pressure High Structural engineer

Treat this as a rough guide, not a diagnosis. The same crack can mean different things in different houses.

What you can check yourself

You can learn a lot without tools:

  • Look at the outside first. Does the ground slope away from the house? Do downspouts end several feet out? Is water pooling against the wall? Fixing drainage is often the first and best step.
  • Mark and date cracks. On a house you own, a pencil line at each end of a crack, with the date written next to it, tells you within a few months whether it's growing.
  • Take photos with a coin or ruler next to the crack for scale.
  • Check the rest of the house for sloping floors, doors that suddenly stick, and diagonal drywall cracks.
  • Look for water at the crack: damp edges, stains, efflorescence or rust marks. See signs of water in a basement for what to look for.

What not to do: don't chip into a crack to "see how deep it goes," don't patch it right before listing or buying, and don't rely on a coat of paint or sealant to settle the question.

When to call a structural engineer

A general home inspector will note cracks and recommend follow-up, but they're doing a visual inspection, not an engineering evaluation (more in what a home inspection covers). Call a structural engineer when you see:

  • Any horizontal crack, or a wall that bows or leans
  • Stair-step cracks that are wide or long
  • Cracks where one side is offset from the other
  • Cracks that are growing
  • Cracks paired with sloping floors, sticking doors or sagging roof lines
  • Signs of a previous foundation repair with no paperwork

If you're buying, an engineer's opinion before closing is far better than a surprise after. A waterproofing contractor is the right call for a leak at a minor crack — but let the engineer speak first if there's any sign of movement.

Bottom line

Thin vertical cracks are common and usually minor; horizontal cracks, bowing walls and wide stair-step cracks are not. Watch for water, offset edges and cracks that grow. When there's any sign of movement, get a structural engineer's opinion — that's not a job for guesswork.
