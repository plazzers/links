Buying an Older Home Checklist: 7 Things to Check

A buying an older home checklist for houses built before 1990: wiring, panels, pipes, sewer line, lead paint, asbestos and radon — and who to call.

Older homes have a lot going for them: solid framing, mature trees, real character. They also have materials and systems that were normal when they were built and are worth a closer look today. If the house you're considering was built before about 1990, a few specific items deserve attention beyond the usual walk-through.

This buying an older home checklist covers seven of them: wiring, the electrical panel, water pipes, the sewer line, lead paint, asbestos, and the basics every house needs, like radon testing and the age of the roof and equipment. For each one, you'll see what you can safely look at yourself and when to call a licensed pro. None of these is an automatic dealbreaker — the point is to know what you're buying.

Start with the year it was built

The age of the house tells you which items are most likely. A 1920s house and a 1980s house have very different lists. Find the build year in the listing or the property records, and note any major renovations. Then plug it into the What to Check in a House Built in… tool for a list tailored to that era.

Keep in mind the dates below are rough ranges. Materials were used earlier and later in different places, and many older homes have been partly updated.

1. Old wiring: knob-and-tube and cloth-covered wire

Homes wired before roughly the 1950s may still have knob-and-tube wiring — single wires run on ceramic knobs and through ceramic tubes, with no ground wire. The old insulation can get brittle, and it was never designed to be buried in insulation or carry today's loads. Some insurers ask about it.

Look yourself: In an unfinished basement or attic, look for ceramic knobs on the joists or fabric-wrapped wires. Count the two-prong outlets. Don't touch or handle the wiring.

Call a pro: A licensed electrician can tell you how much is still live. Check with your insurer before closing. More in knob-and-tube wiring.

2. Aluminum branch wiring

Some homes built roughly from the mid-1960s to the mid-1970s were wired with aluminum instead of copper. The CPSC has long warned that connections at outlets and switches can overheat over time. There are accepted repair methods.

Look yourself: Look for "AL" or "ALUMINUM" printed on visible cable jackets in the basement or attic. Warning signs include warm outlet covers, flickering lights and a burning smell.

Call a pro: Ask whether the connections have already been repaired, and get a licensed electrician's opinion before you close. See aluminum wiring in homes.

3. The electrical panel and service size

Certain older panel brands — mainly Federal Pacific Electric (FPE) "Stab-Lok" and Zinsco — have known concerns about breakers that may not trip when they should. They were common in homes built from roughly the 1950s through the 1980s. Older homes may also have small electrical service, or a fuse box.

Look yourself: Read the brand name on the panel door label. Never remove the panel cover. Note any scorch marks, buzzing or burning smell.

Call a pro: Ask your inspector to note the brand in the report, and have a licensed electrician evaluate it if it's one of those names.

4. Water supply pipes

Two older pipe types come up often:

  • Galvanized steel, common in homes built roughly before the 1960s. It rusts from the inside over time, which can mean low pressure, discolored water and leaks. A fridge magnet sticks to it; it won't stick to copper.
  • Polybutylene, a gray (sometimes blue or black) flexible plastic used roughly from the late 1970s to the mid-1990s. It has a history of leaks, and some insurers ask about it. It's often stamped "PB2110."

Look yourself: Check where the water line enters the house and at the water heater. Run two taps at once and watch the pressure.

Call a pro: A licensed plumber can tell you what's there and whether it should be replaced.

5. The sewer line

Older homes often have cast-iron drains and a clay or similar older-material sewer line to the street. Over decades, roots, cracks and sags are common — and they're buried.

Look yourself: Slow drains on the lowest level, gurgling toilets, and soggy spots in the yard are hints.

Call a pro: A sewer scope inspection sends a camera down the line. It's usually not part of a general home inspection, so book it separately, especially if there are big trees out front.

6. Lead paint (homes built before 1978)

Homes built before 1978 may have lead-based paint, often under newer layers. It's mainly a hazard when it's peeling, chipping, or disturbed by sanding, scraping or renovation — the dust and chips are the risk, especially for young children and pregnant women.

In the US, the EPA's disclosure rule requires sellers of pre-1978 homes to disclose known lead-based paint hazards, provide available records, and give buyers an opportunity to test, typically a 10-day period unless you agree otherwise.

Look yourself: Note peeling paint on windows, sills, doors, porches and railings. Don't sand or scrape anything.

Call a pro: A certified lead inspector or risk assessor can test. Renovation contractors working in pre-1978 homes should be lead-safe certified.

7. Asbestos-containing materials

Many homes built before the 1980s used products that may contain asbestos: some floor tiles, pipe and duct wrap, popcorn ceilings, and vermiculite attic insulation. According to the EPA, material that's in good condition and left alone is generally less of a concern; it's damage, sanding or removal that releases fibers.

Look yourself: Note suspect materials without touching them. Vermiculite looks like loose, pebbly gray-brown or gold granules.

Call a pro: Only a lab test can confirm asbestos. Plan for testing before any renovation, and leave removal to trained abatement professionals. See asbestos in older homes.

And the items every house needs

Older or not, every house deserves these:

  • Radon test. The EPA recommends testing during a home sale and fixing the home at 4 pCi/L or higher.
  • Roof age. Ask for it. Asphalt shingle roofs commonly last roughly 20 to 30 years.
  • Water heater and HVAC age. Check the data plates.
  • Foundation and signs of water. Look for stains, musty smells and cracks — anything that suggests movement deserves a structural engineer.

The short version

  • Know the build year; it tells you what to look for.
  • Wiring, panels, pipes and the sewer line are the big system questions in older homes.
  • Pre-1978 means possible lead paint; pre-1980s means possible asbestos — don't disturb, test.
  • Look and note yourself, then bring in a licensed electrician, plumber or specialist before you close.
