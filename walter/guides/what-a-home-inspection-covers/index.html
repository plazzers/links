What Does a Home Inspection Cover (and What It Doesn't)

What does a home inspection cover? The systems a general inspector looks at, why it's visual and non-invasive, what's usually excluded, and what to add.

A general home inspection is a visual, non-invasive look at the readily accessible parts of a house — the roof, outside, foundation and structure, heating and cooling, plumbing, electrical, attic, insulation, doors, windows and interior. The inspector looks, operates normal controls, and reports what appears to be defective or near the end of its life.

What it doesn't do is just as important. An inspector won't open walls, move the seller's furniture, dig up the yard, or test for radon, mold, lead or asbestos as part of a standard inspection. Sewer lines, wells, septic systems, pools and pest inspections are usually separate. Knowing the difference helps you plan the add-ons that matter for your house.

The details below follow the general shape of the InterNACHI Standards of Practice, one of the widely used industry standards. Your inspector's contract and your state's rules may differ, so read the agreement before inspection day.

What "visual and non-invasive" means

This is the heart of it. A home inspector:

  • Looks at what can be seen and safely reached, without removing finishes or taking things apart.
  • Operates normal controls — turning on the furnace at the thermostat, running faucets, flipping switches.
  • Doesn't open walls, ceilings or floors, or cut into anything.
  • Doesn't move personal belongings, furniture, stored items, rugs or debris that block the view.
  • Doesn't predict the future. An inspection is a snapshot of that day's visible condition, not a promise about how long anything will last.
  • Doesn't check code compliance in most cases. A home inspection is about condition and safety concerns, not a building code review.

So if a stain is hidden behind a bookcase or a leak only shows up in a heavy rain, the inspector may not see it. That's not a flaw in the inspection — it's the nature of a visual one.

What a home inspection usually covers

Under most standards, the inspector looks at these areas and describes what they see:

Area What's typically looked at
Roof Roof covering, drainage, flashing, skylights, chimneys and penetrations, from the roof or from where it's safe to view
Exterior Siding, trim, eaves and soffits, doors, windows, walkways and driveways, stairs, decks, porches and railings, grading and drainage near the house
Foundation and structure Foundation, basement and crawlspace, visible framing, signs of movement or water
Heating Heating system operated with normal controls, visible parts and vent pipes
Cooling Cooling system operated with normal controls, when the outside temperature allows
Plumbing Water supply and drain pipes that are visible, fixtures and faucets, water heater, main shut-off location, sump pump
Electrical Service drop, panel (an inspector may remove the panel cover where safe), a sample of outlets and switches, GFCI and AFCI devices, and whether smoke and CO alarms are present
Fireplace Visible parts of fireplaces and chimneys
Attic and insulation Insulation, ventilation, visible roof framing and sheathing, where safely accessible
Doors, windows and interior Walls, ceilings, floors, stairs and railings, a sample of doors and windows, garage door operation

The inspector will usually note the type and condition of each system, the materials used, and anything that looks unsafe, not working, or worth a specialist's evaluation.

What's usually excluded

These are the gaps buyers are most often surprised by. A standard home inspection typically does not include:

  • Anything hidden behind walls, under floors, underground or under snow, and anything the inspector can't safely access
  • Sewer lines from the house to the street
  • Wells and septic systems, including water quality testing
  • Environmental testing — radon, mold, lead, asbestos, water quality, air quality
  • Pest inspection — wood-destroying insects are often a separate inspection, sometimes required by lenders
  • Pools, spas, irrigation systems and outbuildings (often optional)
  • Life expectancy of systems or the cost of repairs
  • Engineering opinions on structure, or the adequacy of an HVAC design
  • Code compliance, permits or zoning
  • Low-voltage systems, security systems, solar, smart-home equipment and most appliances beyond basic operation

Some inspectors offer extras like radon testing or a sewer scope for an additional fee. Ask what's included before you book.

Add-ons worth considering

Which extras make sense depends on the house:

  • Radon test. The EPA recommends testing during a home sale and fixing the home if the level is 4 pCi/L or higher. It's often the most important add-on.
  • Sewer scope, especially for older homes or homes with big trees. See sewer line camera inspection.
  • Wood-destroying insect inspection, particularly where termites are common.
  • Well and septic inspection if the home isn't on public water and sewer.
  • Lead paint testing for homes built before 1978, and asbestos testing before any renovation in older homes. Don't disturb suspect materials yourself.
  • Specialist follow-ups for anything the inspector flags: a structural engineer for foundation movement, a licensed electrician for panel or wiring concerns, a roofer for roof questions, an HVAC technician for older equipment.

If you're looking at an older house, buying an older home checklist covers the era-specific items.

How to get the most from your inspection

  • Choose a qualified home inspector, and ask which standard of practice they follow.
  • Be there, at least for the end. Seeing a problem in person beats reading about it.
  • Tell the inspector what worried you at the showing — a stain, a smell, a crack.
  • Ask the seller to clear access to the attic hatch, electrical panel, furnace, water heater and crawlspace.
  • Read the whole report, not just the summary. Note every "recommend further evaluation."
  • Follow up before your contingency deadline. Specialist visits take time to schedule.

What you can look at yourself

Your own eyes are a useful first filter before you pay for an inspection. At the showing, look for water stains, musty smells, foundation cracks, roof wear from the ground and the age of major equipment. My list of house red flags at a showing walks through it. Just leave the roof climbing, panel opening and material testing to the professionals.

The short version

  • A home inspection is a visual, non-invasive look at the accessible parts of the house.
  • It covers the major systems but usually excludes hidden areas, sewer lines, wells, septic, pests and environmental testing.
  • Add radon, a sewer scope and specialist visits based on the house.
  • Read the full report and follow up on every "further evaluation" before your deadline.
