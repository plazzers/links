How Long Does a Roof Last? Signs Yours Is Near the End

How long does a roof last? Typical lifespans by roof type, the warning signs you can spot safely from the ground, and when to call a roofer.

A typical asphalt shingle roof lasts roughly 20 to 30 years. Metal roofs often go a good deal longer, and tile or slate can outlast the people who installed it. But those are ranges, not promises. Sun, storms, attic ventilation and how well the roof was put on in the first place all push the real number up or down.

The more useful question is: where is your roof in that range, and is it showing its age? You can answer most of that from the ground, with a pair of binoculars and a flashlight in the attic. You never need to climb up there to do it.

Here's what I'd look for, and where I'd hand it over to a roofer.

Typical lifespans by roof type

These are rough, commonly quoted ranges. Treat them as a planning tool, not a countdown clock.

Roof type Rough lifespan Notes
Asphalt shingles (3-tab) Low end of the shingle range Thinner, flatter look; common on older roofs
Architectural asphalt shingles Roughly 20–30 years Thicker, layered look; most common today
Metal Often 40 years or more Fasteners and seals may need attention sooner
Clay or concrete tile Often 50 years or more Tiles last; the underlayment beneath may not
Slate Can last generations Needs a roofer who knows slate

Two things shorten these numbers a lot: a hot attic with poor ventilation, and a second layer of shingles installed over an old one. Steep roofs also tend to shed water better and last longer than low-slope ones.

First, find out how old it is

Age is the single best clue, and it's often easy to get.

  • Ask. If you're buying, ask the seller how old the roof is, who installed it and whether it has ever leaked. It's on my list of questions to ask the seller for a reason.
  • Look for paperwork. Receipts, warranty cards and permit records usually carry a date. Many towns keep roofing permits on file.
  • Check the attic. Some roofers leave a sticker or a note near the attic hatch.

If nobody knows the age, the condition signs below matter even more.

Signs a roof is near the end (seen from the ground)

Walk around the house on a clear day. Use binoculars for the far side and the upper slopes. Look for:

  • Curling or cupping shingles. Edges that turn up, or middles that sink like a cup, mean the shingles are drying out and losing their shape.
  • Cracked, torn or missing shingles. A few after a big storm can be repaired. Many, spread across the roof, point to age.
  • Bald spots. Shingles are covered in granules that protect them from the sun. Dark, smooth patches mean those granules are gone.
  • Granules in the gutters. Lots of coarse, sand-like grit in the gutters or at the downspout outlets is the roof shedding its protective layer.
  • A sagging roof line. Sight along the ridge and the edges. They should be straight. A dip can mean trouble in the structure underneath, and that's a job for a roofer or a structural engineer.
  • Moss and heavy debris. Moss holds water against the shingles and shortens their life.
  • Damaged flashing. The metal around chimneys, vents, skylights and walls is where many leaks start. Look for bent, lifted or rusty pieces and gaps in sealant.

One or two of these on a younger roof usually means a repair. Several of them together on a roof that's already 20-plus years old usually means it's time to start planning.

What the attic tells you

The underside of the roof often shows trouble before the ceiling does. From the attic hatch, with a good flashlight:

  • Stains or dark streaks on the roof boards or rafters mean water has gotten in, now or in the past. Look again after a heavy rain if you can.
  • Daylight. Turn the flashlight off. Pinpoints of light where there shouldn't be any are holes.
  • Damp or compressed insulation below a leak.
  • Frost or heavy condensation in cold weather, which points to moist house air getting into the attic.

Stay on the joists or a solid walkway, watch for roofing nails coming through overhead, and don't go up on a hot summer afternoon. If the attic has loose gray-brown or gold pebble-like insulation, don't disturb it, since some older vermiculite can contain asbestos. What you should not do yourself

This is the part I want to be very clear about.

  • Don't climb onto the roof. Falls from roofs and ladders are a serious risk, and a steep or wet roof is no place to inspect anything. Binoculars do the job from the yard.
  • Don't walk on an old roof even if it looks flat and easy. Brittle shingles crack under your feet, and you can do damage that wasn't there before.
  • Don't clear snow or ice from up top. If you get thick ice along the roof edge in winter, use a roof rake from the ground or call a pro.
  • Stay clear of power lines near the roof edge and any branches touching them.

When to call a roofer

Call a licensed roofer when:

  • You see an active leak, a water stain spreading on a ceiling, or wet insulation.
  • The roof line sags or dips anywhere.
  • Shingles are missing or lifted after a storm. Ready.gov's severe weather guidance is a good reminder to check for damage once it's safe to go outside.
  • The roof is near the end of its expected range and you're seeing several of the signs above.
  • You're buying a house and the seller can't tell you the roof's age.

A good roofer will tell you whether it needs a repair, a few more seasons of watching, or a replacement. If you're buying, a qualified home inspector will also look at the roof, but many inspect it from the ground or the eaves, so ask how they'll do it. Habits that help a roof last

  • Keep gutters clean so water doesn't back up under the roof edge.
  • Have branches trimmed back so they don't scrape the shingles.
  • Make sure bathroom and kitchen fans vent outside, not into the attic.
  • Look the roof over from the ground every spring and fall, and after any big storm.
  • Write down the roof's age and any repairs, so the next person (or future you) knows.

The short version

  • Asphalt shingle roofs commonly last roughly 20 to 30 years; metal, tile and slate usually last longer.
  • Curling, bald spots, granules in the gutters, missing shingles and a sagging line are the big warning signs.
  • Look from the ground with binoculars and from inside the attic. Never climb up.
  • Leaks, sags and storm damage are a call to a roofer, not a weekend project.
