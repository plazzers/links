Winter Power Outage Checklist: What to Do Before and During

What to do when the power goes out in winter: a plain checklist to get ready, keep pipes from freezing, stay warm safely and avoid carbon monoxide.

A summer outage is an inconvenience. A winter outage is different. When the power goes out in January, most furnaces stop too, even the gas ones, because the blower and the controls need electricity. The house starts cooling down right away, and in a hard freeze the pipes start to worry you within hours.

The good news is that most of what keeps a family safe and a house undamaged in a winter outage is simple, and almost all of it happens before the storm. Here's the plain checklist: what to set up now, what to do when the lights go out, and the mistakes that cause the real trouble.

Before the storm: the 30-minute setup

Do this on a calm afternoon in the fall, not when the forecast turns scary.

  • Find your main water shutoff and make sure it turns. Hang a tag on it. If a pipe freezes and bursts, this is the valve that saves your floors.
  • Know where the electrical panel is and have a flashlight stored next to it.
  • Test your smoke and CO alarms, and make sure at least some of your CO alarms have battery power or battery backup. Plug-in alarms without a backup stop working when the power does.
  • Put together a small outage kit. Ready.gov suggests flashlights and extra batteries (not candles), a battery or hand-crank radio, a phone power bank, water, food that doesn't need cooking, a first aid kit, and warm blankets.
  • Write down important numbers on paper: the power company's outage line, the gas company, a neighbor, family.
  • Learn how to open your garage door by hand. Most openers have a release cord. Find it and try it once while it's light out.
  • If you have a sump pump, think about how it will run in an outage. Our sump pump testing guide covers backups.

If you own or plan to buy a portable generator, read its manual now and decide where it will sit outside. Deciding that in the dark during a storm is how mistakes happen.

When the lights go out

First, check whether it's just your house. Look at the neighbors' windows and the street lights. If it's only you, check the electrical panel for a tripped main breaker. If the whole street is dark, report it to the power company and settle in.

Then:

  1. Turn off or unplug electronics and appliances you were using. Ready.gov notes that power can come back with surges. Leave one lamp switched on so you know when it's back.
  2. Keep the fridge and freezer closed. Ready.gov's guidance is that a closed refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours, and a full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours (about 24 if half full). Every door opening shortens that.
  3. Close off rooms you don't need. Pick one room to live in, ideally on the lowest floor with few windows, and close the doors to the rest.
  4. Close curtains and blinds at night to hold heat in. Open them on the sunny side during the day.
  5. Dress in layers and use blankets. Hats and socks help more than people expect.

Keep the pipes from freezing

Pipes on outside walls, in crawlspaces, unheated garages and under sinks on cold walls freeze first.

  • Open cabinet doors under kitchen and bathroom sinks so room air can reach the pipes.
  • Let a faucet drip on the coldest nights, especially one on an outside wall. Moving water is less likely to freeze.
  • Keep garage doors closed if water lines run through the garage.
  • If a pipe freezes (you open a faucet and nothing or only a trickle comes out), keep the faucet open. When warming a frozen pipe, never use an open flame, a torch or a propane heater. If you can't find the frozen spot or reach it, call a plumber.
  • If a pipe bursts, shut off the main water valve right away and then call a plumber.

If you expect to leave the house for a long outage in very cold weather, ask a plumber ahead of time about whether and how to drain your water lines. It's a job worth learning from someone who knows your system. Our winterize your house checklist covers the rest of the cold-weather plumbing prep.

Stay warm the safe way

This is the part where people get hurt, and almost always with good intentions.

Never use these to heat the house:

  • The gas oven or stovetop
  • A charcoal or gas grill, inside or in the garage
  • A camp stove or outdoor patio heater
  • A generator anywhere inside, including the garage with the door open

All of them can fill a house with carbon monoxide. NFPA and CPSC both warn about this every winter. A space heater is only a safe option if it's made for indoor use and you follow the rules in our space heater safety guide.

A fireplace or wood stove can help keep one room warm, but only if the chimney has been inspected and you use it the right way. Our fireplace safety checklist covers that.

Candles are a common outage fire cause. Use flashlights and battery lanterns instead.

Generator safety in plain words

A portable generator can carry you through a long outage, and it's also one of the most common causes of carbon monoxide poisoning during outages. CPSC's rules are simple:

  • Outside only. Never in the house, garage, basement, crawlspace or shed, even with doors or windows open.
  • Far from the house, with the exhaust pointed away from windows, doors and vents. CPSC recommends at least 20 feet away.
  • Battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms inside the house while it runs.
  • Plug appliances into the generator with heavy-duty outdoor extension cords. Never plug a generator into a wall outlet. Connecting a generator to the house wiring needs a transfer switch installed by a licensed electrician.
  • Let it cool before refueling, and store fuel away from the house in approved containers.

If a CO alarm sounds, get everyone outside to fresh air and call 911 from outside.

When it's too cold to stay

There's no prize for toughing it out. Ready.gov's advice is to go somewhere warm if your home gets too cold: a friend or family member with power, or a local warming center. Many communities announce warming centers on the radio and local news during long outages.

Before you leave in freezing weather, think about the pipes: dripping faucets, open cabinet doors, and ideally a plan from a plumber if the house will be cold for days. Tell a neighbor you're leaving.

And check on your neighbors, especially older neighbors who live alone. A knock on the door is a good habit in any outage.

When the power comes back

  • Turn things back on slowly, a few at a time, so you don't overload circuits.
  • Check the heat. Make sure the furnace starts and runs normally. If it doesn't, or you smell gas, call your heating company or gas company.
  • Walk the house and look under sinks, around the water heater and in the basement for leaks from pipes that froze. A small crack can start leaking once the pipe thaws.
  • Check the fridge and freezer and follow food safety guidance from Ready.gov and FoodSafety.gov. When in doubt, throw it out.
  • Restock the outage kit before you forget.

The short version

Before winter: find the water shutoff, test the alarms, build a small outage kit, read the generator manual. During an outage: one warm room, fridge closed, faucets dripping, cabinet doors open. Never heat the house with the oven, a grill or a generator, and keep the generator outside and far from the house. If it gets too cold, leave and go somewhere warm. After: walk the house and look for leaks. That's the whole plan, and most of it takes one afternoon.
