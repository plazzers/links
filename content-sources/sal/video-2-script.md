[SAL]
Nine things. On almost every menu in America. And I won't touch a single one of them.

Not because they'll kill you. Relax. Because I've *been* in that kitchen... and I know exactly what happens to them.

And number three? I used to sell it. Every. Single. Night.

[B-ROLL: chef's hands plating pasta under heat lamps, busy kitchen line]

I'm Sal Romano. Forty years in restaurant kitchens. Sixteen years old, scraping plates in a chain restaurant. Then line cook. Then Italy. Then twenty-five years running Michelin-starred kitchens in New York and Milan. Then my own place, Romano's, in New Jersey, with my wife Angela.

I've cooked for senators. I've cooked for my grandkids. Trust me... the grandkids are tougher.

[TEKST: 9 THINGS A CHEF NEVER ORDERS]

Now listen. This is not a horror movie. Restaurants are not out to get you. Most cooks I know are good people working hard in a very hot room.

But every kitchen has habits. Little shortcuts. Old tricks. And once you know them... you order differently. Forever.

Nine things. And at the end, I'll tell you what I *do* order. The good stuff.

## 1: The Well-Done Steak (1:01–2:26)

[SAL]
Old kitchen joke. You ready?

What do you do with the worst steak in the walk-in?

You save it for the guy who orders well-done.

[B-ROLL: grey well-done steak on a plate, then a pink medium steak sliced open]

Now, it's a joke. But like every good kitchen joke... there's a little truth in it.

Think about it from the cook's side. Here's a steak with a funny shape. A little too much gristle. Been sitting a day longer than the others. Nobody can serve that one medium-rare. Medium-rare shows you everything. The color. The texture. The grain.

But cook it all the way through? Grey. Every steak is grey. Every steak looks the same.

You can't tell. That's the point.

My first steakhouse job, I was nineteen. A steakhouse chain off the highway. Big sign, little steaks. The grill guy was a giant named Frankie, and he kept a separate tray in the corner. I said, "Frankie, what's that tray?" He said, "Kid, that's the well-done tray." And he winked.

Forty years later. I still remember that wink.

[TEKST: Order medium. Or order something else.]

Now, a great steakhouse will cook you a beautiful steak any way you want. They'll cry a little in the back, but they'll do it right.

The cheap place, though? Different story. If you love well-done, I respect it. Your mouth, your rules. Just order something built to cook long. A burger. A braised short rib.

[SAL]
My rule? Order medium. Or order something else. You're paying for a steak... you should get to see the steak.

## 2: Fish on a Monday (2:26–3:48)

[SAL]
You're in a lovely little town. Five hundred miles from the ocean. It's Monday night. And the special is... sea bass.

Hmm.

[B-ROLL: fish boxes on ice at a back kitchen door, early morning]

Here's an old kitchen rule. Every cook in America has heard it. A lot of restaurants get their big fish deliveries at the end of the week. Thursday. Friday. Because the weekend is when everybody eats out.

Saturday night, the fish flies out the door. Sunday, plenty of suppliers don't deliver at all. So Monday's fish?

Very often... that's Thursday's fish.

Now, a good kitchen stores it right. That's not my point. My point is that fish is a clock. Every day it sits, it loses something. The sweetness. The firm flake. That clean smell of the sea.

In Milan, our fish man came every single morning at five. Rain. Snow. Holidays. And the chef smelled every fish like a bloodhound. Nose right in the gills. If one fish disappointed him, the whole box went back on the truck.

I watched a delivery driver almost cry. Grown man. Almost cried.

That's the standard. Most places can't afford that standard. I get it.

[TEKST: "When did the fish come in?"]

[SAL]
So do me a favor. Ask. "When did the fish come in?" A good server answers in one second. "This morning. Chef's excited about it." Beautiful. Order it.

If they look at the ceiling and say "uh... let me check"? You got your answer. Have the chicken.

## 3: The Sunday Night "Chef's Special" Pasta (3:48–5:12)

[SAL]
Okay. Number three. The confession.

Sunday night. Weekend's over. Everybody in the kitchen is dead on their feet. And on the board, in beautiful chalk handwriting...

"Chef's Special Pasta. Rigatoni, roasted vegetables, sausage, a touch of cream."

Sounds lovely, right?

[B-ROLL: chalkboard "Chef's Special Pasta", then a walk-in cooler full of half-used containers]

Let me tell you what that dish really is. It's the weekend. In a bowl.

The vegetables that didn't sell Saturday. The sausage from Friday's special. The cream that's getting close to its date. Chop it, sauté it, toss it with pasta. And pasta forgives everything. Cream forgives everything. Cheese on top? Forgiven twice.

I was twenty-three. A busy Italian place in North Jersey. Every night there was a pasta special, and every night it was my job. Before service, the owner would walk me through the walk-in. Pointing. "This. This. And that. Make it a pasta."

So I made it a pasta. Every night.

And Sunday? Sunday was the masterpiece. The whole week's leftovers, one pan.

[TEKST: Sal's old job: the "special" pasta]

And it sold. Oh, it sold. Two regulars ordered it every Sunday for a year. Same table, same smile. I never had the heart to tell them it was a different dish every week.

Was it bad? No. I cooked it with love. But nobody designed that dish. The fridge designed it.

[SAL]
So here's my test. Special has six ingredients that don't belong together? That's the fridge talking. Three ingredients that clearly belong together? That's the chef talking.

Order the chef.

## 4: Hollandaise at the End of Brunch (5:12–6:25)

[SAL]
Hollandaise. I love hollandaise. Egg yolks, butter, lemon. When it's fresh, it's silk. It's a little miracle.

And it is a nightmare to keep.

[B-ROLL: eggs Benedict, then a steel container of sauce on a warm shelf by the stove]

Hollandaise hates two things. Too hot, it breaks. Too cold, it sets up like pudding. So a lot of kitchens make one big batch before brunch and keep it warm. Next to the stove. In a thermos. Under a lamp.

For hours.

At ten in the morning, that sauce is gorgeous. At one-thirty? It's been sitting there since before you woke up. It's tired. Thick. A little greasy. The shine is gone. The lemon is gone.

That's not cooking. That's reheating. Actually... it's keeping warm. Which is worse.

Romano's did brunch for exactly one year. One. Angela made the hollandaise every morning, and Angela is a perfectionist. By noon she'd taste it, make a face, and pour it out. New batch. By the third batch on a Sunday, she put the whisk down and looked at me.

"Sal. We are not a brunch restaurant."

She was right. Basta. No more brunch.

[TEKST: Late brunch? Skip the Benedict.]

[SAL]
So. Brunch at nine? Get the Benedict. Enjoy every bite. Brunch at one-thirty? Order something cooked right when you order it. An omelet. Pancakes. French toast.

Something nobody made four hours ago.

## 5: Seafood at the All-You-Can-Eat Buffet (6:25–7:42)

[SAL]
The buffet. Oh, I love a buffet. The ambition! The trays! The little sneeze guard!

But the seafood? I walk right past it.

[B-ROLL: long buffet line, shrimp on ice, crab legs, steam trays]

Do the math with me. You pay twenty-something dollars. All you can eat. Shrimp. Crab legs. Fried fish. Salmon.

Seafood is one of the most expensive things a kitchen buys. So how does that work?

It works because the buffet buys the cheapest seafood it can find. Frozen, in bulk. And then it sits in a steam tray. Seafood is the most delicate thing in the building. Five minutes too long, a shrimp turns to rubber. Twenty minutes under the heat?

That's an eraser with a tail.

My second year washing dishes, the chain I worked for did a Sunday buffet. Guess who refilled the trays? Sixteen-year-old Sal. And the rule was simple. New shrimp go on top. Old shrimp stay underneath. Nobody digs to the bottom.

I dug to the bottom once. Once. That's all I'm gonna say.

[TEKST: Buffet = great potatoes, skip the shrimp]

[SAL]
Look, I'm not telling you buffets are evil. I love the mashed potatoes. I love the tiny desserts. The carving station? Beautiful. The guy's standing right there, cutting it fresh for you.

But the seafood? I don't order it, because I don't know how long it's been sitting there. Seafood deserves better.

And so do you.

## 6: The $18 Salad (8:07–9:19)

[SAL]
Eighteen dollars. For a salad.

Let me ask you something. What's in it?

[B-ROLL: elegant salad on a big white plate, restaurant menu with "$18" circled]

Lettuce. A few tomatoes. Some cucumber. A little shaved onion. Croutons, which is yesterday's bread. Dressing, which is oil and vinegar.

Ask any chef. A basic salad is one of the cheapest things a kitchen makes. Ingredients? A couple of dollars. Maybe three. Thirty seconds to put together. No stove. No oven. Honestly? Not a lot of skill.

My granddaughter makes a better salad. She's eight. She uses a lot of croutons, but still.

[TEKST: Salad ingredients: about $2–3. Menu price: $18.]

And then the add-ons. Chicken? Seven more dollars. Salmon? Ten more. Suddenly you're at twenty-eight dollars for lettuce wearing a hat.

At Romano's I had a salad for sixteen dollars. Good salad. Angela called it "the money salad." Not because of the ingredients. Because of the margin. Every time one went out, she'd tap the counter. Ding. "There's the electric bill."

[SAL]
So who orders the eighteen-dollar salad? Everybody. That's the problem.

Now, a great salad does exist. Peak tomatoes in August. Real burrata. Something the kitchen chose with care. Madonna... that one I'll pay for.

But a pile of lettuce for eighteen bucks? Come on. Not a chance. You can make that at home. In your pajamas.

## 7: Plain Marinara at the Big Italian Chain (9:19–10:34)

[SAL]
Now this one hurts me personally. Because I'm Italian. Because my grandfather came over from Calabria with one suitcase and a bag of tomato seeds.

Spaghetti marinara. At the big Italian chain.

[B-ROLL: plain spaghetti with red sauce, chain-style dining room with big booths]

What is it? Pasta. Tomatoes. Garlic. Olive oil. Basil, if you're lucky. You know what that costs at the grocery store? Per plate?

About a dollar. Maybe a dollar fifty with the good olive oil. And you should buy the good olive oil.

They'll charge you sixteen. Seventeen.

And let me tell you about the sauce. When I was a young line cook at a chain, our red sauce came in a bag. Same bag, every location, every state. We didn't make it. We heated it. That's the whole recipe. Open. Heat. Pour.

Meanwhile my grandfather's sauce took all of Sunday. He'd start it after church and stir it till dinner. The whole house smelled like that pot. You don't get that from a bag.

[TEKST: Order what you can't make at home]

[SAL]
So here's my rule. Order what you can't make at home. You go to a restaurant for something hard. Something with technique. Something that took a professional three hours.

Not something you can make tonight, in twelve minutes, better, for a dollar.

Trust Sal. Make the marinara at home. Order the osso buco.

## 8: The Lemon in Your Water (10:34–11:46)

**Izvor za tvrdnju o istraživanju:** Anne LaGrange Loving i sar., "Microbial flora on restaurant beverage lemon slices", *Journal of Environmental Health*, 2007 (mikroorganizmi pronađeni na oko 70% kriški limuna iz 21 restorana). Sačuvaj link pre objave.

[SAL]
This one's small. Tiny. A little yellow slice. But in forty years, I've never asked for it.

The lemon in your water.

[B-ROLL: glass of ice water with a lemon wedge, then a bar tray of sliced lemons]

Let me tell you where that lemon lives. In a little tray at the bar, or at the server station. Sliced at four in the afternoon. And then... hands.

The bartender's hands. The server's hands. Somebody who just counted cash. Somebody who just carried dirty plates. Grab. Drop. Grab. Drop. All night.

[TEKST: A study tested restaurant lemon slices... and found bacteria on many of them]

A while back, researchers actually tested lemon slices from restaurants. A lot of them had bacteria on them.

Now, I'm not your doctor. I'm a cook. I just skip it. I don't know whose hands touched it.

At Romano's bar, I had one rule. Tongs. Always tongs. My bartender Vinnie hated those tongs. Said they slowed him down. I said, "Vinnie, you know what else slows you down? Me. Standing here. Watching you." He used the tongs.

Twenty years later, Vinnie has his own bar. Guess what's sitting next to the lemons? Tongs. He sends me a picture every Christmas.

[SAL]
So you want lemon? Ask for a fresh wedge on the side. Squeeze it yourself. Or just skip it like me.

Cold water is beautiful all by itself. It doesn't need decoration.

## 9: Anything the Server Can't Describe (11:46–13:05)

[SAL]
Last one. And it's the simplest rule I've got.

If the server can't tell you what's in it... don't order it.

[B-ROLL: server at a table, hesitating, looking back toward the kitchen]

You ask, "What's in the chef's signature sauce?" And the server goes, "Oh, it's like... a sauce? It's really good. Everybody gets it."

Okay. That tells me two things. One. Nobody in that kitchen ever sat the servers down and fed them the food. Two. Maybe nobody back there knows either.

Maybe it came in a bag.

In the Michelin kitchens? Every new dish, the servers tasted it before service. Every single one. And the chef quizzed them like a schoolteacher with a ruler. "What's in the sauce? What's the garnish? Where is the fish from?" Get it wrong, and you're not on the floor tonight.

I did the same thing at Romano's. My servers could tell you which farm the eggs came from. One of them could tell you the chicken's name.

That was a joke. Mostly.

And here's the flip side. A server lights up and tells you the whole story? The farm. The sauce. How long it braised. That's a proud kitchen. Order whatever they're excited about.

[TEKST: Can't describe it? Don't order it.]

[SAL]
A kitchen that teaches its servers is a kitchen that cares. A kitchen that doesn't? Well. You can do the math.

If they can't describe it, I don't order it. Simple.

## WHAT SAL ORDERS (13:05–14:10)

[SAL]
Okay. Enough of what I don't order. Now here's what Sal orders. The good stuff. And notice... none of it is the most expensive thing on the menu.

[TEKST: WHAT SAL ORDERS]

One. A braise. Short ribs. Osso buco. Lamb shank. Anything that cooks low and slow for hours. You can't fake time. A braise is a kitchen showing you its patience. At Romano's, my short ribs went six hours. Angela said the whole street smelled like Sunday.

Two. Fresh pasta, made in house. Even if the menu says it, ask anyway. "Is the pasta made here?" They say yes with a big smile? Order it. Fresh pasta is a different food. Soft. Silky. It drinks the sauce.

Three. Whatever came in fresh today. Remember the fish question? This is the happy side of it. The server's eyes light up. "Chef got beautiful scallops this morning." That's the best plate in the building tonight.

And four. Desserts made in house. I always share one. Because somewhere back there is a pastry cook who got up at five in the morning. Like Angela did. Twelve years.

That person deserves an order.
