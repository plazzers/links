## HOOK (0:00–1:13)

[SAL]
My grandfather came from Calabria with one suitcase. In that suitcase? A knife, a rosary, and a jar of chili peppers.

If he saw what America calls "Italian food" today... he'd cry. Then he'd ask for seconds.

[B-ROLL: an old black-and-white family photo, then a steaming plate of spaghetti and meatballs]

I'm Sal Romano. Sixteen, washing dishes in a chain restaurant. Then twenty-five years in Michelin-starred kitchens. New York. Milan. Then my own place in New Jersey for twelve years.

So today, I'm ranking the big Italian chains. Eight of them. Worst to best.

[TEKST: 8 ITALIAN CHAINS. WORST TO BEST.]

Now listen. I'm not here to trash Italian-American food. I grew up on it. Sunday gravy. Baked ziti. Chicken parm the size of a hubcap. That food is real. It's ours.

The question is simple. Who cooks? And who just reheats?

[TEKST: HOW SAL JUDGES: COOKING. HONESTY. VALUE. ONE DISH.]

Here's how I judge. One: what do they say they actually make in the restaurant? Not what I guess. What they say, or what got reported. Two: is it trying to be Italy, or is it proud Italian-American? Both are fine. Pretending is not. Three: value. What lands on the table for your money.

And every place gets one dish I'd actually order. Even the last one. Fair is fair.

And at the end, I'll show you how to make the real thing at home. Let's go.

## #8: FAZOLI'S (1:13–2:35)

[SAL]
Number eight. Fazoli's.

[B-ROLL: generic fast-casual counter, a tray of breadsticks, a bowl of baked pasta]

This one isn't really a sit-down place. It's Italian at fast-food speed. Started in Kentucky in 1988. Today it's over two hundred restaurants in twenty-six states.

And the famous thing? Unlimited breadsticks. When you eat in, they keep coming. Mental Floss reported the chain makes over a hundred million breadsticks a year.

A hundred million! My nonna made bread every Saturday her whole life. She didn't make a hundred million. She made maybe... a few thousand. And every one was perfect.

[TEKST: 100,000,000+ BREADSTICKS A YEAR]

Is this the Italy I trained in? Come on. Not a chance. A counter, a tray, a number on a receipt.

But here's my compliment, and I mean it. The price. Last year they put ravioli on the menu starting at five ninety-nine. A family of four can eat for what one plate of pasta costs in Manhattan.

When I was sixteen, a place like this would have been my Friday night. No shame in that.

[TEKST: GRISSINI: BORN IN TURIN]

And a little lesson while we're here. The breadstick has a real Italian ancestor. Grissini. From Turin, up north. Thin, dry, crunchy, like a pencil you can eat. Soft and buttery with garlic? That's the American version. Different animal. Both delicious.

My grandkids call every breadstick "Nonno sticks." I don't correct them.

[SAL]
What I'd order? Plain spaghetti, red sauce, a few breadsticks. Keep it simple. Simple is hard to ruin.

## #7: BUCA DI BEPPO (2:35–4:01)

[SAL]
Number seven. Buca di Beppo.

[B-ROLL: red-and-white checkered tablecloths, walls packed with vintage photos, a giant platter in the middle of a table]

Walking in here is like walking into my Aunt Rosa's basement. Checkered tablecloths. Old photos covering every inch of every wall. Dean Martin on the speakers. And one location famously has a room dedicated to the Pope.

The Pope! In my family, the Pope was on the wall too. Right next to Sinatra. Same frame size. Nobody argued.

The concept is family style. Big platters in the middle of the table. Everybody shares. And that part? I love. That is how Italians eat. Nobody gets their own little plate. You fight your cousin for the last meatball.

[TEKST: FAMILY STYLE = HOW ITALIANS REALLY EAT]

Now the food. It's big, it's heavy, it's a party. Personally, it's too much sauce for me. In Italy, the sauce dresses the pasta. It doesn't drown it. Pasta should never swim.

At Romano's, a new cook once sent out a plate with a lake of sauce. I made him eat it in front of me. He never did it again.

They're down to about forty restaurants now. So if you have one near you, it's an experience.

Why number seven, then? Because for me, the walls are doing more work than the stove. When the decorations are louder than the food... I get suspicious. But my granddaughter would rank it number one. She'd want to eat with the Pope.

[SAL]
What I'd order? A platter of spaghetti for the table and a big group of loud people. The room is the reason to go.

## #6: ROMANO'S MACARONI GRILL (4:01–5:16)

[SAL]
Number six. Romano's Macaroni Grill.

And no. No relation. I checked. Twice.

[B-ROLL: a sizzling lasagna on a wooden board, a wood-fired pizza oven glowing]

This chain was founded in 1988 by a guy named Phil Romano. Great name. Back in 2004, there were over two hundred of them. Today? Nine. Nine restaurants. In the whole country.

So it's not that I don't respect it. It's that you probably can't find one. Hard to rank a restaurant you have to drive three states to visit.

What's on the menu? Lasagna bolognese. Brick-oven pizza at some locations. Rustic stuff. I like the idea.

[TEKST: REAL BOLOGNESE: RAGÙ, SLOW, PATIENT]

But let me teach you something about bolognese. In Bologna, ragù cooks for hours. Very little tomato. Meat, soffritto, a splash of milk, a little wine. And they never put it on spaghetti. Tagliatelle. Or lasagne. That's it.

So if you see "spaghetti bolognese" anywhere? A Bolognese grandmother just fainted somewhere.

My first week in Milan, I called it "meat sauce." The sous chef stopped talking to me for three days.

And here's a fair compliment. Survival. Thirty-plus years in this business is a lifetime. Most restaurants don't make it to five. I closed Romano's on my own terms, and I know how hard that is.

[SAL]
What I'd order? The lasagna bolognese. Because at least they put the ragù where it belongs.

## #5: OLIVE GARDEN (5:16–6:49)

[SAL]
Number five. The big one. Olive Garden.

Over nine hundred thirty restaurants. Everybody's been. Your aunt has a gift card in her purse right now.

[B-ROLL: a basket of breadsticks, a bowl of fettuccine in creamy sauce, a salad bowl]

Let's be fair. Some things here are cooked in the restaurant. An Olive Garden representative said the soups are made by hand, from scratch, every morning. Hand-chopped vegetables. That's real work. Respect.

And the deal. This fall, the Never Ending Pasta Bowl is back. Fourteen ninety-nine. Unlimited pasta, unlimited soup or salad, unlimited breadsticks. Dine-in only.

Fourteen ninety-nine for all of that? That's not a meal. That's a hostage situation. You don't leave.

[TEKST: NEVER ENDING PASTA BOWL: $14.99 (DINE-IN)]

Now. The alfredo. Let me tell you the real story.

[TEKST: REAL ALFREDO: BUTTER + PARMIGIANO. NO CREAM.]

Rome, early nineteen hundreds. A man named Alfredo di Lelio makes fettuccine for his wife after she has a baby. Butter. Parmigiano. Pasta water. That's it. No cream. No garlic. No chicken.

Then two Hollywood stars eat it on their honeymoon. They bring it home to America. And America does what America does. Adds cream. Adds more cream.

Is the American version bad? No! It's comfort food. My grandkids love it. But call it what it is. It's a cousin. Not the original.

So why only number five? Because nine hundred restaurants is a machine. Big machines are built to be the same everywhere. That's great for a business. For me? I want a little surprise. A little personality on the plate.

Good news? They added a lighter portions menu. Seven classics, smaller plates, lower price. Smart.

[SAL]
What I'd order? A bowl of soup and a couple of breadsticks. The soup is where the kitchen actually cooks.

## #4: THE OLD SPAGHETTI FACTORY (7:13–8:28)

[SAL]
Number four. The Old Spaghetti Factory.

[B-ROLL: an old trolley car inside a dining room, a plate of spaghetti with grated white cheese and brown butter]

Portland, Oregon. 1969. Started by a couple, Sally and Gus Dussin. And some locations have a real trolley car inside. You eat dinner in a trolley! My grandkids would never leave.

And here's what I like. The complete meal. Soup or salad. Bread. Your entrée. And a little ice cream at the end, vanilla or spumoni. One price. No surprises.

Spumoni! Angela used to make spumoni every summer at Romano's. Three layers, pistachio, cherry, chocolate. People drove from Philadelphia.

[TEKST: ONE PRICE: SOUP/SALAD + BREAD + ENTRÉE + ICE CREAM]

Now the famous dish. Spaghetti with mizithra cheese and browned butter. Same recipe since 1969.

Why number four, ahead of the giants? Because they know who they are. They're not pretending to be Tuscany. They're a family spaghetti house. Proud of it. I respect that more than fake Italy.

Here's the funny part. Mizithra? Not Italian. It's a Greek cheese. Goat and sheep milk.

But you know what? Brown butter and a salty grated cheese on pasta? That's a very Italian idea. In the north we do butter and sage. Same spirit. So I give it a pass. A Greek cheese on Italian pasta in Oregon. That's America. Beautiful.

[SAL]
What I'd order? The mizithra and brown butter. Simple. Honest. Fifty-plus years of the same recipe. That means something.

## #3: BERTUCCI'S (8:28–9:47)

[SAL]
Number three. Bertucci's.

[B-ROLL: flames inside a brick oven, a pizza peel sliding out a blistered pizza, warm rolls in a basket]

Started in 1981 in Somerville, Massachusetts. Only about a dozen left today, mostly in New England. But this one has something most chains don't.

A brick oven. Right in the middle of the restaurant. You can see it.

And they say the dough is scratch-made, and the rolls are freshly baked. The meatballs, made from scratch daily. That's their words, not mine. But that's what I want to hear.

[TEKST: SCRATCH DOUGH. BRICK OVEN. FRESH ROLLS.]

Why does that matter? Because dough is alive. Flour, water, yeast, salt, and time. You can't rush it. If somebody in the building is making dough, somebody in the building is paying attention.

Fire changes everything. A home oven does what, five hundred degrees? A real brick oven goes way past that. The crust puffs, it blisters, it gets a little char. That's flavor you cannot fake.

When I opened Romano's, my first big purchase wasn't a car. It was a wood oven. Angela said, "Sal, we need a new fridge." I said, "The oven is the fridge." Didn't make sense. She still brings it up. Years later. Every Christmas.

Compliment and complaint in one? Only a dozen locations. If you live near one, lucky you.

[SAL]
What I'd order? A simple pizza. Tomato, mozzarella, basil. If the oven is good, you don't need fifteen toppings. Fifteen toppings is hiding something.

## #2: MAGGIANO'S LITTLE ITALY (9:47–11:12)

[SAL]
Number two. Maggiano's Little Italy.

[B-ROLL: dim dining room with white tablecloths, large family-style bowls passed across a table]

This is the dressed-up one. Forty-eight restaurants. Owned by the same company that owns Chili's, believe it or not.

And this year they did something I never see. They made portions bigger. Same price. They announced about twenty percent more pasta on favorite dishes.

Twenty percent more! In this economy! Nonna would light a candle for these people.

You know how rare that is? Every owner I ever met wants to shrink the plate. Same price, a little less pasta, nobody notices. I had a consultant tell me that once at Romano's. I showed him the door. Then I gave him a plate to go. A big one.

[TEKST: ~20% MORE PASTA, SAME PRICE (2026)]

They also do a family-style dinner. Starts at forty-four dollars a person. Salads, appetizers, entrées, desserts. And refills. That's a real Italian Sunday. Three hours at the table, nobody's in a rush.

And they say the desserts are scratch-made. Listen, my wife Angela made every dessert at Romano's by hand. So when a chain says "scratch-made desserts"? I'm ordering one. For research.

Why not number one? Because it's polished. Very polished. Sometimes so polished you forget a person cooked it. Number one has to feel like a family. Not like a hotel ballroom.

Is it Milan? No. It's Italian-American. Big, generous, a little loud. Nothing wrong with that. That's my childhood on a plate.

[SAL]
What I'd order? The family-style. Bring six people. Share everything. That's how you eat Italian.

## #1: CARRABBA'S ITALIAN GRILL (11:12–12:31)

[SAL]
And number one. Carrabba's Italian Grill.

[B-ROLL: flames under a grill grate, chicken getting grill marks, a cook plating with a spoon of sauce]

Why number one? Because it starts with a family. Houston, 1986. Johnny Carrabba and his uncle, Damian Mandola. And many of the recipes came from Damian's mother, Grace, and his sister, Rose.

Mothers and sisters! That's how every great Italian recipe starts. Not in a test kitchen. In somebody's house.

[TEKST: RECIPES FROM GRACE & ROSE]

Second reason. The wood-burning grill. That's what they're known for. And they say their pasta sauces are made from scratch every day. That's the line I look for.

In Michelin kitchens, the first job every morning was the sauces. Before coffee. Before anything. If the sauce is made today, somebody cares.

And a grill is honest. You can't hide anything on a grill. Too much heat, it burns. Too little, it's grey and sad. A cook has to stand there and watch. That's real cooking. Not reheating.

My grandfather grilled sausages in the backyard in Paterson every Sunday. Peppers, onions, a little oregano. The whole street could smell it. Neighbors who didn't even like us showed up with plates.

Now, is it Calabria? No. It's a chain with around two hundred restaurants. But it feels like somebody's family is still in there.

[SAL]
What I'd order? The Chicken Bryan. Grilled chicken, goat cheese, sun-dried tomatoes. My grandfather would ask, "What is a Bryan?" Then he'd eat the whole thing.

## WHAT SAL ORDERS + MAKE IT REAL AT HOME (12:31–14:07)

[SAL]
So. Here's what Sal orders at any Italian chain. Anywhere in America.

[TEKST: WHAT SAL ORDERS]

One. Whatever the kitchen actually cooks. Soup made that morning. Something off the grill. Something out of a real oven.

Two. Simple pasta. Fewer ingredients, fewer places to hide.

Three. I skip anything with "alfredo" and three proteins piled on top. Not because it's evil. Because I can make the real one at home in ten minutes.

And now I'll show you. Two dishes. Make them real.

[B-ROLL: a pan of butter melting, Parmigiano being grated like snow]

Real fettuccine Alfredo. Boil your fettuccine. Melt butter in a pan. Add the pasta with a splash of pasta water. Then a mountain of Parmigiano. Toss. Toss. Toss. It turns creamy all by itself. No cream. Ever.

[TEKST: CARBONARA: EGGS. GUANCIALE. PECORINO. PEPPER.]

Real carbonara. Guanciale, that's pork cheek, crispy in a pan. Eggs and pecorino whisked in a bowl. Lots of black pepper. Hot pasta into the guanciale, off the heat. Then the eggs. Stir fast, with a little pasta water. No cream. No peas. No garlic.

Put cream in carbonara at my table? We are not friends anymore. Capisce?

One more. Spaghetti and meatballs? Delicious. Italian-American. In my grandfather's village, meatballs came on their own plate. The pasta came first.

Both are right. Just know which one you're eating.

And chicken parm? You won't find it in Italy. They have melanzane alla parmigiana. Eggplant. Layers of fried eggplant, tomato, cheese. The chicken version was born right here. In places like Paterson. And I'm proud of it.

[TEKST: CHICKEN PARM = 100% ITALIAN-AMERICAN]

Last tip. Salt your pasta water like the sea. Save a cup before you drain. That starchy water is the secret behind every restaurant sauce you ever loved. Trust Sal.
