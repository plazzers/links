## DISH 1: Garlic Butter Breadsticks (1:17–3:34)

[SAL]
We start where every chain dinner starts. The breadsticks.

[B-ROLL: a basket of golden breadsticks, steam rising]

At the restaurant they're "free." Free with a plate of pasta that costs you twenty bucks. Nothing is free, my friend. You're paying for the bread. You're just paying for it inside the alfredo.

At home? About twenty-five cents a breadstick. A whole batch of twelve for about three dollars.

[TEKST: BREADSTICKS: about $0.25 each. Batch of 12: about $3]

Here's what you need. Warm water. A packet of yeast. Two tablespoons of sugar. Four cups of flour. A little soft butter. Salt. That's it. That's the whole secret.

What's in a bag of flour? Five pounds, about three dollars. A packet of yeast, under a dollar. You're making restaurant bread out of pocket change.

And don't be scared of the yeast. People see the word "yeast" and they order takeout. Come on. It's two hours, but your hands are busy for maybe twenty minutes. The rest of the time? The dough does the work. You watch TV.

[B-ROLL: yeast foaming in a glass bowl of warm water]

Yeast and sugar into warm water. Not hot. Warm, like a baby's bath. Wait five minutes until it foams. No foam? Your yeast is dead. Buy new yeast.

Flour, butter, salt. Knead it seven, eight minutes. By hand is good therapy. A mixer is five minutes. Smooth and soft, like a pillow.

Let it rise an hour. Then twelve pieces. Roll each one into a rope, about seven inches. Let them rise again, forty-five minutes.

[B-ROLL: hands rolling dough ropes on a floured board, lining them up on parchment]

Four hundred degrees. Twelve to fifteen minutes. Light golden. Not brown. Light golden.

Why not brown? Because a brown breadstick is a crunchy breadstick. And nobody wants a crunchy breadstick. You want soft. You want it to pull apart.

Now listen. I was sixteen years old in a chain kitchen. My first job that wasn't the dish pit? Breadsticks. Eight hours a day. And the manager, big guy, mustache, he grabs my arm the first day. He says, "Kid. You brushed these once."

I said, "Yeah?"

He says, "Twice. Always twice."

[TEKST: SAL SAYS: BRUSH TWICE]

Here's the trick. Melted butter, garlic powder, salt, a pinch of oregano. Brush them the second they come out of the oven. Then wait two minutes... and brush them again.

[B-ROLL: close-up of breadsticks being brushed with garlic butter, then brushed a second time]

The first coat soaks in. The second coat sits on top and shines. That second brush is why you can't stop eating them. Forty-six years later, I still hear that guy. "Twice, kid."

Beautiful.

## DISH 2: Real Fettuccine Alfredo (3:34–5:58)

[SAL]
Now. The alfredo.

[B-ROLL: a wide bowl of fettuccine alfredo, glossy sauce clinging to the noodles]

This is the plate my grandson paid the most for. At a sit-down chain, a plate of alfredo runs you somewhere around twenty dollars. Add chicken, more.

At home? About two-fifty a plate. Four plates for about ten bucks.

[TEKST: ALFREDO: about $2.50 a plate]

And I'll be honest with you. I don't order alfredo at the chains. Why? Because a lot of the time, to me, it tastes like it came out of a bag. Maybe it did, maybe it didn't. But real alfredo takes fifteen minutes. There's no reason for a bag.

Here's the list. A pound of fettuccine. Six tablespoons of butter. A cup and a half of heavy cream. Two cloves of garlic. A cup and a half of Parmigiano-Reggiano. Salt, pepper, a tiny pinch of nutmeg.

Pasta into well-salted water. Salty like the sea. Pull it one minute before it's done. And save a cup of that pasta water. That water is gold.

[B-ROLL: pasta water being scooped out with a mug before draining]

Meanwhile, butter in a wide pan. Medium-low. Garlic for thirty seconds. Then the cream. Let it simmer three, four minutes, until it gets a little thick.

Heat down to low. Now the cheese. A handful at a time. Stir. Another handful. Stir.

Now here's where everybody messes it up.

[TEKST: SAL SAYS: GRATE YOUR OWN CHEESE]

The cheese. Grate. Your. Own.

The bag of pre-shredded cheese at the store? It's coated with an anti-caking powder so it doesn't clump in the bag. Great for the bag. Terrible for your sauce. It doesn't melt clean. Your sauce turns grainy and sad.

And the green can? Come on. Not a chance.

[B-ROLL: close-up of a wedge of Parmigiano being grated on a box grater, snowy pile on the board]

In Milan, my first week as a young cook, I grated cheese from a bag at home for my roommates. Pre-shredded. I was proud of myself. The old chef I worked for came over for dinner. He took one bite. Put the fork down. Didn't say a word. He just walked to my fridge, found the bag, and threw it out the window.

The window! Third floor!

Lesson learned.

So. Pasta into the pan. Toss. A splash of pasta water. Toss. Another splash. You want it silky. Shiny. Not soup, not cement. Pepper. Tiny pinch of nutmeg. Serve it right now. Alfredo does not wait for anybody.

[B-ROLL: Sal tossing fettuccine in the pan, sauce turning glossy as pasta water goes in]

Look at this. That's dinner.

What's the most expensive thing in the pan? The cheese. A good wedge is the splurge. Everything else is butter, cream and pasta. Pennies. And still, two-fifty a plate. Against twenty at the restaurant.

Trust Sal. Grate your own.

## DISH 3: House Salad with Italian Dressing (5:58–8:14)

[SAL]
Third thing on my grandson's table. The salad. The one that never ends.

[B-ROLL: a big bowl of house salad with red onion, tomato, pepperoncini, black olives and croutons]

Now, I love this salad. I'm not gonna pretend I don't. Crunchy, tangy, a little creamy. Everybody at the table fights over the pepperoncini.

But it's lettuce. It's lettuce and dressing. At home this costs you about a dollar a serving. Six big servings for about six bucks.

[TEKST: HOUSE SALAD: about $1 a serving]

Iceberg and romaine, chopped. A little red onion, sliced thin. Two Roma tomatoes. Pepperoncini. Black olives. Croutons. Done.

Iceberg? Sal, really? Yes, really. Iceberg is the crunch. Romaine is the flavor. You need both. A head of iceberg is two bucks. A jar of pepperoncini lasts you a month of salads.

The magic is the dressing. Half a cup of olive oil. A quarter cup of white wine vinegar. Grated parmesan. A little water, a little sugar, Italian seasoning, garlic powder, salt, lemon juice.

And one more thing. The thing nobody puts in.

[TEKST: SAL SAYS: A SPOON OF MAYO]

Two tablespoons of mayonnaise.

I know. I know. An Italian chef telling you to put mayo in the dressing. My grandfather from Calabria is rolling over right now. Sorry, Grandpa Vincenzo. It works.

[B-ROLL: mayonnaise spooned into a mason jar of dressing, lid on, jar shaken until creamy]

Here's why. Oil and vinegar hate each other. You shake them, two minutes later they split up. Like a bad marriage. The mayo holds them together. It makes it creamy. And it doesn't separate in the fridge.

At Romano's, Angela made the staff meal salad every day at four o'clock. Fifteen cooks, one giant bowl. And every day the dressing split, and the guys at the end of the line got straight vinegar. One day she put a spoon of mayo in the jar. Never split again. The guys at the end of the line almost cried.

Shake it all in a jar. Chill it thirty minutes if you can. A jar of this keeps a week in the fridge. Make it Sunday, you got salad dressing all week. And it costs less than the bottle at the store. Dressing goes on just before you serve. Then croutons, so they stay crunchy. Little parmesan on top.

[B-ROLL: dressing poured over salad, tongs tossing, parmesan grated on top]

Beautiful. And it never has to end. Because it's your fridge.

## DISH 4: Cheddar Garlic Biscuits (8:40–11:01)

[SAL]
You know the biscuits. The cheesy, garlicky ones that come warm in a basket at the seafood chain. People go there for the shrimp. They leave talking about the biscuits.

[B-ROLL: a basket of golden cheddar biscuits, one broken open, steam coming out]

At home? About twenty cents a biscuit. A dozen for the price of a cup of coffee.

[TEKST: CHEDDAR BISCUITS: about $0.20 each]

And these are fast. Twenty-five minutes, start to finish. No yeast. No rising. No excuses.

You know what goes with these? Everything. Soup. Chili. Fried chicken. A cup of coffee at ten at night when nobody's watching. Me, I eat them standing at the counter. Angela hated that.

The most expensive thing here is the cheese. Buy the block, not the bag. Same reason as the alfredo. You grate it, it melts better, and it's cheaper by the pound.

Oven to four-fifty. Hot.

Two cups of flour. A tablespoon of baking powder. A little sugar, garlic powder, salt. Then half a cup of butter, cubed.

Cold butter. Right out of the fridge.

[TEKST: SAL SAYS: COLD BUTTER, QUICK HANDS]

Here's the trick. Cold butter, quick hands.

You rub the butter into the flour with your fingertips. Fast. Until it looks like coarse crumbs, little pebbles. Why fast? Because your hands are warm. Warm butter melts into the flour. And melted butter gives you hockey pucks.

Cold butter stays in little pieces. In the oven those pieces turn to steam. Steam makes pockets. Pockets make fluffy.

[B-ROLL: fingertips quickly rubbing cubes of cold butter into flour in a steel bowl]

Last Christmas my granddaughter wanted to make these with me. Nine years old. Beautiful kid. She takes the butter, she squeezes it, she rolls it, she's playing with it like it's clay. Ten minutes. We baked them anyway.

Hockey pucks. She could have played for New Jersey.

So now when we make them, she says it before I do. "Cold butter, quick hands, Grandpa." Second batch? Perfect.

Okay. Stir in a cup and a half of sharp cheddar. Then three-quarters of a cup of cold milk. Stir just until it comes together. Lumpy is fine. Lumpy is good. Don't overmix.

Twelve spoonfuls on a lined sheet. Ten to twelve minutes, until golden.

[B-ROLL: rough spoonfuls of dough dropped onto parchment, then pulled from the oven golden and craggy]

And right out of the oven? Melted butter, garlic powder, dried parsley. Brush them while they're hot.

Look at that. Crispy outside. Soft, cheesy inside.

And here's a bonus. Make the dough, drop the spoonfuls on a sheet, and freeze them. Then into a bag. Next time? Straight from the freezer into the oven, couple extra minutes. Warm biscuits on a Tuesday. Trust Sal.

## DISH 5: Smash Burgers with Special Sauce (11:01–13:21)

[SAL]
And now. The one my grandkids beg for.

The smash burger.

[B-ROLL: a double cheeseburger with melted American cheese dripping over crispy-edged patties]

Burger night for two at a sit-down place, with fries and drinks? Easily forty bucks these days. At home? About two-fifty a burger. And that's a double. With cheese.

[TEKST: SMASH BURGER (DOUBLE): about $2.50]

A pound of ground beef. Eighty-twenty. Not lean. You want the fat. Fat is flavor. Make eight loose balls. Don't pack them. Loose.

American cheese. Yes, American cheese. I trained in Milan and I'm telling you, on a burger, nothing melts like it. Soft potato buns, toasted with butter. Onion, sliced thin. Pickles. Shredded lettuce.

And the special sauce. Half a cup of mayo. Ketchup. Sweet relish. A little mustard. A splash of white vinegar. Pinch of sugar, salt, pepper. Mix it. Chill it.

That's the sauce every burger place is so secretive about. It's in your fridge right now.

[B-ROLL: sauce stirred in a small bowl, pink and creamy]

Now. Cast-iron pan. Or a griddle. Very hot. Hotter than you think.

Ball of beef down. Put a little square of parchment on top so the spatula doesn't stick. Then press with everything you got. Lean into it. And here's the whole secret.

[TEKST: SAL SAYS: SMASH ONCE. FIRST 10 SECONDS.]

You smash it in the first ten seconds. Hard. Thin. With a stiff spatula. And then you never touch it again.

[B-ROLL: a stiff spatula pressing a ball of beef flat on a smoking griddle, edges sizzling]

Why? In those first seconds the meat is cold and the pan is screaming hot. You smash it flat, all that beef touches the pan. And the edges go dark and crispy. Like lace. That crispy edge is the whole point.

Smash it again a minute later? You squeeze out all the juice. Now you got a dry, grey hockey puck. And we already had hockey pucks today. Basta. I mean... enough.

[SAL]
At Romano's, Sunday night after we closed, I made smash burgers for the whole staff. White tablecloths, candles, the fancy plates. And on the flat-top in the back? Burgers. My sous chef used to say it was the best thing we ever served. And it was never on the menu.

Salt and pepper. Two minutes, until the edges are dark. Flip. Cheese on. Thirty seconds.

Two patties. Toasted bun. Sauce. Onion. Pickles. Lettuce.

And toast the bun. Please. Butter, in the pan, thirty seconds. A cold bun on a hot burger? Who does that? Nobody you want to eat with. The toasted bun holds the sauce. The cold bun falls apart in your hands.

[B-ROLL: Sal stacking two cheesy patties on a toasted bun, sauce dripping down the side]

Madonna. Look at this burger.
