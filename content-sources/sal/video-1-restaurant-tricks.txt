The most expensive thing in a restaurant? It's not the steak. It's not the lobster.

It's the menu.

That little piece of paper they hand you? Somebody spent more time designing it than the chef spent on your dinner. And I know that...
because I was that somebody.

I'm Sal Romano. Sixteen years old, I was washing dishes in a chain restaurant. Then twenty-five years running Michelin-starred kitchens. New York. Milan. Then my own restaurant in New Jersey. Forty years. From the freezer bag...
to the tasting menu.

And now I'm retired. Which means nobody can fire me. Which means today, you get the truth.

Every restaurant you walk into is playing a game with your wallet. Sixteen tricks. Some of them I invented myself. God forgive me. And at the end? I'll tell you exactly what I order. So you can beat them at their own game.

Let's go.

Open a menu. Where do your eyes go first?

Middle. Top right. Top left. In the business, we call that the golden triangle. And whatever sits there is not the best dish.

It's the dish with the best *margin*.

At Romano's, top right of my menu? A chicken dish. Cheap chicken, cheap sauce, twenty-two dollars. Beautiful. My veal, the dish I was actually proud of? Bottom left. Hidden. Veal cost me a fortune, and I barely made a dime on it.

Rule number one. The dish in the spotlight is the dish *they* want you to order. Not the dish *you* want. Read the whole menu. The good stuff is hiding.

Now look for the box.

A little border around one dish. A shaded box. A tiny chili pepper. A photo. Your eye goes straight there. It can't help it.

And what's inside the box? You already know. The margin dish. Again.

At Romano's, I put a box around my penne alla vodka. Pasta, a little cream, a splash of vodka. Nineteen dollars. First month with that box? Penne orders went through the roof. I didn't change one thing in the pot. Just the box.

Photos? A fancy place almost never uses them. Photos say "family restaurant." But on a family menu, one good photo and that dish sells like crazy. And who picks which dish gets the photo? Not the chef. The guy with the calculator.

So when a dish is wearing a box...
read *around* the box. That's where the chef's favorites live.

Look at the prices. Something's missing.

No dollar sign. Just "24." That is not an accident. Cornell's hospitality school studied this. When people see a dollar sign, they spend less. The dollar sign reminds you it's real money. Take it away...
and it's just a number.

First week I opened Romano's? Dollar signs, gone. In the fine-dining kitchens, we never had them in the first place. My average check went up. Am I proud of it? No. Am I gonna lie to you about it? Also no.

So next time you see a menu with no dollar signs, do me a favor. Put it back. In your head. Every single time.

Quick one. Look at how the price *ends*.

Fast food loves a nine. Nine ninety-nine. Four ninety-five. That nine whispers "deal." "Bargain." "You're saving money."

A fancy place? Never. Round numbers. "Twenty-four." "Thirty-eight." Clean. A round number doesn't say "cheap." It says "quality." It says "don't even think about it."

In Milan, a new manager once printed a price ending in fifty cents. The owner saw it and went white. He made him reprint every single menu that night. "We are not a gas station!" He was right. Nobody pays three hundred dollars for a tasting menu that ends in ninety-nine.

So don't let the ending set the mood. Nine ninety-nine isn't automatically a deal. Thirty-eight isn't automatically quality. Look at the plate. Not the punctuation.

Oh, this one is beautiful. Evil...
but beautiful.

Every menu has one crazy expensive item. The seventy-nine dollar seafood tower. The ninety dollar tomahawk. And you think, who orders that?

Nobody. That's the point.

That dish is there to make everything else look cheap. You see seventy-nine first, and suddenly the thirty-four dollar steak looks reasonable. Smart, even. You feel careful. Responsible. Meanwhile, you just paid thirty-four dollars for a steak that cost the restaurant maybe nine.

It's called anchoring. I had a "Chef's Seafood Platter for Two" on my menu for years. Sold maybe four a month. But let me tell you...
it sold a *lot* of shrimp scampi.

So when you see that crazy price? Smile at it. "Thank you. I see what you're doing." Then look at what you actually want. On its own.

You want to know where a restaurant really makes its money? Not the food.

The drinks.

A fountain soda costs pennies. Syrup, bubbles, ice. You pay three, four dollars. Free refills? Of course free refills. The refills cost them nothing either.

Coffee, same story. Iced tea? Even better. Iced tea might be the highest-margin thing in the whole building.

And wine...
madonna. A bottle that costs the restaurant fifteen dollars goes on the list for forty-five, fifty. Three times the cost. Totally normal in this business.

Now here's an old wine trick. Nobody wants to order the cheapest bottle. Too embarrassing. So they order the second cheapest. Some owners know that. So they put a bottle with an even bigger markup right there. In the second-cheapest spot. Not everybody does it. But enough do.

My advice? Just thirsty? Water. Done. Want wine? Ask the server one question: "What's the best value on this list?" A good server knows exactly where the deal is.

Listen to how the server talks. They don't say "would you like an appetizer?" No, no, no. They say, "Can I start you off with the spinach dip...
or the calamari?"

Hear the difference? The first question has an easy answer. No. The second one makes you choose. Your brain goes "which one?" instead of "do I even need one?"

We trained our servers to do this. Everybody does. It's called suggestive selling, and it works on smart people too. It worked on *me*. And I wrote half those lines!

And appetizers? After drinks, often the most profitable thing on the menu. Fried, cheap, big price.

So when they say "can I start you off with...
," you smile. "We're good, thanks. Straight to the mains." Boom. Twelve, fifteen dollars, saved.

And then comes the little question. The add-on.

"Make it a double?" "Add chicken to that salad?" "Want avocado on that?"

Sounds like service. It's arithmetic. A few slices of avocado? Three dollars. A piece of grilled chicken on your salad? Six, seven dollars. For a piece of chicken the kitchen buys by the case.

And the beauty is how small it feels. Three dollars here. Four dollars there. Who says no to three dollars? Nobody. But four people, two add-ons each? That's a whole extra entrée on the bill.

At Romano's I had a server, Tony. Sweetest kid. Every single table: "Want some shrimp on that?" Tony's checks were always the biggest in the room. I gave him a raise. Then I gave everybody his script.

So before you say yes...
do the math out loud. "Six dollars for chicken?" Half the time, you'll laugh and say no.

Quick one before we keep going. Every rule I learned in forty years, I put on one page. What to order. What to skip. What to ask your server. "Sal's 25 Rules for Eating Out." Free. First link in the description. Print it, put it in your wallet, and next time you go out? You're the smartest person at the table.

Okay. We're halfway. Eight more. And they get sneakier.

Now let me be fair, because I love a real special. The kitchen gets beautiful fresh fish that morning, and the chef can't wait to cook it? That's a gift. Order it.

But a special can also be something else.

Sometimes it's how you use up product before it goes bad. You turn it into a pasta, a soup, a "chef's creation." Give it a fancy name, and it's gone by nine o'clock.

Every restaurant does this a little. It's not illegal. It's usually not dangerous. It's business. You don't throw money in the garbage.

But notice something. The special almost never has a price next to it. The server says it out loud. You're too polite to ask...
and then the bill comes. That special was eight dollars more than anything on the menu.

Two rules. One: "How much is the special?" Nobody thinks you're cheap. They think you're smart. Two: "What came in fresh today?" That one question separates the real special from the clean-out-the-fridge special.

"Hand-crafted." "Slow-roasted." "Farm-fresh." "Artisan."

On a menu, most of these words mean nothing. Legally, nothing. "Artisan bread" can come out of a freezer bag. "Slow-roasted" means it was cooked slowly. Somewhere. Sometime. Maybe in a factory.

Researchers have studied this for years. Describe a dish with prettier words, and people say it tastes better. And they pay more. Same food. Better words. Higher price.

At Romano's, my "Nonna's Sunday Gravy" was the same marinara I put on everything else...
plus sausage. Delicious? Absolutely. My nonna's recipe? Well...
she was from the same region.

So read the menu like a contract. Ignore the adjectives. Find the noun. "Chicken." "Pasta." "Potatoes." That's what you're buying.

Free bread. Free chips. Why? Because they love you?

They like you, sure. But salty bread and salty chips make you thirsty. Thirsty people order another drink. And we already know what the drinks are about.

At Romano's, Angela baked focaccia every morning. Warm, salty, olive oil on top. Our soda sales? Beautiful.

The bread does one more job. It keeps you happy while the kitchen is slow. A happy table doesn't complain. And a table that doesn't complain...
tips better.

Am I telling you to skip the bread? Never. Eat the bread! Enjoy the bread! Just notice when you're on soda number three and you only came in for a plate of pasta.

Nobody believes this one. But it's true.

The restaurant controls how fast you eat.

Studies going back to the eighties. Slow music, people stay longer. Stay longer, order more drinks, more dessert. Fast music, people eat fast and leave. So a busy lunch spot plays it fast. A nice dinner place plays it slow.

Lights, same game. Bright lights, hard chairs: eat and go. Dim lights, soft chairs, candles: stay, have another glass, look at the dessert menu.

Friday night at Romano's? Lights down, Dean Martin on. Tuesday lunch? Lights up, music faster. I needed those tables back.

Nothing wrong with any of it. Just know the mood is part of the menu. So when you think "one more glass would be nice"...
ask yourself. Is that you? Or is that Dean Martin?

This one breaks hearts.

A lot of restaurant desserts were never made in that restaurant.

Food distributors sell restaurants frozen cheesecake, frozen tiramisu, frozen lava cake. Thaw it. A strawberry on top. A little chocolate drizzle on the plate...
ten dollars a slice.

Is it bad? No. Some of it is pretty good. But it's the same cake you'd find in a grocery store freezer for a fraction of the price.

At Romano's? My wife Angela made every dessert by hand. Her tiramisu was the reason half our customers came back. But plenty of places don't. And honestly, I get it. Real dessert takes a pastry chef's whole morning.

So ask. "Are the desserts made here?" If they hesitate, or say "it's from a bakery"...
now you know. If their eyes light up and they say "oh yes, the owner's wife makes them"? Order two.

Now let's talk about free stuff. Everybody loves free.

"Join our club, get a free dessert on your birthday!" Beautiful. Who says no to free cake?

But think about it. Do you go out alone on your birthday? No. You bring six people. Six dinners. Six drinks. Maybe a bottle of wine for the table. That free slice of cake? Cheapest advertising in the whole building.

Same with the points card. "Buy nine, the tenth one's free." So you come back to the same place nine times. You never try the new spot down the street. They didn't give you a dessert. They bought your loyalty for the price of one.

At Romano's, we sang for every birthday. Angela put a candle in a cannoli. Cost me maybe a dollar. That family came back for every birthday for eleven years. Best dollar I ever spent.

Take the free dessert. Enjoy it! Sing along! Just don't plan your whole night around it. Free cake...
is never the whole story.

Two ways to make you feel like you got a deal. Give you more food...
or make it *look* like more food.

Big plates make portions look small. So places that want to look generous use smaller plates. Or wide-rim plates that make a little bit of food look elegant. Then they pile on cheap pasta, cheap fries, and the plate looks full. Meanwhile the expensive part, the meat, the fish? Small.

And "upgrade your side for just two dollars"? Almost pure profit. A potato costs pennies. A side salad costs pennies.

So look at the expensive part of the plate. How much steak? How much shrimp? That's what you're paying for. The fries are decoration.

And the last one. My favorite.

"Market price."

Sometimes it's honest. Lobster, some fish, the price really changes every week.

But some people never ask. They're on a date. They're with the boss. They don't want to look cheap. And restaurants know it. So "market price" becomes whatever the market will pay. And the market, my friend...
is you.

I had a guy once. Lovely man. Anniversary dinner, ordered two lobsters, never asked the price. When the check came...
his face. I felt so bad I took one lobster off the bill. A lot of places won't.

Always. Ask. The price. Out loud. Nobody who matters will judge you.

So. Now you know the tricks. Here's what Sal orders when Sal eats out.

One. Water, or one drink. That's it.

Two. I skip the appetizer, unless the kitchen is clearly proud of it.

Three. "What came in fresh today?" and "How much is the special?" Every time.

Four. I order what's hard to make at home. A braise. A real sauce. Fresh pasta. Something that took somebody hours. Twenty-two dollars for chicken parm I can make for four? Come on. Not a chance.

And five. Desserts made in house? I share one. Not made in house? I go home and have a cookie.

And speaking of making it at home. My grandkids kept asking me, "Nonno, how do you make the breadsticks from that Italian chain? The alfredo?" So I wrote it all down.
