## HOOK (0:00–1:01)

[SAL]
Twenty dollars for a plate of food. Somebody heated it up.

Not cooked. Heated.

[B-ROLL: a sealed plastic bag of frozen food dropped into a pot of simmering water]

I'm not talking about some gas station. I'm talking about sit-down restaurants. Tablecloth. Server. Tip. And the dish came in on a truck... frozen, par-baked, or sealed in a bag.

Is frozen food bad? No! I'm gonna say that a lot today. Frozen is not a crime. My own freezer is full.

But here's my problem. You're paying fresh-restaurant prices... for reheated food.

[TEKST: FRESH PRICE. REHEATED FOOD.]

I'm Sal Romano. I started washing dishes in a chain restaurant at sixteen. I unloaded those trucks myself. Then twenty-five years running Michelin-starred kitchens in New York and Milan. Then twelve years in my own place in New Jersey.

So I know what comes in a box. And I know what comes in a bag.

Today, nine dishes. Some I'll name, because it's been publicly reported. Some I won't, because it's the whole industry. And for every single one, I'll tell you how to get it fresh. At home, or by ordering something smarter.

Let's go.

## DISH 1: The "Fresh-Baked" Bread at Panera (1:01–2:35)

[SAL]
Let's start with bread. Because bread is personal.

[B-ROLL: golden loaves coming out of a deep oven, steam rising]

Panera built its whole name on bread baked in the café. Beautiful idea. For years, the dough came from Panera's own fresh dough facilities, delivered to the cafés.

But that's changing. Panera has been closing those facilities. And according to reporting in the trade press, Restaurant Business and Nation's Restaurant News, the new model is this: outside bakeries make the dough with Panera's recipes. They par-bake it. It's frozen. Then the café finishes it in the oven.

[TEKST: Par-baked = partly baked, frozen, finished in the oven]

Now, fair is fair. Panera says this lets them bake all day long, not just at five in the morning. You want a bagel at four in the afternoon? You get a warm bagel. That's a real benefit. I respect it.

But listen. "Baked in the café" and "finished in the café" are two different sentences.

At sixteen, my first job was the chain's "fresh bread." I pulled frozen rolls out of a box, put them on a tray, and baked them for twelve minutes. The manager called it "baking." I called it "warming." He didn't like that. I was sixteen. I had a mouth.

And the customers? They loved those rolls. They'd say, "Smell that, fresh bread!" And I'd smile. Because the smell is real. The oven is real. Only the story is a little bit shorter.

[SAL]
The fix? Real bread is flour, water, yeast, salt, and time. A simple loaf at home costs you under a dollar. Or find the little bakery in your town that opens at five a.m. Their bread was born in that building.

## DISH 2: The Breadsticks at Olive Garden (2:35–3:58)

[SAL]
Now the most famous breadstick in America.

[B-ROLL: warm breadsticks in a basket, butter glistening]

Olive Garden's website says freshly baked. And they are baked in the restaurant. Out of the oven, brushed with butter and garlic salt. That part is true.

But where does the dough start? Back in 2000, Olive Garden's parent company, Darden, publicly honored an outside bakery. Turano Baking. For supplying breadsticks to its Midwest restaurants. That's in their own press release.

[TEKST: Supplier named by Darden, 2000]

And according to people who say they worked there, as reported by food sites like Tasting Table, the sticks arrive par-baked. Then the kitchen finishes them.

So is that terrible? No. Honestly? It's smart. When you serve that many breadsticks, you need every one the same. I get it.

But here's the thing. The breadstick is "free." It's unlimited. It's the hook. And the hook... comes from a factory.

At Romano's, Angela made focaccia every morning at six. I used to steal the corner piece. Every day. Twelve years. She knew. She cut it bigger for me.

That's the difference. Bread from a factory is the same every day. Bread from a person? Some days it's a little different. And somehow that's the bread you remember.

[SAL]
The fix? Breadsticks are the easiest bread in the world. Two hours, mostly waiting. Roll the dough into ropes, bake them, then brush with garlic butter. Twice. That second brush? That's the secret. About a quarter each. First recipe in my cookbook.

## DISH 3: The Mozzarella Sticks (Almost Everywhere) (3:58–5:19)

[SAL]
I'm not naming a chain on this one. Because it's almost all of them.

[B-ROLL: golden fried cheese sticks, cheese pulling apart]

Mozzarella sticks. Breaded, frozen, in a big case from the food distributor. The kitchen drops them in the fryer. Three minutes. Out. Marinara cup. Ten, twelve dollars.

[TEKST: Frozen from the distributor → fryer → your table]

Who breads their own mozzarella sticks in a busy casual restaurant? Almost nobody. Too slow. Too messy. The cheese leaks if you don't freeze them anyway.

And look, I've eaten a thousand of them. They're fun. Hot cheese. Crunchy outside. Nobody's crying.

But you can buy the same kind of stick in the grocery freezer. Box of them. For about the price of one restaurant order. Same fryer idea. Same cheese pull.

At my first chain job, appetizers were my station. Seventeen years old. Mozzarella sticks, onion rings, poppers. All from the same freezer. All the same three minutes. The cooks had a name for that station. "The Microwave With a Fryer." Not very nice. Pretty accurate.

Friday night, I'd open a new case every hour. Two hundred sticks. Never touched a block of cheese all night. Not once.

[SAL]
The fix? At home, cut fresh mozzarella into sticks. Flour, egg, breadcrumbs, then twice more. Freeze them thirty minutes, so they don't leak. Then fry. Fifteen minutes of work. Twice the cheese.

At the restaurant? Skip the frozen appetizer. Ask what the kitchen actually makes in the building.

## DISH 4: The Mac and Cheese at Panera (5:19–6:37)

[SAL]
Mac and cheese. The comfort dish. The one your grandkids order every time.

[B-ROLL: creamy mac and cheese in a bowl, spoon lifting a cheesy bite]

According to people who worked there, as reported by Business Insider back in 2019, a lot of Panera's food is shipped to the store in a bag. And that includes the mac and cheese. Same report: the soups come pre-packaged too.

[TEKST: "Shipped to the store in a bag." Employees, via Business Insider]

So the kitchen heats it. Scoops it. Bowl. Done.

Is it tasty? Plenty of people love it. Creamy, cheesy, consistent. Hey, consistency is hard. I'll give them that.

But think about what you're paying for. Mac and cheese is the cheapest dish on earth. Pasta, butter, milk, cheese.

My granddaughter is seven. Last summer she told me, "Nonno, your mac and cheese is better than the restaurant." I said, "Of course. Mine is made by a man who loves you." She said, "No, yours has the crunchy top." Kids. Brutally honest.

But she's right. The crunchy top only happens in an oven. A bag can't give you that. A steam table can't give you that. Only a hot oven and a little patience.

[SAL]
The fix? Real baked mac and cheese. Cook the pasta one minute short. Make a quick sauce with butter, flour, milk, and sharp cheddar you grate yourself. Into a dish, breadcrumbs on top, oven until golden. Feeds the whole family for less than one bowl out.

## DISH 5: The Biscuits at Cracker Barrel (6:37–7:57)

[SAL]
Now this one hurts. Because Cracker Barrel's biscuits are a legend.

[B-ROLL: a basket of fluffy golden biscuits, butter melting on one]

But in September 2025, the New York Post and The Independent reported on what employees said had changed in early 2025. According to those employees, the biscuits stopped being made fresh every day. Baked a day ahead, frozen, then reheated in hot boxes.

[TEKST: Employees, as reported by NY Post / The Independent (Sept 2025)]

One more thing to be fair. That's what employees told reporters, about that time. Things change. Kitchens change. Maybe your location is different today.

But a biscuit? A biscuit has one job. Be warm, flaky, and fresh. A day-old biscuit is not a biscuit. It's a hockey puck with a résumé.

And I still say this about Cracker Barrel. That country breakfast, that big plate, eggs to order? That's still a real meal at a fair price. Credit where it's due.

My mother made biscuits in a cast iron pan. Every Sunday. Cold butter, buttermilk, hot oven. Fifteen minutes. We ate them standing at the counter, burning our fingers. She'd slap my hand. "Wait for your father!" I never waited. Neither did my father.

[SAL]
The fix? Biscuits are the fastest bread there is. Flour, baking powder, cold butter, buttermilk. Don't overwork it. Fold it twice for layers. Hot oven, twelve minutes. Make a double batch and freeze the raw dough. That's frozen done right. You bake them fresh, whenever you want.

## DISH 6: The "Slow-Braised" Short Rib (8:20–9:35)

[SAL]
No chain name here. Because this is the whole casual dining business.

[B-ROLL: a dark, glossy braised short rib on mashed potatoes]

"Slow-braised." "Fall-off-the-bone." "Six hours." Beautiful words. And the short rib probably was braised for six hours.

Just not in that kitchen.

[B-ROLL: vacuum-sealed bags of cooked meat in a bath of hot water]

A lot of these dishes are cooked in a big food plant. Sealed in a vacuum bag. Chilled or frozen. Shipped. Then in the restaurant, the bag goes into hot water or a steamer. Cut it open. Plate it. Sauce from another bag on top.

[TEKST: Cooked somewhere else. Reheated here.]

I'm not mad at the technique. We used bags in Michelin kitchens too. Sous vide. But we cooked it ourselves, that morning, in that building. That's the difference.

In Milan, a young cook once served a short rib straight from yesterday's bag, cold in the middle. The guest sent it back. My chef tasted it. Didn't yell. Just slid the plate back and said, "Tomorrow you start at five." He did. Never happened again. Today he runs his own kitchen in Turin. And I promise you, his short rib is born in his building.

[SAL]
The fix? Braising is the easiest thing a home cook can do. Brown the meat. Onion, carrot, tomato paste, red wine. Lid on. Low oven, three hours. You do nothing. You watch TV. And it's better than the bag.

## DISH 7: The Meatloaf at Cracker Barrel (9:35–10:53)

[SAL]
Back to Cracker Barrel. Same reporting. Same employees.

[B-ROLL: thick slices of meatloaf with tomato glaze on a plate]

According to employees, as reported by the New York Post and The Independent, the meatloaf changed in early 2025 too. Before? Cooked in the oven, cut right from the pan on the line. After? Made the day before, then microwaved for about a minute before serving.

[TEKST: "Microwave it for just over a minute." Employees, Sept 2025]

Meatloaf is homey food. You order it because it reminds you of Mom. And Mom never microwaved the first slice. Maybe the leftovers on Tuesday. Not the first slice.

That's not cooking. That's reheating.

Again, fair is fair. Meatloaf is one of the few dishes that's actually good the next day. Cold meatloaf sandwich? I'd fight you for it. But that's at home. For free. Not on a restaurant check.

At Romano's, we made meatloaf only on Wednesdays. One big batch, out of the oven at five. When it was gone, it was gone. People called at four to reserve a slice. That's how you treat meatloaf. With respect.

[SAL]
The fix? Meatloaf at home. Beef and pork, half and half. Bread soaked in milk, so it stays tender. Egg, onion, parmesan. Shape it free on a sheet pan, not in a loaf pan, so it gets a crust all around. Ketchup glaze, last fifteen minutes. And the next day? Microwave a slice. Totally fine. You earned it.

## DISH 8: The Molten Lava Cake (10:53–12:12)

[SAL]
No name on this one either. Because you'll find it on almost every casual dining menu in America.

[B-ROLL: a chocolate lava cake split open, warm chocolate flowing, scoop of vanilla ice cream]

The molten chocolate cake. Big food distributors sell them frozen. By the case. Individually wrapped. The kitchen pops one in the microwave or the oven. Ice cream on top. Chocolate drizzle. Nine, ten dollars.

[TEKST: Frozen by the case → 60 seconds → $10]

And you know what? That's how it works in a lot of places. A busy chain can't have a pastry chef on every shift.

But a real lava cake is not hard. It's maybe the easiest "fancy" dessert there is.

At Romano's, Angela made lava cakes to order. Butter, chocolate, eggs, sugar, a spoon of flour. Into the little cups. Twelve minutes in the oven. One night a customer asked if they were frozen. Angela walked out of the kitchen, in her apron, and served him personally. Told him, "Watch me bake the next one." He became a regular for nine years. Always the same table. Always the lava cake. And every time, he'd look at me and say, "Not frozen, right?" Every time.

[SAL]
The fix? Make them at home. Four cups, about twelve minutes. Make the batter in the afternoon, keep the cups in the fridge, bake when you sit down to dinner. Or at the restaurant, ask one question: "Is the dessert made here?" If they hesitate... you got your answer.

## DISH 9: The Cheesecake at The Cheesecake Factory (12:12–13:39)

[SAL]
And the last one. My favorite. Because it's right there in the name.

[B-ROLL: a tall slice of strawberry cheesecake on a white plate]

The Cheesecake Factory's cheesecake is not made in the restaurant. This one isn't a rumor. It's in the company's own annual report. Their bakery division runs two facilities. One in California, one in North Carolina. They produce the cheesecakes and desserts for the restaurants. Then they ship them out.

[TEKST: Made in 2 company bakeries, not in the restaurant (company annual report)]

Now here's the twist. And I love this. The same report says that substantially all of their other menu items are prepared from scratch, daily, in the restaurant. Everything except the desserts from the bakery.

So the one thing in the name... is the one thing they don't make there.

Madonna. You can't write that better.

And honestly? Those cheesecakes are good. Consistent. Huge. A factory bakery does that well. But you're paying restaurant prices, per slice, for a cake that rode a truck.

Angela made cheesecake at Romano's. Ricotta, Calabrian style, lemon zest. Tall, but light. Every few weeks a customer would ask her for the recipe. She'd smile and say, "Come back tomorrow, I'll make you another slice." Never gave it out. Not even to me.

[SAL]
The fix? At the restaurant, flip it. Order the fresh stuff they cook in the building, and skip the slice. At home? A classic New York cheesecake is cream cheese, sugar, eggs, sour cream, a graham crust. Bake it low, let it cool in the oven. One cake is twelve slices. For less than two slices out.
