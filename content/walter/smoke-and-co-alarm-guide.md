For smoke alarms, NFPA's guidance is simple: one inside every bedroom, one outside each sleeping area, and at least one on every level of the home, including the basement. For carbon monoxide alarms, put one outside each sleeping area and on every level, and follow the maker's instructions for exactly where and how high to mount it.

Then do the part people skip: test them every month, and replace them when they get old. Smoke alarms should be replaced every 10 years. CO alarms usually have a shorter life, often somewhere around 5 to 7 years, and the date is printed on the back.

Here's the plain version of where they go, where they don't, and how to keep them working.

## Smoke alarms vs. CO alarms

They protect against two different dangers, and you need both.

- **Smoke alarms** sense smoke from a fire. They buy you time to get out.
- **Carbon monoxide (CO) alarms** sense CO, a gas you can't see or smell. It comes from burning fuel: gas or oil furnaces, water heaters, gas stoves and dryers, fireplaces, and cars in an attached garage.

If your home has any fuel-burning appliance or an attached garage, you need CO alarms. Combination smoke and CO alarms exist, and they're a fine way to cover both, as long as they're placed where both kinds of alarms belong.

## Where smoke alarms go

Following NFPA's guidance:

- **Inside every bedroom.**
- **Outside each sleeping area,** in the hallway that leads to the bedrooms.
- **On every level of the home,** including the basement. On levels without bedrooms, a living room or family room or near the stairs is a good spot.
- **On the ceiling or high on a wall.** Smoke rises. If you mount on a wall, follow the manufacturer's directions on how far down from the ceiling.
- **Away from the kitchen stove.** NFPA recommends at least 10 feet from a cooking appliance to cut down on nuisance alarms.

In larger homes, you may need more than one per level. And NFPA recommends **interconnected** alarms, so when one sounds, they all do. That way a fire in the basement wakes someone in a bedroom upstairs. Many newer homes have hardwired, interconnected alarms; wireless interconnected models are available for older homes.

## Where CO alarms go

Following NFPA and the manufacturer:

- **Outside each separate sleeping area,** close enough to wake people.
- **On every level of the home,** including the basement.
- **Wherever local codes or laws require them.**
- **At the height and location the manufacturer specifies.** CO mixes with air, and different models are tested for different mounting spots, so read the instructions instead of guessing.

## Where not to put them

A badly placed alarm either goes off all the time (and gets unplugged) or doesn't sense danger in time. Avoid:

- **Right next to the stove or toaster,** or right outside a steamy bathroom
- **Near windows, doors or air vents,** where drafts can push smoke or gas away from the sensor
- **Inside the furnace room right next to the furnace** for CO alarms, unless the maker says so; follow the instructions
- **Dead-air spots,** like the very peak of a sharply pitched ceiling or the corner where wall meets ceiling, unless the instructions allow it
- **Garages, attics and other unheated spaces,** unless the alarm is made for those temperatures

## Testing and batteries

- **Test every alarm monthly** with the test button. It takes a few minutes for the whole house.
- **Make sure everyone knows the sound,** and that the smoke and CO signals are different on combination units.
- **Change batteries** in replaceable-battery alarms at least once a year, or when they chirp. Changing them when the clocks change is an easy habit. Sealed 10-year alarms don't need battery changes.
- **Use a sturdy step stool,** not a chair, and don't stretch off the top of a ladder.
- **Never pull the battery to stop a nuisance alarm.** Fan the air, open a window, or move the alarm to a better spot.

If anyone in the house is hard of hearing, there are alarms with strobe lights and bed shakers. And if an alarm chirps once every minute or so, that's usually a low battery or an end-of-life warning. Deal with it the same day.

## When to replace them

- **Smoke alarms: every 10 years,** per NFPA, even if the test button still works. The sensor wears out.
- **CO alarms:** follow the manufacturer's end-of-life date. Many last around 5 to 7 years, and newer ones often chirp or show a message when they reach the end.
- **Look at the date on the back.** If there's no date, or it's older than you can remember, replace it.
- **Hardwired alarms** that need replacing are often a simple swap with a matching model, but if you're not comfortable with wiring, have an electrician do it.

## What to do when an alarm goes off, and who to call

**Smoke alarm:** get out and stay out. Call 911 from outside. Have two ways out of every bedroom, and a meeting spot outside. Our [nightly home safety routine](../nightly-home-safety-routine/) has a few easy habits that help.

**CO alarm:** get everyone outside into fresh air right away. Call 911 or the fire department from outside. Don't go back in until responders say it's safe, even if the alarm stops. Afterward, have a qualified technician check your fuel-burning appliances. Our guide to [furnace warning signs](../furnace-warning-signs/) explains what they'll look at.

### When to call a pro

- An electrician for hardwired or interconnected alarms you'd like added or replaced.
- An HVAC technician or plumber if a CO alarm has gone off, even once.
- The fire department non-emergency line if you're unsure about placement. Many fire departments help with alarm questions, and some offer free alarms or installation.

## Bottom line

- Smoke alarms go in every bedroom, outside sleeping areas and on every level; keep them at least 10 feet from the stove.
- CO alarms go outside sleeping areas and on every level, mounted the way the maker says.
- Test monthly, and replace smoke alarms every 10 years and CO alarms by their end-of-life date.
- If one goes off, get out first and ask questions from outside.
