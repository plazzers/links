A GFCI outlet is the one with the TEST and RESET buttons in the middle. GFCI stands for ground fault circuit interrupter. Its job is to cut the power in a fraction of a second if electricity starts leaking somewhere it shouldn't, like through water or through a person. That's why you find them near sinks, in bathrooms, in garages and outside.

A regular breaker protects the wiring from overheating. A GFCI protects *you* from a serious shock. They're one of the simplest, most useful safety devices in a house, and testing one takes seconds.

Here's how they work, where you should have them, how to test them, and when it's time to call an electrician.

## How a GFCI works, in plain English

Electricity goes out through one wire and comes back through another. Normally the amount going out and the amount coming back match.

A GFCI keeps watching both. If a tiny bit of current goes missing, it means that current is finding another path to the ground. That path might be a puddle, a wet hand, or someone's body. When the GFCI sees that mismatch, it trips and shuts off power almost instantly, quickly enough to help prevent a serious injury.

A GFCI is not the same as a three-prong grounded outlet, either. It can add shock protection to an older two-prong circuit with no ground wire, but that's a decision for an electrician, and those outlets should be labeled.

It doesn't stop every electrical hazard, and it's no excuse to use a hair dryer in the bath. But it's a big layer of protection in the rooms where water and electricity meet.

## Where your home should have GFCI protection

Requirements have grown over the years, so a newer home usually has more of them than an older one. As a practical guide, I'd want GFCI protection at:

- **Kitchen counter outlets,** especially near the sink
- **Bathrooms**
- **Laundry areas** and near utility sinks
- **Garages**
- **Outdoor outlets**
- **Unfinished basements and crawlspaces**
- **Anywhere near a sink, tub, pool or hot tub**

An older house may have none of these, or only a few. That isn't unusual, but it's worth fixing. A licensed electrician can add GFCI protection without rewiring the house. If you're buying, note where they are and where they're missing, and see our [buying an older home checklist](../buying-an-older-home-checklist/) for other electrical things to look at.

## One GFCI can protect several outlets

This confuses a lot of people. A single GFCI outlet can be wired to protect other, ordinary-looking outlets further along the same circuit. So a plain outlet in a bathroom might be protected by a GFCI in the next bathroom, the garage or even the basement.

Signs an outlet is protected that way:

- A small sticker that says "GFCI Protected"
- It goes dead when you press TEST on another GFCI nearby

Some homes also have GFCI protection built into breakers in the electrical panel instead of the outlets. If you have outlets that seem protected but you can't find the GFCI, ask an electrician to show you. Don't take the panel cover off yourself.

## How to test a GFCI outlet

The CPSC recommends testing GFCIs regularly, and monthly is an easy habit. It goes like this:

1. **Plug something in** that shows power, like a night light or a small lamp, and turn it on.
2. **Press TEST.** You should hear a click, and the light should go out.
3. **Check the other outlets** on that circuit if you know which ones they are. They should be off too.
4. **Press RESET.** The light should come back on.

If that all works, the GFCI is doing its job.

Outlet testers with a GFCI button are also handy for checking outlets that are protected by a GFCI somewhere else. They're inexpensive and plug right in.

## What the results mean

- **Light goes out on TEST, comes back on RESET:** working as designed.
- **Light stays on after TEST:** the GFCI isn't tripping. It may have failed, or it may be wired wrong. Stop relying on it and call an electrician.
- **RESET won't stay in:** there may be a fault on the circuit, or the device may be worn out. Don't keep forcing it.
- **Nothing has power, before or after:** the GFCI may already be tripped, a breaker may be off, or an upstream GFCI may be tripped. Check other GFCIs nearby first.
- **It trips again and again:** something on that circuit may be leaking current, such as a damaged cord, a wet outdoor outlet or an appliance with a fault. Unplug things one at a time to see if it stops. If it keeps happening, that's an electrician call.

GFCIs do wear out over time, and older ones may stop protecting you without any obvious sign. That's another reason to test them.

## What to leave to an electrician

Testing is a homeowner job. Wiring is not. Call a licensed electrician to:

- Replace a GFCI that fails the test or won't reset
- Add GFCI protection to kitchens, baths, garages or outdoor outlets that don't have it
- Track down repeated tripping that you can't trace to one plug-in appliance
- Look at any outlet that's warm, cracked, scorched or buzzing
- Work on anything inside the electrical panel

While they're there, ask about AFCI protection too. Those devices are aimed at a different hazard, arcing in wiring that can start fires, and newer codes require them in many rooms.

## A few habits that help

- Test your GFCIs every month, or at least when the clocks change.
- Keep outdoor outlet covers closed, and use covers that stay closed with a cord plugged in.
- Don't use extension cords as permanent wiring to reach a spot that needs its own outlet.
- Add GFCI testing to your [monthly house walk](../monthly-house-walk/) so it doesn't get forgotten.

## The short version

- A GFCI cuts power fast when current leaks where it shouldn't, protecting people from shock.
- You want them in kitchens, baths, laundry areas, garages, outdoors and unfinished basements.
- Press TEST (power goes off), then RESET (power comes back). Monthly is a good habit.
- If one won't trip, won't reset or keeps tripping, call a licensed electrician.
