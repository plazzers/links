Summer storms come in fast. Heavy rain, strong wind, hail and lightning can show up with an afternoon's notice, and in some parts of the country hurricane season runs all summer and into the fall. The good news is that most storm prep for your house is done well before the sky turns dark.

The goal is simple: **keep water out, keep things from flying into the house, and be ready to go a few days without power.** None of that requires climbing on the roof or doing anything risky.

Here's the checklist I'd work through at the start of summer, plus what to do when a storm is actually on the way.

## Trees and branches

Falling limbs do a lot of storm damage. Walk the yard and look up.

- **Dead branches** — no leaves when everything else is green — are the first to come down.
- **Limbs hanging over the roof** can scrape shingles in wind or fall through in a bad storm.
- **Trees leaning toward the house**, or with cracks, hollow spots or mushrooms at the base, deserve a closer look.

Small, low branches you can reach from the ground with a pole saw are fine to trim yourself. Anything big, high, or near power lines is a job for a tree service or certified arborist. Never trim near power lines yourself.

## Roof, gutters and drainage

A storm puts a lot of water on your roof in a short time. Everything has to flow.

- **Look at the roof from the ground** with binoculars. Missing or lifted shingles and loose flashing let wind-driven rain in. If something looks wrong, call a roofer — don't climb up.
- **Clear the gutters** if they're full of seeds, leaves or debris from spring. Only from a ladder on a single-story house with level ground; otherwise hire a gutter service. The [fall gutter and drainage guide](../fall-gutter-and-drainage-guide/) has the ladder safety basics.
- **Check every downspout** sends water several feet from the foundation.
- **Look at the ground** near the house. It should slope away so heavy rain runs off, not in.

## Sump pump and basement

If your basement has a sump pump, summer storms are when it earns its keep — and when power outages can stop it cold.

1. **Test it.** Slowly pour a bucket of water into the pit. The pump should start on its own and empty it. Step-by-step instructions are in [how to test a sump pump](../how-to-test-a-sump-pump/).
2. **Check the discharge pipe outside** is clear and sends water away from the house.
3. **Ask about a backup.** A battery backup or water-powered backup pump can keep running when the power goes out. A plumber or waterproofing contractor can tell you what fits your house.
4. **Move valuables off the basement floor**, especially papers and electronics.

Ready.gov's flood guidance also suggests keeping important documents in a waterproof container and knowing whether your area is flood-prone. Standard homeowner policies often don't cover flood, so it's worth asking your insurance agent.

## Loose items around the yard

Strong wind turns patio furniture into projectiles.

- Know what you'll bring inside or tie down: chairs, umbrellas, grills, planters, trash cans, toys, trampolines.
- Close and secure patio umbrellas — they catch wind like a sail.
- If you live in a hurricane area, check your storm shutters or pre-cut plywood at the start of the season, not when a storm is two days out.

## Windows, doors and the garage

- **Check that windows close and latch** properly. A window that doesn't seal lets wind-driven rain in.
- **Look at caulk and weatherstripping** around windows and doors.
- **Garage doors** are often the weakest point in high wind. If you're in a hurricane area and your door isn't rated for wind, ask a garage door company about bracing options.

## Get ready for a power outage

Ready.gov recommends being prepared to manage on your own for several days. Before the season:

- **Flashlights and fresh batteries.** Skip candles — they're a fire risk.
- **Water and non-perishable food** for everyone in the house, including pets.
- **Medicines** you'd need for a few days, and a plan for anything that needs power or refrigeration.
- **Phone chargers and a battery bank**, charged.
- **A first-aid kit.**
- **Know how to open your garage door manually** if the power is off.
- **Take photos or a short video of each room** for insurance. Keep a copy somewhere other than your phone.

### Generator safety

If you use a portable generator, this is the single most important rule: **run it outdoors only, far from doors, windows and vents.** Never in a garage, basement or porch, even with the door open. The CPSC warns that generator exhaust contains carbon monoxide, which you can't see or smell and which can kill quickly.

- Have working **CO alarms** inside on every level and near bedrooms. See the [smoke and CO alarm guide](../smoke-and-co-alarm-guide/).
- Never plug a generator into a wall outlet. That can send power back into the lines and endanger utility workers. A licensed electrician can install a proper transfer switch.

## When a storm is on the way

When the forecast turns:

- Bring in or tie down loose outdoor items.
- Charge phones and battery banks.
- Turn the fridge and freezer to their coldest settings so food stays cold longer if the power goes out.
- Know where your main water shut-off and electrical panel are. Don't open the panel — just know where it is.
- Stay inside, away from windows, during high wind and lightning.

## After the storm

Once it's safe to go outside:

- **Stay far away from downed power lines** and anything touching them. Report them to the utility.
- **Look at the house from the ground.** Missing shingles, damaged gutters, broken windows, branches on the roof.
- **Check the attic and ceilings** for new water stains.
- **Check the basement** for water.
- **Photograph damage** before cleanup for your insurance claim.

Leave roof repairs, tree removal and electrical problems to a roofer, tree service or licensed electrician. If floodwater reached outlets, the panel or appliances, don't touch them — call an electrician.

## The short version

- Trim dead branches, but leave big limbs and anything near power lines to a pro.
- Keep gutters, downspouts and the sump pump working.
- Know what you'll bring inside when wind is coming.
- Stock up for a few days without power.
- Run a generator outdoors only, and keep CO alarms working inside.
