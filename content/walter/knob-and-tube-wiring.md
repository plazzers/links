Knob and tube wiring is the original electrical system in many houses wired roughly before the 1950s. It gets its name from what you see: single wires run through porcelain tubes where they pass through framing, and are held off the wood by porcelain knobs where they run along it.

It isn't automatically dangerous just because it's old. A lot of it did its job for decades. The concern is that it was designed for a very different house: no ground wire, far less electrical load, and open air around the wires to shed heat. Many of these systems have also been patched, extended or buried in insulation over the years.

If you suspect you have it, the practical steps are: find out where it is (by looking, not touching), find out whether it's still live, and have a licensed electrician evaluate it. If you're buying, also check with your insurer early.

## How to recognize knob and tube wiring

You can often spot it in unfinished spaces without touching anything:

- **In the basement or attic**, look along the joists for white porcelain knobs nailed to the wood, with single wires stretched between them.
- **Where wires pass through framing**, look for short porcelain tubes set into holes in the joists or studs.
- **Wire jackets** that look like cloth or fabric, often dark, sometimes with a rubbery layer underneath.
- **Two separate wires running apart** from each other, instead of one cable with several wires bundled inside.

Other hints around the house: lots of two-prong outlets, few outlets per room, and a fuse box or a small, older panel. None of those prove it on their own, but they're worth noting. For a broader list of what tends to come with a house from a given decade, try [What to Check in a House Built in…](../../house-age/).

One important caution: seeing old knobs doesn't always mean the wiring is still in use. Sometimes it was abandoned when the house was rewired and simply left in place. Only an electrician can tell you what's live.

## Why electricians and insurers ask about it

Here are the main concerns, in plain terms:

- **No ground.** Knob and tube has no grounding conductor, which modern appliances and electronics expect. That's also why swapping in three-prong outlets on this wiring can be misleading. The outlet looks grounded but isn't.
- **Aging insulation.** The old rubber and cloth insulation can get brittle and crack over time, especially where it's been disturbed or exposed to heat.
- **It needs air.** The system was designed to shed heat into open air. When attic or wall insulation is blown in around it, it can't do that the same way. Many electricians and local codes advise against covering it with insulation.
- **Amateur changes.** After decades, many systems have been spliced into or extended by previous owners. Those connections are often the weak spot.
- **Modern loads.** The original circuits weren't planned for today's kitchens, window AC units and electronics. Heavy use can lead to overloaded circuits, and sometimes to someone "fixing" blown fuses with a bigger fuse, which is a real hazard.

Because of all this, some insurance companies ask about knob and tube wiring, and some may limit coverage or require an electrician's evaluation. Policies vary a lot, so ask your own insurer.

## What you can safely check yourself

There's useful work you can do without touching any wiring:

- Note where you see knobs, tubes or cloth-covered wire, and take photos.
- Count two-prong outlets, and note which rooms have very few outlets.
- Look at your panel from the outside. Is it a fuse box or a breaker panel? Is it labeled? Don't open it. See [older electrical panels](../older-electrical-panels/) for what to look for.
- Watch for warning signs: lights that flicker or dim, outlets or switch plates that feel warm, a burning or hot-plastic smell, fuses that blow often, or scorch marks around an outlet.
- Check whether extension cords and power strips are carrying everyday loads because there aren't enough outlets.

If you notice heat, burning smells or scorching, stop using that circuit and call an electrician promptly.

## What to leave to a licensed electrician

Everything beyond looking belongs with a licensed electrician:

- Figuring out how much knob and tube is still live and what it feeds.
- Testing the condition of the wiring and its connections.
- Advising on whether to replace it entirely, replace it in stages, or protect certain circuits in the meantime.
- Any changes at all: new outlets, new circuits, moving fixtures, or panel work.

Don't pull on old wires to "see where they go," and don't add insulation in an attic or wall cavity that has live knob and tube until an electrician has looked at it. In older houses, rewiring is often done in stages, starting with the kitchen, bathrooms and other high-load areas. Your electrician can explain the options for your house.

## Buying a house with knob and tube wiring

This is usually something to plan for, not a reason to panic. A few steps I'd take:

- **Ask the seller** whether any original wiring is still in use and whether any rewiring has been done, with dates and paperwork. Add it to your [questions to ask the seller](../questions-to-ask-the-seller/).
- **Read the inspection report carefully.** A home inspector will often note visible knob and tube, but they usually can't see inside walls. That's why an electrician's evaluation is the next step.
- **Call your insurer before closing** to ask whether they'll cover the house as-is and what they'd need from you.
- **Plan for rewiring** in your first years of owning the house if the electrician recommends it.

This often shows up alongside other older-home items, so it's worth reading a full [older home checklist](../buying-an-older-home-checklist/) before your inspection.

## The short version

- Knob and tube is the original wiring in many homes built roughly before the 1950s: porcelain knobs, tubes and cloth-covered single wires.
- It's not automatically unsafe, but it has no ground, may be brittle, and shouldn't be buried in insulation.
- Look and note where you see it, but don't handle it.
- Have a licensed electrician check what's still live, and talk to your insurer early if you're buying.
