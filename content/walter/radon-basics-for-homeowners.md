Radon testing is the only way to know if your house has a radon problem. Radon is a radioactive gas that comes up naturally from the soil, and you can't see, smell or taste it. It can build up in any home, old or new, with or without a basement, in any part of the country. A simple home test kit tells you where you stand.

The number to know is **4 pCi/L** (picocuries per liter). EPA recommends fixing your home if radon is 4 pCi/L or higher, and suggests considering a fix if it's between 2 and 4. There's no known safe level, so lower is better.

The good news: testing is easy, and high levels can be brought down. Here's how it works.

## What radon is and why it matters

Radon forms when uranium in soil and rock breaks down. It moves up through the ground and into houses through cracks in the foundation, gaps around pipes, sump pits, crawlspaces and other openings. Houses tend to pull air in from the soil, so radon can collect indoors, especially on lower levels.

Why care? EPA says radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the United States, after smoking, and the leading cause among people who have never smoked. The risk comes from breathing it over many years, which is exactly why a quiet, invisible gas deserves a simple test.

Some regions tend to have higher levels than others, and EPA and state radon programs publish maps of that. But a map is no substitute for a test. Your neighbor's result doesn't tell you much about yours either. Two houses on the same street can test very differently.

## Short-term vs. long-term tests

EPA describes two kinds of tests:

- **Short-term tests** stay in place for a few days (EPA's range is 2 to 90 days, but many home kits run 2 to 4 days). They're quick and give you a first read.
- **Long-term tests** stay in place for more than 90 days. Because radon levels go up and down with weather and seasons, a long-term test gives a better picture of your year-round average.

Home test kits are inexpensive and sold at hardware stores, online, and in some areas through your state radon program. Some are charcoal canisters you mail to a lab; others are digital monitors. You can also hire a qualified radon measurement professional to do the test, which is common in real estate deals.

## How to test with a home kit

Follow the kit's instructions exactly. In general:

1. **Pick the right spot.** Test the lowest level of the home that you live in or could live in, such as a finished basement or the first floor. Use a regular room, not a kitchen, bathroom, laundry room or closet.
2. **Place it properly.** Set it at roughly breathing height, away from drafts, exterior walls, heat sources and high humidity.
3. **Keep the house closed** for short-term tests. Keep windows and outside doors closed as much as possible, starting about 12 hours before the test and throughout it. Normal coming and going is fine. Don't run fans that bring in outside air.
4. **Don't disturb the kit** during the test.
5. **Record the start and end times,** seal it, and mail it to the lab quickly if it's that kind of kit.

Winter, when the house is closed up anyway, is a convenient time to test. I'd retest every couple of years, and after any big change, like finishing a basement or major foundation work.

## What your result means

| Result | What EPA suggests |
|---|---|
| Below 2 pCi/L | Low. Retest occasionally, and after big changes to the house. |
| 2 to 4 pCi/L | Consider fixing your home. No level is known to be risk-free. |
| 4 pCi/L or higher | Fix your home. EPA's action level. |

If a short-term test comes back at 4 or higher, EPA recommends a follow-up test before deciding. A second short-term test gives a quick answer, and a long-term test gives a better year-round picture. If the two results average 4 or higher, it's time to act.

## How radon is fixed (and who does it)

Radon reduction is a job for a qualified radon mitigation contractor. Many states license or certify them, and your state radon office can point you to a list.

The most common method in homes with a basement or slab is often called sub-slab depressurization. In plain terms:

- A pipe is set through the floor into the soil or gravel below.
- A fan draws radon from under the house and sends it up and out, above the roof line.
- Cracks and openings in the foundation are sealed to help the system work.

Crawlspace homes may get a sealed plastic barrier over the dirt with a similar fan and pipe.

After a system is installed:

- **Retest** to confirm the level came down.
- **Check the system's gauge or alarm** now and then. Many have a simple U-shaped gauge on the pipe; if the liquid levels look even, the fan may not be running.
- **Don't turn the fan off.**

Sealing cracks alone usually isn't enough to bring radon down. Don't try to build your own system.

## Radon when buying or selling a home

Radon often comes up during a home sale. A few tips:

- Ask the seller whether the house has been tested and what the result was. It's on our list of [questions to ask the seller](../questions-to-ask-the-seller/).
- A standard home inspection may not include radon. Ask, and book a test before closing if needed. See [what a home inspection covers](../what-a-home-inspection-covers/).
- If the house already has a mitigation system, ask for the post-installation test results and when it was last checked.

EPA's Home Buyer's and Seller's Guide to Radon covers this in more detail.

## Bottom line

- Radon is invisible and common, and testing is the only way to know your level.
- Test the lowest lived-in level with the house closed up, following the kit's directions.
- Fix at 4 pCi/L or higher; consider fixing between 2 and 4.
- Leave mitigation to a qualified radon contractor, and retest afterward.
