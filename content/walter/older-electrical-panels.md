The electrical panel is where power enters your house and gets split into circuits. In an older home, that panel may be a fuse box, an undersized breaker panel, or a brand that home inspectors commonly flag for a closer look. None of that is cause for panic, but it's worth knowing what you have.

The rule for this whole article is simple: **you look, a licensed electrician opens.** There's a lot you can learn from the outside of the panel, its label, and how the house behaves. Removing the cover exposes live parts that can injure or kill, even with the main breaker off.

Here's how to tell what kind of panel you have, which warning signs matter, and what to ask an electrician.

## What you can safely check

Start by finding the panel. It's usually in the basement, garage, utility room or a hallway closet. Then, without removing any cover or screws:

- **Is it a fuse box or a breaker panel?** Fuses are round screw-in plugs or cartridges. Breakers are switches that flip.
- **Read the label.** The brand and model are usually printed on the door or on a sticker inside the door. Take a photo.
- **Look for the main size.** The main breaker or main fuse often shows a number for the service size in amps. Note it.
- **Check the circuit directory.** Is each breaker or fuse labeled? A clear label saves real time in an emergency.
- **Check the area.** The panel should be easy to reach, with clear space in front of it, and not blocked by shelves or stored boxes.

Opening the small hinged door to read labels and flip a breaker is normal. Removing the inner metal cover (the "dead front") is not. That's electrician work.

## Fuse boxes in older homes

Fuse boxes are common in houses built roughly before the mid-1960s. A fuse box isn't automatically unsafe. Fuses protect circuits well when they're the right size. The concerns are usually about how the system has been used and changed:

- **Wrong-size fuses.** When a fuse blows often, someone may screw in a larger one. That lets the wire carry more current than it's meant to, which can overheat it.
- **Limited capacity.** Older services were sized for a lot less electricity than a modern household uses.
- **Few circuits.** That often leads to extension cords and power strips doing permanent duty.
- **Insurance questions.** Some insurers ask about fuse boxes.

If fuses blow regularly, that's a message about overloaded circuits. It isn't a reason to use a bigger fuse. Ask an electrician whether the service and the panel should be updated. Older homes with fuse boxes often also have older wiring, so see [knob and tube wiring](../knob-and-tube-wiring/) as well.

## Panel brands inspectors commonly flag

Some breaker panels from roughly the 1950s through the 1980s have a reputation among electricians and home inspectors for breakers that may not trip when they should. The two names you'll hear most often are:

- **Federal Pacific Electric (FPE), "Stab-Lok"** panels.
- **Zinsco** panels (also sold under the Sylvania-Zinsco name).

If you see either name on your panel label, don't assume the worst, and don't ignore it either. **Ask a licensed electrician to evaluate it**, and follow their advice about whether it should be replaced. Inspectors often note these brands in reports for exactly that reason. Some insurers also ask about them.

There are other older or less common panels an electrician may comment on as well, so a photo of your label is the most useful thing to bring to the conversation.

## Warning signs that mean "call an electrician"

These signs matter with any panel, old or new:

- **The panel or a breaker feels warm or hot** to the touch on the outside.
- **Buzzing, humming or crackling** from the panel.
- **Scorch marks, melted plastic or discoloration** around breakers or the cover.
- **A burning or hot-plastic smell** near the panel.
- **Rust or water stains** on or inside the door, or water dripping near the panel.
- **Breakers that trip often**, or one that won't stay reset.
- **Lights that dim or flicker** when a large appliance starts.

If you notice heat, a burning smell, smoke or scorching, don't keep resetting breakers. Call an electrician right away. If there's smoke or fire, leave the house and call emergency services.

## Things an inspector may write up

A home inspection report may mention items you can't see from outside the cover. Here are a few you'll see often, explained in plain terms:

- **Double-tapped breakers:** two wires under one breaker screw that's only designed for one. It can lead to loose connections.
- **Missing knockouts or open slots:** gaps in the cover where fingers or debris could reach live parts.
- **Undersized service** for the size and use of the house.
- **Aluminum branch wiring** at some breakers. See [aluminum wiring](../aluminum-wiring-in-homes/).
- **Missing GFCI or AFCI protection** where modern practice expects it. See [GFCI outlets explained](../gfci-outlets-explained/).

Each of these is a reason to have an electrician evaluate the panel. None are DIY fixes.

## What you should never do

- **Never remove the panel cover** or reach inside the panel.
- **Never put in a larger fuse or breaker** to stop it from tripping.
- **Never tape a breaker on** or hold it in the "on" position.
- **Never ignore a warm, buzzing or scorched panel** because it "still works."
- **Never store flammable items** or pile clutter in front of the panel.

## Buying an older home: what to ask

- What brand is the panel, and how old is it? Ask your inspector to note the brand and model in the report.
- What size is the electrical service, and has it been upgraded? When, and by whom?
- Is there a permit or paperwork for any panel work?
- Has the insurer accepted the panel as-is?

## The short version

- Look at the label, the type (fuses or breakers) and the service size, and take a photo. Never open the cover.
- Federal Pacific Stab-Lok and Zinsco panels are commonly flagged. Ask a licensed electrician to evaluate them.
- Warmth, buzzing, scorch marks, burning smells and frequent tripping all mean "call an electrician."
- Never fix a tripping circuit with a bigger fuse or breaker.
