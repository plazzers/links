A flipped house isn't automatically a bad house. Plenty of flippers do careful work, pull permits and fix the systems that matter. But a fast, cheap flip has a recognizable pattern: the parts you see in photos are brand new, and the parts you don't see are the same tired ones that were there before.

So here's how to spot a bad flip. Look past the paint. Check the age of the big systems, ask about permits, and pay close attention to the places where water and shortcuts hide. Then get a thorough inspection and the right specialists before you're committed.

## What makes a flip "bad"

A bad flip usually has one or more of these traits:

- **Money went to looks, not systems.** New floors, counters and fixtures — but the same old roof, furnace, panel and water heater.
- **Problems were covered instead of fixed.** Fresh paint on a water stain, new flooring over a soft subfloor, drywall over old damage.
- **Work was done without permits,** especially electrical, plumbing and structural changes.
- **Rushed workmanship** that looks fine at a glance and falls apart under a closer look.

You can't see everything at a showing, but you can see enough to know where to dig.

## Check the age of the big systems

This is the fastest test. Find each of these and look for its age:

- **Roof.** Ask for the install date and any paperwork. From the ground, look for curling, missing or mismatched shingles.
- **Furnace and AC.** The data plate usually has a manufacture date, or it's coded in the serial number.
- **Water heater.** Same idea — check the label. Tank heaters commonly last roughly 8 to 12 years.
- **Electrical panel.** Note the brand on the label without opening the cover. Some older brands are worth asking an electrician about.
- **Water supply pipes.** Look where the water line enters the house. Older galvanized steel or gray polybutylene pipe is worth a plumber's opinion.

A gleaming kitchen next to a decades-old furnace and panel tells you where the budget went.

## Look for cover-ups

These are the classic hiding spots:

- **Fresh paint on one ceiling patch** or the lower part of basement walls — often over water stains.
- **New flooring only in the basement,** or carpet laid over concrete in a damp basement.
- **Strong scents** — candles, plug-ins, air freshener in every room — can mask musty or pet odors.
- **Caulk and trim used to fill big gaps** where walls, floors and cabinets don't meet.
- **Furniture or rugs placed in strange spots** that might cover floor damage.

Press gently on the floor around toilets and tubs with your foot. Soft spots there usually mean a long, slow leak. Open sink cabinets and feel the bottom for swelling. See [signs of water in a basement](../signs-of-water-in-basement/) for what to look for downstairs.

## Test the workmanship

Small details tell you how careful the whole job was:

- **Doors and windows** should open, close and latch smoothly. Test several.
- **Tile** should be even, grout lines consistent, and caulk neat. Tap tiles gently — hollow sounds can mean poor installation.
- **Outlets** should be straight and solid. A cheap plug-in tester shows whether three-prong outlets are actually grounded. Outlets near water should have GFCI protection — press test, then reset.
- **Bathroom fans** should actually pull air and vent outside, not into the attic.
- **Cabinets and fixtures** should be firmly attached.
- **Railings** on stairs, decks and porches should be solid when you grab them.

One sloppy detail isn't a dealbreaker. A pattern of them is a strong hint about the work you can't see.

## Ask about permits

This is one of the most important questions on a flip. Ask:

- What work was done, and when?
- Were permits pulled for electrical, plumbing, structural, roofing or HVAC work?
- Were those permits closed out with final inspections?
- Who did the work — licensed trades or the flipper's own crew?

Many local building departments let you look up permit history for an address. If a house has a new electrical panel, moved walls or a new bathroom and there's no permit record, that's worth a serious conversation. Unpermitted work can create problems with insurance, resale, and safety. Include it in your [questions to ask the seller](../questions-to-ask-the-seller/).

## Older flips: lead and asbestos

Many flips are older houses. If the home was built before 1978, US sellers must disclose known lead-based paint hazards and give buyers a chance to test, according to the EPA. Renovation in pre-1978 homes should follow lead-safe work practices. Ask whether the flipper's contractors were lead-safe certified.

Older homes may also have materials that contain asbestos — some floor tiles, pipe wrap, popcorn ceilings and vermiculite attic insulation. Ask whether anything was tested or removed, and by whom. Don't scrape, sand or break suspect materials yourself; testing and removal are jobs for trained professionals.

If you know the year the house was built, the [What to Check in a House Built in…](../../house-age/) tool lists era-specific items to ask about.

## Your inspection plan for a flip

On a flip, your inspection matters even more. Some ideas:

- **Hire your own qualified home inspector,** and tell them it's a flip and what concerned you.
- **Be there for the inspection** and ask questions as you go.
- **Add a sewer scope** on an older house — a [sewer line camera inspection](../sewer-line-camera-inspection/) is usually separate from a general inspection.
- **Bring in specialists** for anything the inspector flags: a licensed electrician for the panel and wiring, a plumber for pipes, a roofer for the roof, a structural engineer for cracks, sloping floors or removed walls.
- **Ask for receipts and warranties** for new roof, HVAC or major systems.

Remember that a home inspection is visual and non-invasive. Your inspector won't open walls, so what's been covered up can stay hidden. That's why the permit history and the specialists matter.

## Bottom line

A good flip fixes the systems; a bad flip decorates over them. Check the age of the roof, furnace, panel and water heater, look for cover-ups, ask about permits, and bring in a qualified inspector and licensed trades before you commit. A beautiful kitchen is nice — a dry, sound house is the point.
