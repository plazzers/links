You feel it before you see it. A cold spot on the couch. A chill across your ankles in the hallway. A bedroom that never warms up no matter what the thermostat says. That's air leaking in from outside, and warm air leaking out somewhere else.

The good news: finding drafts doesn't take special tools. A cool, windy day, your hands, a flashlight and a sheet of paper will find most of them. Fixing the easy ones is usually a weekend job. The trick is knowing where to look, because the biggest leaks are often not the ones you can feel.

Here's how to do it, room by room.

## Where drafts actually come from

Most people blame the windows. Sometimes that's right. But energy.gov's air sealing guidance points out that some of the biggest leaks are hidden: in the attic, the basement and the places where pipes, wires and ducts go through walls and ceilings.

The usual suspects:

- Gaps around doors and windows, especially worn weatherstripping
- Electrical outlets and switch plates on outside walls
- Where pipes and wires go through walls, floors and ceilings
- The attic hatch or pull-down stairs
- Recessed lights in the ceiling below an attic
- The fireplace damper when it doesn't close tight
- The rim joist in the basement, where the house sits on the foundation
- Dryer vents and bathroom fan openings
- Gaps around a window air conditioner left in for winter

Warm air rises and escapes high in the house, through the attic. That pulls cold air in low, through the basement, crawlspace and lower floors. So the draft you feel at your ankles may be caused by a hole in the ceiling two floors up.

## The simple tests

Pick a cool, windy day. Turn off the furnace fan for a few minutes so moving air from the vents doesn't fool you. Then try these:

**The hand test.** Slightly dampen the back of your hand and move it slowly around the edges of doors, windows, outlets and baseboards on outside walls. A leak feels like a cool line of air.

**The paper test.** Close a door or window on a sheet of paper. If it slides out with almost no pull, the seal there isn't touching. Try it in several spots along the edge, because seals wear unevenly.

**The light test.** At night, have someone shine a flashlight around the edges of an outside door while you stand on the other side with the lights off. Any light you see is a gap air can come through. In daytime, look for daylight around closed doors.

**The tissue test.** Hold a thin strip of tissue near a suspected leak and watch for it to flutter. It's a gentle way to check outlets and switch plates. Skip candles and lit incense for this. A flame near curtains is a new problem, not a test.

Make a list as you go. A simple floor sketch with an X for every leak helps you see patterns, like one side of the house that faces the wind.

## Doors and windows

Doors are usually the easiest win.

- **Weatherstripping** around the door frame flattens, cracks and peels over time. If it's squashed flat or missing, replace it.
- **The door sweep** at the bottom should touch the threshold. If you can see light under the door, that's your draft.
- **Exterior door hinges and strike plates** can sag, so the door no longer presses into the seal. Tightening loose hinge screws sometimes fixes a draft on its own.

For windows:

- Lock them. On many windows the lock pulls the sashes together and closes a gap.
- Check the weatherstripping on movable parts and the caulk on the outside trim.
- Look for cracked glazing putty on older wood windows.

energy.gov's general rule is a good one to keep in mind: use caulk for cracks and gaps between things that don't move, and weatherstripping for parts that do, like doors and operable windows.

## Outlets, switches and walls

Outlets and switches on outside walls can leak more than you'd expect. Foam gaskets made for this go behind the cover plate. Turn off the power to that circuit at the breaker before you take the cover off, and if you're not comfortable working around outlets, leave it for an electrician.

Also look where things go through the wall: under the kitchen and bathroom sinks, behind the washer, where the cable or phone line comes in. Small gaps around pipes and wires can be sealed with caulk. Bigger gaps need the right foam or material for the spot.

## The attic and the basement

These are where the big leaks usually are, and where it's worth being careful.

**In the attic,** start with the hatch. It's often just a loose board with no seal and no insulation on top. Weatherstripping around the edge and insulation on the back of the hatch are easy fixes. Then look, with a good flashlight and safe footing on the joists, for gaps around plumbing vents, wiring holes and the tops of interior walls. Our guide on [attic ventilation basics](../attic-ventilation-basics/) explains why the attic should breathe at the roof but stay sealed from the rooms below.

**In the basement,** look at the rim joist, where the wood framing sits on top of the foundation wall. Cold air often comes in along there. Also check where pipes and wires go through the foundation.

**Around chimneys, flues and furnace vents,** don't use ordinary foam or caulk. energy.gov notes these need fire-resistant materials, because they get hot. This is a good spot to let a pro do the work.

## Before you seal everything: air and safety

Sealing drafts is a good thing. But a house with fuel-burning appliances needs enough air for them to work safely. Furnaces, water heaters, fireplaces and gas dryers all need air, and they need to vent their exhaust outside.

If you do a lot of air sealing in a house with fuel-burning appliances, it's worth having a heating professional or an energy auditor check that everything still vents properly. And keep working carbon monoxide alarms on every level, as CPSC recommends. Our [smoke and CO alarm guide](../smoke-and-co-alarm-guide/) covers where they go.

## Which drafts to fix first

You can't fix everything in one weekend, so go in this order:

1. **Doors that let in daylight.** Weatherstripping and a door sweep are quick.
2. **The attic hatch.** Small job, often a big difference.
3. **Outlets and switches on outside walls.** Cheap foam gaskets, but power off first.
4. **Gaps around pipes and wires** under sinks and in the basement.
5. **Bigger attic and rim joist sealing.** Worth doing, but it's more work. Consider a pro.

If drafts are everywhere and the house still feels cold, think about a professional energy assessment. An auditor can run a blower door test, which depressurizes the house so leaks are easy to find, and can look at insulation at the same time.

## When to call a pro

Do it yourself: weatherstripping, door sweeps, window locks, small gaps with caulk, foam gaskets behind outlet covers with the power off.

Call a pro: sealing near chimneys, flues and furnace vents; large attic air sealing; anything involving old insulation you're not sure about, like loose, gray-brown pebbly vermiculite (leave that alone and have it tested); and a check of your fuel-burning appliances after major sealing. If your house is older, our [winterize your house checklist](../winterize-your-house-checklist/) covers the rest of the cold-weather prep.

## The short version

On a windy day, check with your hand, a sheet of paper and a flashlight. Start with the doors, the attic hatch and outlets on outside walls. Caulk for things that don't move, weatherstripping for things that do. Leave chimneys and flues to a pro, and keep CO alarms working when you tighten up the house.
