If a house was built before 1978, there's a real chance it has lead-based paint somewhere, usually buried under newer coats. That year matters because the US banned lead-based paint for homes in 1978. A newer house can still surprise you, but the date is the line most guidance uses.

Here's the calm part: lead paint that's intact and left alone is generally a lower risk. The trouble starts when it turns into dust or chips. Sanding, scraping, heat-stripping, and the everyday rubbing of old windows are what release it, and young children and pregnant women are the most at risk.

So the plan is simple. Know your house's age, know where lead tends to hide, don't disturb anything you haven't tested, and bring in a certified pro before any work that touches old paint.

## Why the 1978 date matters

Lead was added to house paint for durability for a long time. The older the house, the more likely it is that some of the original layers contain it, and houses from well before the 1950s tend to have more of it than houses from the 1970s.

That doesn't mean every older house has a problem. Many have been repainted, re-sided or renovated over the years. But a fresh coat of paint doesn't remove lead underneath, it only covers it. ## Where lead paint tends to show up

You can't spot lead paint by looking. What you can do is notice the places where old paint is most likely to wear, chip or turn to dust:

- **Windows.** Sills, sashes and frames rub every time a window opens and closes. That friction is one of the most common sources of lead dust.
- **Doors and door frames**, especially where doors stick or rub.
- **Porches, railings and stairs**, inside and out.
- **Exterior trim and siding**, where weather peels paint over time.
- **Soil around the foundation**, where old exterior paint may have flaked off over the years.

What to look for yourself, without touching anything: peeling, chipping, cracking or "alligatored" paint, and fine paint dust on sills or the floor beneath windows. Just note it and take a photo. Don't pick at it to see how deep it goes.

## What you should never do

This is the part that matters most. If the house is pre-1978 and the paint hasn't been tested, treat it as if it contains lead.

- **Don't dry-sand or dry-scrape** old paint.
- **Don't use a heat gun or torch** to strip it.
- **Don't power-wash** old exterior paint without knowing what's in it.
- **Don't sweep up paint chips** with a regular broom or ordinary vacuum, which can spread the dust around.

A lot of lead exposure in homes comes from well-meaning DIY projects, like scraping a window before repainting or sanding a door. The job looks small, but the dust travels.

## Testing: how you actually find out

Only a test tells you for sure. You have a few routes:

- **A certified lead inspector or risk assessor.** An inspection tells you whether lead paint is present and where. A risk assessment looks at whether there are actual hazards right now, such as deteriorating paint or dust. For a full picture of a home, this is the route I'd take.
- **EPA-recognized test kits.** These can give you a quick answer on a specific surface, but they have limits. They're a starting point, not a full survey of the house.
- **A lab test of paint chips or dust**, collected the way a lab or pro tells you.

A general home inspection usually doesn't include lead testing. If you're buying, ask what's included in the [home inspection](../what-a-home-inspection-covers/) and book lead testing separately if you want it.

## Buying a pre-1978 home: the disclosure rule

In the US, sellers and landlords of most housing built before 1978 have to follow a federal disclosure rule. In plain terms:

- The seller must tell you about any **known** lead-based paint or lead hazards and share any reports they have.
- You should receive the EPA pamphlet about protecting your family from lead.
- The sales contract includes a lead disclosure form.
- Buyers generally get a window (commonly 10 days) to arrange a lead inspection or risk assessment, which you can also choose to waive.

"Known" is the key word. A seller who never tested may honestly disclose nothing. That isn't the same as "no lead." If young kids will live in the house, I'd use the testing window rather than waive it. It also belongs on your list of [questions to ask the seller](../questions-to-ask-the-seller/), such as whether any testing, abatement or lead-safe renovation has been done, and whether there's paperwork.

## Renovating: hire lead-safe certified contractors

If you own a pre-1978 home and you're planning work that disturbs paint, like replacing windows, opening walls, or redoing trim, EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) rule applies to paid contractors. Firms doing that work in pre-1978 homes are supposed to be **EPA lead-safe certified** and follow lead-safe work practices: containing the area, limiting dust, and cleaning up properly.

When you get quotes, ask each contractor directly: "Is your firm lead-safe certified under the RRP rule?" Ask to see the certificate. A contractor who brushes off the question is telling you something.

Removing lead paint for good, known as abatement, is a separate specialty. It's done by certified abatement professionals, not general painters.

## Everyday steps that help

While you live in an older house, a few habits reduce dust without anyone touching the paint:

- Wipe window sills and wells with a damp cloth or wet paper towel regularly, and throw it away.
- Wet-mop hard floors rather than dry-sweeping.
- Keep an eye on painted surfaces during your [monthly house walk](../monthly-house-walk/) and note any new peeling.
- Wash children's hands and toys often, especially after playing outside near the foundation.
- If you're worried about a child's exposure, talk to your doctor about a blood lead test.

## The short version

- Pre-1978 house? Assume old paint may contain lead until a test says otherwise.
- Look and note peeling or chipping paint, but never sand, scrape or heat-strip it yourself.
- Use a certified lead inspector or risk assessor for real answers, and use the disclosure testing window when you buy.
- For renovations, hire an EPA lead-safe certified (RRP) firm.
