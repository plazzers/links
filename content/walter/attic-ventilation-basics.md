Attic ventilation lets outside air flow through the attic so it doesn't trap heat in summer or moisture in winter. In most homes, air comes in low, through vents in the soffits under the roof's overhang, and goes out high, through a ridge vent, gable vents or roof vents. When that flow works, the roof boards stay drier, the attic stays closer to the outdoor temperature, and the roof tends to last longer.

When it doesn't work, the signs often show up as frost on the roof boards in winter, dark stains or mold on the underside of the roof, ice dams along the roof edge, or an upstairs that bakes in summer. The cause is often simple: insulation stuffed against the soffit vents, or bathroom fans blowing damp air into the attic.

Here's how a ventilated attic is supposed to work, what you can look at from the hatch, and when to bring in a pro.

## How attic ventilation works

Think of it as a chimney effect. Warm air rises and leaves through the exhaust vents up high. That pulls cooler, drier outside air in through the intake vents down low. A healthy attic has both:

- **Intake vents,** usually in the soffits under the eaves, sometimes at the roof edge.
- **Exhaust vents,** usually a ridge vent along the peak, or box vents, turbines or gable vents.

Balance matters. All exhaust and no intake doesn't move much air. And mixing different exhaust types on the same roof can sometimes short-circuit the flow, so one vent pulls air from another instead of from the soffits. Sizing and layout follow building codes and the shingle maker's requirements, and that's a roofer's call, not a guess.

One important note: some homes have attics that are *designed* not to be vented, often with spray foam on the underside of the roof. Don't add vents to one of those without talking to a pro, because it can cause the very moisture problems you're trying to avoid.

## Why it matters

- **Moisture.** In cold weather, warm, damp air from the house leaks up into the attic. If it isn't carried away, it condenses on the cold roof boards. Over time that means mold, rot and wet insulation. EPA's guidance is simple: control moisture and you control mold.
- **Ice dams.** A warm attic melts snow on the roof. The water runs down and refreezes at the cold edge, building a ridge of ice that can push water under the shingles.
- **Heat.** In summer, a poorly vented attic gets very hot, which can make upstairs rooms harder to cool and can shorten the life of shingles. More on that in our guide to [how long a roof lasts](../how-long-does-a-roof-last/).

## Ventilation is only part of the story

The biggest attic moisture problems usually start with air leaking up from the house, not with the vents. Energy.gov recommends sealing those leaks: gaps around light fixtures, plumbing and wiring holes, the attic hatch and chimney chases. Good air sealing plus enough insulation plus working ventilation is the combination you want.

Adding vents to an attic that's full of leaks from below is like opening a window while the kettle boils. It helps, but it doesn't fix the source.

## Signs your attic isn't breathing well

From inside the house:

- Ice dams or long icicles along the roof edge in winter
- Upstairs rooms that are much hotter than the rest of the house in summer
- Peeling paint or damp spots on upper-floor ceilings near outside walls

From the attic hatch, with a flashlight:

- **Frost or water droplets** on the roof boards or nail tips in cold weather
- **Dark staining or mold** on the underside of the roof sheathing
- **Damp, matted or compressed insulation**
- **Rusty nails** poking through the roof boards
- **A musty smell**
- **Soffit vents buried** under insulation, with no daylight visible along the eaves
- **Bathroom or kitchen fan ducts** that end in the attic instead of running outside, or that have come loose

## How to look safely

You can learn a lot from the attic hatch without walking around up there.

- Use a sturdy ladder and a good flashlight.
- If you do go in, step only on the joists or a solid walkway. The ceiling below won't hold you.
- Watch your head; roofing nails come through the underside of the roof.
- Avoid hot summer afternoons; attics can get dangerously hot.
- Wear a dust mask and gloves around insulation.
- **Don't disturb loose gray-brown or gold pebble-like insulation.** That may be vermiculite, which can contain asbestos. Leave it alone and have it tested by a qualified professional.
- **Don't touch wiring,** especially old cloth-covered or knob-and-tube wiring.

From outside, you can look for soffit vents and a ridge vent from the ground. You don't need to get on the roof.

## Common fixes (and who does them)

- **Baffles at the eaves** keep insulation from blocking the soffit vents. An insulation contractor or roofer can add them.
- **Rerouting bath and kitchen fans** to vent outside, often a job for an electrician, roofer or HVAC technician.
- **Air sealing the attic floor,** usually by an insulation contractor or energy auditor.
- **Adding or changing roof vents,** a job for a licensed roofer, often done when the roof is replaced.

## When to call a pro

Call a roofer, insulation contractor or qualified home inspector when:

- You see frost, mold or dark staining on the roof boards.
- Insulation is wet, or you see signs of a leak.
- You get ice dams most winters.
- Fans vent into the attic.
- You suspect vermiculite or other asbestos-containing material.

## Bottom line

- Air should come in low at the soffits and go out high at the ridge or roof vents.
- Frost, mold, rusty nails and ice dams are the classic signs of a poorly ventilated attic.
- Seal air leaks from the house and keep insulation off the soffit vents.
- Look from the hatch, stay on the joists, and leave vents, wiring and suspect insulation to the pros.
