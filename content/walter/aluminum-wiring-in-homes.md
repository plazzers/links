Some homes built roughly from the mid-1960s to the mid-1970s were wired with aluminum instead of copper for the small branch circuits that feed outlets, switches and lights. Copper prices were high at the time, and aluminum was a cheaper substitute.

The wire itself isn't the main issue. The weak spots are the **connections**, the places where aluminum wire meets an outlet, switch, splice or fixture. Over time those connections can loosen, corrode and overheat. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) has published guidance on this for years and describes accepted ways to repair it.

So if your house is from that era, the plan is: find out whether you have it, watch for warning signs, and have a licensed electrician evaluate the connections. It's a known issue with known fixes, not a reason to panic.

## Why aluminum branch wiring causes concerns

Aluminum behaves differently from copper in a few ways that matter at a connection:

- It **expands and contracts** more with heat, so screw terminals can work loose over years of heating and cooling.
- It forms a surface **oxide** that doesn't conduct electricity well, which adds resistance at the connection.
- It's softer and can **"creep"** under pressure, which also loosens connections over time.
- Many outlets and switches from that period **weren't designed for aluminum**, and neither are many devices sold today.

A loose, high-resistance connection gets hot. That heat is what can damage the outlet, the insulation and the materials around it. This is why the focus is on connections rather than the wire inside the walls.

Large aluminum conductors, such as the main service cable or the wire feeding a range or dryer, are common even in newer homes and are a different matter. This article is about the smaller branch circuits.

## How to tell if you have aluminum wiring

Start with the age of the house. If it was built or significantly expanded between roughly 1964 and the mid-1970s, it's worth checking. For more on what tends to come with a house from your era, try [What to Check in a House Built in…](../../house-age/).

Things you can look at without opening anything:

- **Cable jackets** you can see in an unfinished basement, attic, crawlspace or garage. Look for "AL" or "ALUMINUM" printed along the outer jacket every few feet.
- **The color of exposed wire ends**, if you happen to see any already exposed (for example, at an open junction box an inspector has pointed out). Aluminum is a dull silver color, while copper is reddish. Don't remove anything to check.
- **Inspection reports.** A home inspector may note aluminum branch wiring if they see it at the panel.

Don't remove outlet covers or open the panel to look. Leave that to an electrician.

## Warning signs to watch for

These are reasons to call an electrician promptly, whether or not you know you have aluminum wiring:

- Outlet covers or switch plates that feel **warm** to the touch.
- A **burning or hot-plastic smell** near an outlet, switch or fixture.
- **Flickering lights** that aren't explained by a bulb.
- Outlets or lights that **stop working** for no clear reason.
- **Discoloration or scorch marks** on outlets, switches or cover plates.
- **Sparks or crackling** sounds when you plug something in.

If you notice heat, smoke or a burning smell, stop using that circuit, switch off that circuit's breaker if you can do it safely without removing any panel cover, and call an electrician. If there's smoke or fire, get out and call emergency services. Working smoke alarms matter in every home. See the [smoke and CO alarm guide](../smoke-and-co-alarm-guide/).

## What the repairs look like

CPSC's guide on repairing aluminum wiring describes the repair methods it considers acceptable and cautions against some quick fixes. In broad strokes, the options an electrician may discuss are:

- **Rewiring with copper**, the most complete fix and often the most disruptive.
- **Connecting short copper "pigtails"** to the aluminum wires using connectors specifically designed and listed for joining aluminum to copper, installed the way the manufacturer and CPSC guidance describe.
- In some limited cases, **outlets and switches rated for aluminum wire** (marked CO/ALR) may be part of the discussion, though they are not generally considered as thorough as the other approaches.

Which approach fits depends on the house, local code and the electrician's judgment. This is not a DIY job. Repairs involve working inside boxes on every outlet, switch and fixture, and the correct connectors need special tools and techniques.

## What you should never do

- **Don't swap outlets or switches yourself** on aluminum wiring. Ordinary devices aren't rated for it, and a bad connection is exactly the problem you're trying to avoid.
- **Don't use ordinary twist-on wire connectors** to join aluminum to copper.
- **Don't open the panel.** See [older electrical panels](../older-electrical-panels/) for what you can safely check from outside.
- **Don't ignore a warm outlet** because it "has always been like that."

## Buying or owning a home with aluminum wiring

If you're buying a house from this era:

- Ask whether the house has aluminum branch wiring and whether it's been repaired, by whom, when, and with what method. Ask for paperwork. This belongs with your other [questions to ask the seller](../questions-to-ask-the-seller/).
- If the inspection report mentions it, get a licensed electrician's opinion before closing.
- Call your insurer. Some insurers ask about aluminum wiring and may want proof of repairs.

If you already own the house, have an electrician check the connections, especially in rooms with heavy use. In the meantime, keep an eye on outlets and switches during your [monthly house walk](../monthly-house-walk/) and note anything warm or discolored. A few habits also help: avoid running several high-wattage appliances, like space heaters and hair dryers, on the same circuit, and don't rely on extension cords or power strips for everyday loads. Keep a list of which outlets and switches the electrician has already checked or repaired, so you know where you stand.

## The short version

- Some homes built roughly from the mid-1960s to the mid-1970s have aluminum branch wiring.
- The concern is the connections, which can loosen and overheat over time.
- Look for "AL" on visible cable jackets, and watch for warm plates, flickering and burning smells.
- CPSC describes accepted repair methods. Have a licensed electrician do the work, and never swap devices yourself.
