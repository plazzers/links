The furnace warning signs that matter most are a carbon monoxide alarm going off, soot or scorch marks around the furnace, a burning or gas smell, and a flame that looks yellow and lazy instead of crisp and blue. Those are safety signs. Other warnings, like strange noises, rooms that won't warm up, short on-and-off cycles or a furnace that keeps needing a reset, usually mean something is wearing out.

Most of these you can notice just by paying attention on a cold night. None of them are things to fix yourself. A furnace burns fuel inside your house, and the parts that keep that safe are a job for a qualified HVAC technician.

Here's what to watch for, what each sign might mean, and what to do when you see it.

## Carbon monoxide comes first

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a gas you can't see or smell. A gas, oil or propane furnace makes it as part of burning fuel, and the venting is supposed to carry it outside. A cracked part, a blocked flue or a disconnected vent pipe can let it into the house instead.

That's why the most important furnace safety step isn't on the furnace at all. It's a working CO alarm.

- Have CO alarms outside each sleeping area and on every level of the home, following the manufacturer's directions. Our [smoke and CO alarm guide](../smoke-and-co-alarm-guide/) covers placement.
- Test them monthly with the test button.
- Replace them when they reach the end-of-life date printed on the back.

**If a CO alarm sounds:** get everyone, pets included, outside into fresh air right away. Call 911 or the fire department from outside. Don't go back in until responders say it's safe. Don't assume it's a false alarm.

Also know the symptoms. CO poisoning can feel like the flu: headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, confusion. If several people in the house feel sick at once, or feel better when they leave home, treat it as an emergency.

## Safety warning signs: call a technician now

These signs suggest a combustion or venting problem. Turn the system off at the thermostat if it's safe to do so, and call an HVAC technician.

- **Soot or black streaks** on or around the furnace, the vent pipe or nearby walls.
- **A yellow, orange or flickering flame** where you'd normally see a steady blue one (on furnaces where the burner flame is visible). A flame that looks wrong can mean incomplete burning.
- **Rust or heavy corrosion** on the vent pipe, or a vent pipe that's loose, sagging or disconnected.
- **A burning smell that doesn't go away.** A dusty smell for the first few minutes of the season is common. A smell that lasts, or smells like hot plastic or wiring, is not.
- **Water pooling** under a high-efficiency furnace, which can mean a drain or condensate problem.
- **Frequent CO alarm chirps or alarms** near the furnace or bedrooms.

**If you smell gas** (often described like rotten eggs): don't touch switches, the thermostat or anything that could spark. Leave the house and call your gas company or 911 from outside.

## Wear-and-tear warning signs

These are less urgent but still worth a service call before the coldest part of winter.

- **New noises.** Banging, booming at startup, screeching or grinding can point to ignition trouble or a worn motor or belt.
- **Short cycling.** The furnace turns on and off every few minutes. It can be a dirty filter, a thermostat issue, overheating or a sizing problem.
- **Uneven heat.** Some rooms stay cold while others are fine.
- **Running all the time** but never quite reaching the set temperature.
- **Rising heating bills** without colder weather or a rate change to explain it.
- **Repeated resets.** If you keep having to reset the furnace or relight a pilot, something underneath is wrong.
- **Age.** Furnaces commonly last somewhere around 15 to 20 years. An older unit showing several of these signs is one to start planning around.

## What you can check yourself

You can do a few simple things without opening the furnace.

- **Check the filter.** A clogged filter makes the system work harder and can cause overheating and short cycling. Replace it when it looks dirty, and follow the size printed on the old one.
- **Look, don't touch.** Look around the furnace for soot, rust, water and loose vent pipes. Don't remove panels or covers.
- **Clear the area.** Keep boxes, paint, laundry and anything that burns well away from the furnace and water heater. NFPA recommends keeping anything that can burn at least three feet from heating equipment.
- **Check the outside vents.** On high-efficiency furnaces that vent through a side wall, make sure snow, leaves or nests aren't blocking the pipes outside.
- **Walk the house.** Turn the heat up and check that every room warms up.

## What to leave to a pro

- Anything inside the furnace cabinet: burners, igniters, the heat exchanger, gas valves and wiring.
- Diagnosing a cracked heat exchanger. You usually can't see it, and it takes a technician with the right tools.
- Gas line work of any kind.
- Vent pipe repairs, even if it looks like it just slipped apart.

A yearly tune-up by a qualified HVAC technician is the best way to catch these problems before the first cold night. Fall is the easy time to book it, and it fits nicely into a [winterize your house checklist](../winterize-your-house-checklist/).

## Heating safety beyond the furnace

Winter is when a lot of home CO and fire problems show up, and not all of them come from the furnace.

- Never use a gas oven, stove or grill to heat the house.
- Run portable generators only outside, well away from doors, windows and vents, never in a garage.
- Give space heaters plenty of clearance and turn them off when you leave the room or go to bed.
- Have fireplaces and chimneys checked and cleaned as needed.

## The short version

- A CO alarm is your most important furnace safety device. If it sounds, get outside and call for help.
- Soot, a wrong-looking flame, loose vent pipes or a lasting burning smell mean shut it down and call a technician.
- Noises, short cycling and uneven heat usually mean wear; book a service call before deep winter.
- Change the filter and keep the area clear yourself. Leave everything inside the cabinet to a pro.
