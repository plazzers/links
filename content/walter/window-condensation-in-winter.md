On a cold morning you open the curtains and the bottom of the window is fogged over, maybe with a little puddle on the sill. On a really cold morning, it might even be a layer of frost on the inside of the glass. Condensation on windows in winter is common, and a little of it now and then is nothing to worry about.

But it's also the house telling you something about the moisture in the air. When it's heavy, happens every day, or leaves water sitting on wood, it's worth paying attention. Here's why windows sweat, what's normal, what isn't, and what to do about it.

## Why windows sweat in winter

Indoor air always holds some water vapor. Cooking, showers, laundry, houseplants and simply breathing all add more. Warm air can hold more moisture than cold air, so when warm indoor air touches a cold surface, it cools down and drops some of that water. You've seen the same thing on a cold glass of water in summer.

In winter, the coldest surface in most rooms is the window glass, especially near the bottom edge and the frame. So that's where the water shows up first. When the glass is cold enough, the water freezes and you get frost on the inside.

Two things drive how much condensation you see:

- **How humid the indoor air is.** More moisture, more condensation.
- **How cold the glass is.** Single-pane windows, older double-pane windows and metal frames get colder than newer, better-insulated windows, so they sweat sooner. Energy.gov's window guidance explains how storm windows, window coverings and window upgrades change how cold the inside surface gets.

That's why condensation often gets worse on the coldest nights, in bedrooms with the door shut, in bathrooms after showers, and in kitchens while dinner is cooking.

## What's normal and what's worth a closer look

### Usually normal

- **A little fog at the bottom of the glass** on very cold mornings that clears up as the day warms.
- **Steam on the bathroom window** after a shower that clears in a short while with the fan on.
- **Brief condensation in the kitchen** while you're boiling pasta.
- **Condensation on the outside of the glass** on cool mornings. That's outdoor humidity, and it's a sign the window is keeping the heat in.

### Worth attention

- **Condensation or fog between the panes** of a double- or triple-pane window. You can't wipe it off from either side. That usually means the seal around the glass unit has failed. It isn't an emergency, but the window won't insulate as well as it should, and the fog usually doesn't go away on its own.
- **Water that runs down and pools** on the sill or frame most days.
- **Peeling paint, dark stains or soft wood** on sills and frames, or swollen trim.
- **Black or green spots** on the frame, the caulk or the edges of the glass.
- **A musty smell** near windows, in closets on outside walls, or in rooms that are often closed up.
- **Condensation on many windows all over the house**, all winter long. That points to high humidity in the whole house rather than one cold window.

EPA's guide to mold and moisture says the key to mold control is moisture control, and that wet materials should be dried quickly. Water sitting on a wood sill day after day is exactly the kind of moisture that can lead to rot and mold.

## Simple steps you can take yourself

Most of the fix is reducing moisture at the source and getting air moving across the glass.

1. **Run the bathroom fan** during showers and for a while afterward. If your fan barely moves air, hold a single square of toilet paper near the grille. If it doesn't pull the paper up, the fan or its duct may need attention.
2. **Use the kitchen range hood** when you cook, especially when boiling or simmering. A hood that vents outside does the most good. Energy.gov's ventilation guidance explains why spot ventilation in kitchens and baths matters.
3. **Don't block heat registers or vents.** Furniture, long drapes or rugs over the vents under a window keep warm air from washing across the glass.
4. **Open curtains and blinds during the day.** Heavy curtains that stay closed trap cold air against the glass, which makes condensation worse. Close them at night for warmth if you like, then open them in the morning.
5. **Check your humidity.** An inexpensive humidity gauge (a hygrometer) tells you what's going on. EPA's moisture guide recommends keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent. In very cold weather, you may need to stay toward the lower end of that range to keep windows clear.
6. **Turn the humidifier down.** If you run a whole-house or room humidifier, check its setting. Many people turn it up in fall and forget it. If windows are dripping, it's probably set too high for the weather. Follow the maker's instructions for cleaning it, too.
7. **Vent the clothes dryer outdoors** and keep that vent clear. Drying laundry on racks inside adds a lot of moisture, so open a door to a ventilated room or run a fan.
8. **Wipe and dry the sills** when water collects. A towel in the morning keeps water from soaking into wood and paint.
9. **Keep interior doors open** when you can, so heat and air reach rooms on the cold side of the house.
10. **Seal drafts around the window trim.** Energy.gov's air sealing guide covers caulk and weatherstripping. Cold drafts make the frame and glass colder.

Give changes a week or two of cold weather to judge whether they've helped. The [how to find drafts in your house](../how-to-find-drafts-in-your-house/) guide is a good next step if certain windows feel noticeably colder than others.

## When to call a pro

Call in help when the problem goes beyond wiping a window:

- **Fog between the panes.** A window installer can tell you whether the glass unit can be replaced or the whole window needs attention.
- **Soft, rotted or crumbling wood** on sills or frames. A carpenter or window contractor should look at how far the damage goes.
- **Mold that keeps coming back** or covers more than a small area. EPA's guidance covers when to bring in a professional, and the moisture source needs to be found and fixed, not just the spots cleaned.
- **Condensation on most windows even with humidity in a reasonable range.** An HVAC contractor or energy auditor can check your ventilation, your humidifier, and whether your fans and dryer vent actually reach the outside.
- **Fans that don't move air**, or bathroom fans that seem to vent into the attic. That can cause moisture problems up there, too. Our [attic ventilation basics](../attic-ventilation-basics/) guide explains why.
- **Water stains on the wall below a window.** Sometimes that's condensation running off the sill. Sometimes it's a leak from outside. A window or siding contractor can tell the difference.

If anyone in your home has health concerns related to mold or damp air, talk with a doctor and follow EPA's guidance on cleanup.

## If you're buying a house in winter

A winter showing is actually a good time to look at windows, because condensation shows you things a summer visit can't.

- **Look at the glass and sills in every room**, especially bedrooms, bathrooms and the kitchen.
- **Check for fog between the panes.** It's easy to spot once you know what you're looking for: hazy glass that a sleeve can't wipe clear.
- **Press gently on the bottom corners** of wood sills and frames. Soft or spongy wood is worth noting.
- **Look for fresh paint** only on sills and the wall below windows. It may be nothing, or it may be covering water stains.
- **Notice the smell** in closets on outside walls and in the basement.
- **Ask the seller** how old the windows are and whether any glass units have been replaced.

None of these is a reason to walk away by itself. They're things to write down and ask your home inspector to look at. Our guide to [buying a house in winter](../buying-a-house-in-winter/) covers what else to check when there's snow on the ground.

## The short version

Windows sweat when warm, damp indoor air meets cold glass. A little fog on the coldest mornings is normal. Fog between the panes, water pooling on sills, soft wood, stains, mold or a musty smell are worth attention. Run your fans, don't block the vents, open the curtains in the daytime, keep humidity in a sensible range and turn the humidifier down if the windows are dripping. Call a window contractor for failed seals or rotted wood, and an HVAC pro or energy auditor if the whole house stays too damp.
