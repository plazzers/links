Two kinds of water supply pipe come up again and again in older-home inspections: **polybutylene**, a gray plastic pipe used in many homes built roughly from the late 1970s to the mid-1990s, and **galvanized steel**, a gray metal pipe common in homes built roughly before the 1960s.

Both have a history of trouble. Polybutylene has a track record of leaking at fittings and as it ages. Galvanized steel rusts from the inside over decades, which can mean low pressure, discolored water and eventually leaks. Neither one means the house is a bad buy, but both are worth knowing about before they surprise you.

The good news is that identifying them is one of the more DIY-friendly checks in an older house. You can usually spot them in a few minutes. Deciding what to do about them is a job for a licensed plumber.

## Polybutylene pipes: what they are

Polybutylene (often shortened to "PB" or "poly") is a flexible plastic pipe that was widely installed for water supply lines for a couple of decades. It was easy to work with and inexpensive, so it ended up in a lot of houses, mobile homes and condos.

Over time, it built a reputation for failing without much warning, both at the fittings and in the pipe itself. The usual explanation is that the material can break down with age and exposure to treated water. Because of that history, many plumbers recommend replacing it, and some insurers ask whether a house has it.

## How to identify polybutylene

Look where pipes are visible: near the water heater, under sinks, in an unfinished basement or crawlspace, and where the main water line comes into the house.

- **Color:** usually gray, sometimes blue (more often on the outside service line) or black.
- **Feel and shape:** flexible plastic, typically about the size of a finger, sometimes bending in gentle curves.
- **Markings:** often stamped with a code such as "PB2110" along the side.
- **Fittings:** older systems often used plastic or copper/aluminum crimp rings at the joints.

Be careful not to confuse it with **PEX**, a newer plastic pipe that's commonly white, red or blue and is marked "PEX." If you're not sure which you have, take a clear photo of the pipe and any printing on it, and ask a plumber.

## Galvanized steel pipes: what they are

Galvanized pipe is steel coated with zinc to slow rust. It was a standard material for water supply lines for a long time. The problem is that the coating wears away over decades, and the inside of the pipe slowly rusts and narrows with mineral buildup, a bit like a clogged artery.

The signs tend to creep up slowly:

- **Low water pressure**, especially when two fixtures run at once.
- **Brown or rusty water**, often when a tap is first turned on after sitting.
- **Uneven pressure**, where one bathroom is fine and another is weak.
- **Leaks or rust spots** at threaded joints.

If an older home is fed by an old service line, it's also reasonable to ask your water utility what material the line from the street is made of.

## How to identify galvanized steel

- **Color:** dull gray or silver metal, sometimes with rust spots.
- **Threads:** pipes join with threaded fittings, so you'll often see threads at the joints.
- **The magnet test:** a fridge magnet will stick firmly to galvanized steel. It won't stick to copper or to plastic. This is a simple, safe check.
- **Scratch test (gently):** a light scratch on copper shows a penny color. Galvanized stays gray. Don't dig into anything that's corroded.

Old gray **drain** pipes can be cast iron or galvanized too. That's a separate topic from supply lines. If you have slow drains in an older house, a [sewer line camera inspection](../sewer-line-camera-inspection/) is often the next step.

## What you can check yourself

Without any tools beyond a flashlight and a magnet:

- Identify the pipe type at the water heater, under sinks and where the main line enters.
- Run two taps at the same time and see whether pressure drops noticeably.
- Look for drips, damp spots, white crusty buildup or rust at fittings.
- Find your **main water shut-off** and make sure you can reach it. In a leak, it's the most important valve in the house.
- Check under sinks and around the water heater during your [monthly house walk](../monthly-house-walk/).

A slow leak inside a wall or under a floor can do a lot of damage before you notice it. Watching your water meter when nothing is running is a simple way to catch hidden leaks, and it's one of the ideas behind EPA's Fix a Leak Week. For more on small problems that grow, see [signs of water in the basement](../signs-of-water-in-basement/).

## When to call a licensed plumber

Call a licensed plumber when:

- You find polybutylene anywhere in the house.
- Pressure is dropping, or water runs brown at first, in a house with galvanized supply lines.
- You see active leaks, corrosion or wet spots at fittings.
- You're buying a house and the inspection report mentions either type.

A plumber can tell you how much of the system is affected, whether partial or whole-house replacement makes sense, and how urgent it is. Don't try to cut out or re-fit old pipe yourself. Mixing pipe materials without the right fittings can cause new leaks.

## Buying a house with these pipes

- Ask the seller whether the supply pipes have been replaced, when, and by whom.
- Ask your inspector to note the pipe types in the report.
- Call your insurer before closing, since some ask about polybutylene and may limit coverage.
- If replacement is likely, get a plumber's opinion during your inspection period so you know what you're taking on.

## The short version

- Polybutylene: gray (sometimes blue or black) flexible plastic, often stamped "PB2110," common in homes from roughly the late 1970s to the mid-1990s.
- Galvanized: gray threaded steel, magnet sticks, common in homes from roughly before the 1960s.
- You can identify both yourself and watch for leaks, low pressure and brown water.
- Decisions about repair or replacement belong to a licensed plumber.
