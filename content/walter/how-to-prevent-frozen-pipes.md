A frozen pipe doesn't usually announce itself. The faucet works on Tuesday night, the temperature drops hard, and on Wednesday morning you open the tap and get a trickle or nothing at all. If you're lucky, that's the whole story. If you're not, the ice has split the pipe, and the real trouble starts when it thaws.

The good news is that most frozen pipes happen in the same few places, for the same few reasons, and most of the prevention is simple. Here's how to find your risky pipes, what to do before winter, what to do on the coldest nights, and what to do if one freezes anyway.

## Why pipes freeze (and why they burst)

Water expands as it freezes. In a pipe, the ice plug squeezes the water between itself and the closed faucet, and the pressure builds there. That's why a pipe often splits a little way from where it froze, and why a burst often isn't noticed until the ice melts and water starts running.

Pipes freeze when cold air reaches them faster than the house can keep them warm. So the question for every pipe is simple: is it in a cold place, and is cold air moving across it?

## Find the pipes most likely to freeze

Take a flashlight and walk the house on a calm afternoon. You're looking for water lines in these places:

- **Outside walls**, especially under kitchen and bathroom sinks where the cabinet sits against an exterior wall.
- **Crawlspaces**, where vents, gaps and missing insulation let winter air in.
- **Unheated garages**, including pipes that feed a laundry area or a bathroom over the garage.
- **Attics**, if any plumbing runs up there.
- **Basements with cold corners**, like the spot where the main line enters the house or a rim joist with gaps.
- **Outdoor faucets** and the short pipe behind them inside the wall.
- **Additions and bump-outs**, where a bathroom or kitchen was added and the floor or wall may have less insulation.

Make a short list. If a pipe has frozen before, put it at the top. Pipes that froze once tend to freeze again until something changes.

## Before winter: the prevention list

Most of this is one afternoon of work, and much of it is free.

1. **Disconnect garden hoses** and drain the outdoor faucets. If your house has an indoor shutoff valve for each outdoor faucet, close it and then open the outside faucet so trapped water can drain. Frost-free faucets still need the hose removed, or they can't drain.
2. **Insulate exposed pipes you can reach easily.** Foam pipe sleeves from the hardware store slip over pipes in basements, crawlspaces and garages. Energy.gov's insulation guidance covers where insulation helps most in a house.
3. **Seal the drafts near pipes.** Look for gaps where pipes and wires go through walls and rim joists, and around crawlspace vents and doors. Energy.gov's air sealing guide explains simple caulk and foam work. Cold moving air freezes pipes much faster than still air.
4. **Find the main water shutoff** and make sure it turns. Tag it so anyone in the house can find it in the dark. If it's stiff or crusted, don't force it; have a plumber look at it now.
5. **Close the garage door** habitually if water lines run through the garage.
6. **Check any heat cable (heat tape)** already installed on pipes. Look for damage, kinks or a cable that looks old, and read its label and instructions. Damaged heat cable is a job for a pro, not a fix with electrical tape.

If you have a crawlspace with gaps or a pipe that keeps freezing, it's worth having a plumber or insulation contractor look at it before the cold arrives. Fixing the cause beats fighting the same pipe every January.

## On the coldest nights

When the forecast drops well below freezing, a few small habits make a big difference. Ready.gov's winter weather guidance covers most of these:

- **Open the cabinet doors** under sinks on outside walls so warm room air reaches the pipes. Move cleaning products up high if you have kids or pets.
- **Let a faucet drip** on the pipes most at risk, both hot and cold if it's a mixing faucet. Moving water is less likely to freeze, and an open faucet gives pressure somewhere to go.
- **Keep the heat steady day and night.** Turning the thermostat way down overnight can let cold spots get colder than you'd expect.
- **Keep interior doors open** so heat reaches the rooms on the cold side of the house.
- **Don't heat a crawlspace or garage with an open flame** or a fuel-burning heater. If you use a space heater anywhere, follow the [space heater safety rules](../space-heater-safety/): plenty of clearance, plugged straight into the wall, never left running unattended.

## When you're away in winter

An empty house in a cold snap is how small problems become big ones, because nobody notices the first drip.

- **Leave the heat on.** A common guideline is to set it no lower than about 55°F, and higher if you have pipes on outside walls.
- **Ask someone to check the house** if you'll be gone more than a few days, and show them where the main water shutoff is.
- **Consider shutting off the main water** and draining the faucets if you'll be gone a long time. If you have a boiler with hot-water heat, a fire sprinkler system, or anything else that needs water pressure, ask a plumber before you shut the water off.
- **Think about the power going out.** If the power fails, many furnaces stop too. Ready.gov's power outage guidance and our [winter power outage checklist](../winter-power-outage-checklist/) cover what to do.

## What to do if a pipe freezes

You open a faucet and get nothing or only a trickle, while other faucets work. That usually means a frozen spot somewhere on that line.

1. **Keep the faucet open.** As the ice melts, water needs somewhere to go, and running water helps melt the rest.
2. **Look for the frozen spot** along the pipe: frost on the pipe, a section that feels much colder, or a pipe that runs along an outside wall or through a cold space.
3. **Warm it gently.** Ready.gov suggests safe options like a hair dryer, a heating pad, or towels soaked in hot water wrapped around the pipe. Start near the faucet and work back toward the frozen part.
4. **Never use an open flame**, a torch, a propane heater or a charcoal heater to thaw a pipe. That's how a frozen pipe turns into a house fire.
5. **Call a plumber** if you can't find the frozen spot, can't reach it, or it doesn't thaw. Frozen pipes inside walls are a plumber's job.

When water comes back, check the whole length of that pipe for drips and look at the ceiling and walls nearby over the next day.

## If a pipe bursts

- **Shut off the main water valve right away.** This is why you tagged it.
- **If water is near outlets, cords or the electrical panel, stay out of the water** and, if you can reach the panel safely from a dry spot, turn off power to that area. If you can't do it safely, call the power company or an electrician.
- **Call a plumber.** Then take photos of the damage before you clean up, which helps if you need to talk to your insurance company.
- **Dry things out quickly.** Wet drywall, insulation and flooring can grow mold within a day or two, so get air moving and ask a pro about anything that stays wet.

## The short version

Find the pipes in cold places. Insulate what you can reach and seal the drafts near them. Disconnect the hoses. Know where your main shutoff is. On the coldest nights, open the cabinets and let a faucet drip. And if a pipe freezes, warm it gently and never with a flame.

Most of this takes an afternoon. It's one of the cheapest winter jobs there is, and it's the one you'll be gladdest you did.
