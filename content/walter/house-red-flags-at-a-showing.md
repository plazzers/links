A showing is short, the house is staged, and everybody is in a good mood. That's exactly when it's easy to miss the things that matter. You don't need tools or training to catch the biggest house red flags — you need about 30 minutes, a flashlight, and the habit of looking at the boring parts of the house instead of the kitchen counters.

None of what follows means "walk away" on its own. A red flag is a question. Some questions have easy answers. Others need a qualified home inspector, a structural engineer, a plumber or an electrician before you sign anything. The goal at a showing is simple: notice, write it down, and ask.

## Why a showing is different from an inspection

At a showing, you're looking for reasons to dig deeper — not reaching conclusions. A professional home inspection comes later, and it's much more thorough (here's [what a home inspection covers](../what-a-home-inspection-covers/) and what it leaves out). But an inspection usually happens after your offer is accepted. If you spot a serious concern at the showing, you can shape your offer, ask better questions, or save yourself the inspection fee on a house that was never going to work.

So treat this as a first pass. Look, smell, touch where it's safe, and keep a list.

## 1. Signs of water where it shouldn't be

Water is behind most of the expensive problems in a house. Look for:

- **Stains on ceilings**, especially under bathrooms and near outside walls
- **A musty smell** in the basement, closets or under sinks — your nose is a good inspection tool
- **White chalky deposits** on basement walls, which are minerals left behind by water moving through
- **Swollen or soft cabinet floors** under the kitchen and bathroom sinks
- **Fresh paint on just one patch** of ceiling or one section of basement wall

That last one deserves a polite question. Sometimes it's simple touch-up. Sometimes it covers a stain. For a deeper walk-through, see [signs of water in a basement](../signs-of-water-in-basement/).

## 2. Foundation cracks and floors that slope

Some cracking is normal. Thin vertical hairline cracks in a poured concrete wall are common. The ones I'd pay attention to:

- **Horizontal cracks** in a foundation wall, which can mean soil is pushing on it
- **Stair-step cracks** that zig-zag along mortar joints in block or brick
- **Walls that bow or lean inward** — sight down the length of the wall
- **Diagonal cracks** running from the corners of doors and windows inside
- **Floors that slope** enough that a ball rolls quickly across the room

Any of these is a reason to have a structural engineer look before you close.

## 3. A roof near the end of its life

Don't climb onto the roof. Stand back from the house — binoculars help — and look for curled, cupped or missing shingles, bare spots, and a roof line that dips or sags. Look at the ground below the downspouts: a lot of shingle granules there suggests the roof is wearing out. Inside, check the top-floor ceilings and, if you're allowed into the attic, look for dark stains on the underside of the roof boards.

Then ask the simplest question of all: how old is the roof? Asphalt shingle roofs commonly last roughly 20 to 30 years, depending on the material and climate. The answer tells you what's coming.

## 4. Electrical warning signs

Never open the electrical panel cover. Just look at it and around it:

- Scorch marks, a burning smell, or buzzing at the panel
- The panel brand — some older brands have known concerns about breakers that may not trip, so note the name on the label
- Exposed wiring, open junction boxes, or extension cords being used like permanent wiring
- Very old cloth-covered or knob-and-tube wiring visible in the basement or attic
- Outlets that are loose, cracked or warm to the touch

Any of these goes on the list for a licensed electrician. Some insurers also ask about older wiring and panels, so it's worth knowing before closing.

## 5. Plumbing and water heater clues

Turn on a sink and a shower at the same time and see whether the pressure holds up. Fill a sink and let it drain — slow or gurgling drains can point to clogs, venting issues or sewer line trouble. Look under every sink with a flashlight. Find the water heater and check the label for its age; tank heaters commonly last roughly 8 to 12 years. Rust or water around the base is a bad sign.

If the house is older or has big trees out front, the sewer line is worth a separate look. A [sewer scope inspection](../sewer-line-camera-inspection/) is usually not part of a standard home inspection.

## 6. Cosmetic work that hides bigger problems

A freshly renovated house can be great — or it can be a nice coat of paint over old trouble. Be curious when you see:

- New flooring in only the basement
- Air fresheners or scented candles running in every room
- Heavy furniture or rugs placed in odd spots
- Brand-new finishes paired with an old furnace, old panel and old water heater

That last pattern is common in quick flips. The visible surfaces got the budget, and the systems behind the walls didn't.

## 7. Smells, pests and things you can't see

Some of the biggest concerns don't show up in photos. A sulfur smell, a gas smell, or a strong musty odor all deserve answers. If you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas company from outside. Look along the foundation for termite mud tubes — thin dirt tunnels climbing the wall — and in cabinets for mouse droppings.

And remember the invisible one: radon. It's a gas with no smell or color, and it shows up in houses of every age. The EPA recommends testing during a home sale and fixing the home if the level is 4 pCi/L or higher. You can't spot it at a showing, but you can ask whether it's been tested.

## What to do with what you found

Write down what you saw, where, and a photo if the seller allows it. Then:

- **Ask the seller or listing agent** directly about each item. My list of [questions to ask the seller](../questions-to-ask-the-seller/) is a good starting point.
- **Tell your home inspector** what worried you, so they pay extra attention there.
- **Call the right specialist** for anything structural, electrical, sewer-related or involving possible asbestos or lead. Don't poke, scrape or test suspect materials yourself.

If you know the year the house was built, the [What to Check in a House Built in…](../../house-age/) tool lists the era-specific things worth asking about.

## The short version

- Look at water, foundation, roof, electrical and plumbing before the finishes.
- A red flag is a question, not a verdict — write it down and ask.
- Don't climb roofs, open panels or disturb old materials; that's a job for a pro.
- Use what you find to guide your inspection and your offer.
