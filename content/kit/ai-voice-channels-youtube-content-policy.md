AI voices and avatars make it possible to run a channel without recording yourself. That's allowed on YouTube, but a few policies matter more for these channels than for others, especially if you want to join or stay in the YouTube Partner Program. This guide gives a plain-English overview of those policies and links to YouTube's official help pages, which are the only source that counts.

**This is not legal advice, and it isn't an official interpretation.** YouTube updates its policies and wording, and YouTube decides how they apply to a given channel. Read the official pages linked below before you rely on anything here.

## The official pages to read

Start with these YouTube Help pages:

- [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392): the rules for channels in the YouTube Partner Program, including the sections on reused content and inauthentic content.
- [Disclosing use of altered or synthetic content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491): when YouTube asks creators to label realistic AI-generated or altered content, and how.
- [Video Chapters](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9884579): not a policy page, but useful for structuring longer videos (see the [chapters guide](../youtube-chapters-timestamps/)).

Bookmark them and check them again every few months.

## Reused content: adding something of your own

YouTube's monetization policies include a section on **reused content**. In general terms, it covers channels that use content that isn't theirs, such as other creators' videos, clips, or material found online, without adding significant original commentary, modification, or educational or entertainment value.

For a faceless channel, this is the policy that's easiest to run into by accident. Some practical ways to think about it:

- **Your script is your contribution.** Original research, explanation and structure, written by you (even with AI help that you direct and edit), are what make a video yours.
- **Stock footage is a tool, not the video.** Using licensed stock clips to illustrate your own narration is common. A compilation of clips with little narration or editing is a different thing.
- **Commentary needs to be real commentary.** Reacting to or reviewing someone else's video means adding your own analysis throughout, not reading the original aloud.
- **Licenses still matter.** Having permission to use a clip doesn't automatically make the video original under monetization policies. The two are separate questions.

The official page lists examples of what is and isn't allowed. Read them; they're more precise than any summary.

## Inauthentic content: avoiding mass-produced uploads

The monetization policies also cover what YouTube now calls **inauthentic content** (previously described as "repetitious content"). Broadly, it's about content that is mass-produced or repetitive, such as many videos made from the same template with only small changes, or content that looks made at scale with little variation between uploads.

AI tools make it easy to produce many videos quickly, which is exactly why this matters for AI voice channels. Questions worth asking about your own channel:

- **Would a viewer see a real difference between my last five videos,** beyond the topic word in the title?
- **Does each video have its own script,** with its own research and structure, or the same script with names swapped?
- **Am I uploading more than I can actually check?** If you can't watch every video before it goes live, quality and accuracy suffer.

A steady, realistic schedule is safer and usually better for viewers too. The [weekly content calendar guide](../content-calendar-one-person-channel/) shows a pace one person can keep. If you cut Shorts from long videos, make each one a distinct idea; [how many Shorts to cut from one video](../how-many-shorts-from-one-video/) covers that.

## Disclosing altered or synthetic content

Separately from monetization, YouTube asks creators to **disclose realistic altered or synthetic content**: content that a viewer could easily mistake for a real person, place or event. You do this with a setting in YouTube Studio when you upload or edit the video, and YouTube may then show a label to viewers.

According to YouTube's help page, examples that typically need disclosure include making a real person appear to say or do something they didn't, altering footage of real events or places, and generating realistic scenes that didn't happen.

Examples that typically don't need disclosure include clearly unrealistic or animated content, minor edits like color correction or beauty filters, and using AI for productivity tasks such as drafting a script or generating captions.

For AI voice and avatar channels, some practical notes:

- **A synthetic voice reading your own script** about a topic generally isn't the kind of realistic deception the policy targets, but check the official examples.
- **Cloning a real person's voice,** or making a realistic avatar of a real person, is a different matter. Disclosure applies, and you should also think about consent and YouTube's privacy guidelines.
- **A realistic avatar of a person who doesn't exist** sits closer to the line. When in doubt, disclosing is the cautious choice.

## Being honest with viewers

Beyond the formal settings, viewers appreciate knowing how a channel works. A short line in your channel description or video descriptions, such as "Narration on this channel uses an AI voice; scripts are written and fact-checked by me", sets expectations and builds trust. The [YouTube description guide](../youtube-description-template/) shows where a line like that fits.

## A pre-publish checklist for AI channels

- The script is original: your research, your structure, edited by you
- Stock and third-party clips are licensed and illustrate your narration rather than replacing it
- The video is clearly different from your recent uploads, not a template copy
- Facts are checked, especially numbers, names and dates
- Realistic altered or synthetic content is disclosed in YouTube Studio
- No real person's voice or likeness is used without permission
- You've watched the final video yourself before publishing

Policies change, and enforcement examples get clearer over time.
