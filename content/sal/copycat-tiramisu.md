Tiramisu is the dessert people order when they want to feel like they're in Italy. Soft layers of coffee-soaked ladyfingers, a cloud of mascarpone cream, a dusting of bitter cocoa on top. It's elegant. It's comforting. And it's one of the easiest desserts you'll ever make.

No oven. No mixer gymnastics. About 30 minutes of hands-on work, then the fridge does the rest while it sets for about six hours. At home it comes to roughly $1.50 a piece, a fraction of what a slice costs at a restaurant.

The secret isn't a secret ingredient. It's restraint: a quick dip, a light hand, and enough patience to let it chill.

## Why make it at home instead of ordering it

To me, a lot of restaurant desserts taste like they arrived in a box and came out of a freezer. Maybe some did, maybe some didn't, but I explain how to spot the signs in [restaurant dessert tricks](../restaurant-dessert-tricks/). A homemade tiramisu tastes like fresh cream and real coffee, because that's all it is.

What makes a good one work:

- **Ladyfingers that are moist, not mushy.** They should hold their shape when you cut a slice.
- **A cream that's rich but light.** Egg yolks and sugar whisked pale, mascarpone folded in, whipped cream for air.
- **Real coffee flavor.** Strong coffee or espresso, cooled. Weak coffee gives you a sweet, sad dessert.
- **Time.** The layers need hours together so the flavors marry and it slices clean.

## Ingredients at a glance

By name only. The exact amounts and the full method are in Sal's Restaurant Copycat Cookbook and in Sal's Kitchen App.

- **Egg yolks** – the traditional base. Use pasteurized eggs if you prefer, since nothing gets cooked.
- **Sugar** – whisked with the yolks until pale and thick.
- **Mascarpone** – at room temperature so it blends smoothly.
- **Cold heavy cream** – whipped to soft peaks for lightness.
- **Strong coffee or espresso** – brewed and cooled completely.
- **Coffee liqueur or dark rum** – optional, stirred into the coffee.
- **Ladyfingers (savoiardi)** – the crisp Italian kind, not the soft cake-style ones.
- **Unsweetened cocoa powder** – for the top.

## Sal's key technique tips

### One second in the coffee. One.

This is the rule. Dip each ladyfinger, flip, out. That's it. A quick in-and-out. Don't count to two. Savoiardi are thirsty, and they keep soaking up coffee as the dessert sets. Over-dip them and you get a puddle at the bottom of the dish and a tiramisu that slumps when you cut it.

### Room-temperature mascarpone

Cold mascarpone straight from the fridge fights you. It turns lumpy when it meets the yolks. Let it sit out a little first, then fold it in gently until smooth.

### Don't overwhip

Mascarpone can go grainy if you beat it too hard, and cream goes from soft peaks to butter faster than you think. Whip the cream to soft peaks, fold everything together with a spatula, and stop when it's smooth.

### Cold coffee only

Hot coffee melts the cream layer and makes the ladyfingers fall apart. Brew it early and let it cool.

### Cocoa right before serving

Dust with cocoa through a small sieve just before it goes to the table. If you dust too early, the cocoa soaks up moisture and turns into a dark paste.

## Common mistakes

1. **Soaking the ladyfingers.** One second. I'll say it again: one second.
2. **Not chilling long enough.** Cut it after an hour and it's soup. Give it the full time, or make it the night before.
3. **Soft sponge-style ladyfingers.** They dissolve. Look for crisp savoiardi.
4. **Weak coffee.** Make it stronger than you'd drink it.
5. **Lumpy cream.** Cold mascarpone or overbeating. Room temperature and gentle folding fix it.
6. **Too much booze.** A splash adds warmth. Too much and the whole thing tastes like a bar.

## Assembly and variations

### How to assemble it without a mess

You don't need the exact recipe to understand the shape of the job. Think of it as an assembly line, and set it up before you start:

- **Station one:** a shallow dish of cooled coffee, with the liqueur stirred in if you're using it.
- **Station two:** your bowl of mascarpone cream, smooth and ready.
- **Station three:** the serving dish, a square or rectangular one so the ladyfingers fit snug in rows.

Dip, lay it in the dish, dip the next one. Fill the bottom in a single tight layer, spread half the cream over it in an even blanket, then repeat. Smooth the top with the back of a spoon or an offset spatula, cover it, and into the fridge. Don't add the cocoa yet.

When you cut it, use a sharp knife wiped clean between slices and a flat spatula to lift each piece. That's how you get those neat restaurant-looking layers.

### Variations

- **Alcohol-free.** Skip the liqueur. Add a tiny splash of vanilla to the coffee instead.
- **Individual glasses.** Layer broken ladyfingers and cream in small glasses or jars. Great for a dinner party, and they set faster.
- **Chocolate shavings.** Grate a little dark chocolate over the cocoa for extra drama.
- **Berry version.** In summer, some people swap the coffee for a berry syrup and add fresh berries between layers. Not traditional, but delicious.

## Serving ideas

- **After a big Italian dinner.** It's the perfect finish after something rich like [copycat fettuccine alfredo](../copycat-fettuccine-alfredo/) or chicken parm. Small squares are plenty.
- **With an espresso.** Coffee with coffee. Nobody complains.
- **Make-ahead for guests.** Assemble the night before, dust with cocoa when you serve. You're relaxed, they're impressed.

## The short version

- Strong, cold coffee. One-second dip, no more.
- Room-temperature mascarpone, soft-peak cream, gentle folding.
- Chill it for hours, ideally overnight.
- Cocoa on top right before serving. Mangia.
