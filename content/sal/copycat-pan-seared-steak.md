You know the steak. It arrives sizzling, with a deep brown crust, a shiny glaze of butter on top and a little sprig of herbs leaning against it like it owns the place. You cut in and it's juicy and pink from edge to edge. Then you look at the menu again and see what you paid for it.

Here's the thing nobody at the steakhouse wants you to know: there's no magic in that kitchen. Salt early, get the pan hot, baste with butter, let it rest. That's all a steakhouse does. And at home, a good sirloin done this way takes about 20 minutes and costs roughly $7 a steak.

With the holidays coming and the evenings getting dark early, this is my favorite date-night dinner. Let me show you how it works.

## What makes the steakhouse version work

A great steak is two things at once: a dark, crunchy, savory crust on the outside and a tender, juicy middle. Everything the steakhouse does is about getting both.

- **A dry surface.** Water is the enemy of a crust. A wet steak steams before it sears, and you get gray instead of brown.
- **Salt applied ahead of time.** Salt pulls moisture to the surface, then that moisture gets drawn back into the meat. The result is a steak seasoned deeper than the surface and a drier outside that browns beautifully.
- **Serious heat.** Steakhouse grills and broilers run incredibly hot. At home, a heavy pan heated until it's smoking gets you most of the way there.
- **A butter baste at the end.** Butter, garlic and herbs go in during the last minute or two, foam up, and get spooned over the steak again and again. That's the glossy, nutty, garlicky finish.
- **A rest.** The steak sits for a few minutes before anyone cuts it. Patience, my friend.

No secret seasoning blend. No special equipment you don't own. Just the order of operations.

## Sal's key tips

### Salt early. Really early.

If you remember one thing, make it this. Salt the steak well ahead of cooking, ideally the night before, uncovered on a rack in the fridge. Even forty-five minutes on the counter makes a difference. What doesn't work well is salting a few minutes before it hits the pan: that's the moment the salt has pulled water to the surface but the meat hasn't taken it back yet, so you're searing a wet steak.

### Pat it dry anyway

Even after an overnight salt, give the steak a good pat with paper towels right before cooking. Every bit of surface water you remove turns into crust instead of steam.

### The pan has to be heavy and very hot

Cast iron or a thick stainless steel skillet. A thin pan loses its heat the second the cold steak lands, and then it never recovers. Heat it longer than feels reasonable, add a little high-heat oil, and wait until it just starts to smoke. Open a window. Turn on the fan. Tell the smoke alarm it's a special occasion.

### Leave it alone, then flip often

Let the first side sit untouched until it releases easily and has a real crust. After that, many cooks flip every minute or so, which helps the steak cook more evenly. Either way works. Poking and pushing it around in the first minute does not.

### Butter goes in last

Butter burns at high heat, so it never starts in the pan. Turn the heat down a bit near the end, add the butter, smashed garlic and thyme or rosemary, tilt the pan, and spoon that foaming butter over the steak. Thirty seconds of basting and the whole kitchen smells like a steakhouse.

## The ingredients (names only)

By name only. Exact amounts and the full step-by-step are in Sal's Restaurant Copycat Cookbook and in Sal's Kitchen App.

- **Steaks** – sirloin, ribeye or strip, cut about an inch to an inch and a quarter thick. Thinner steaks overcook before they brown.
- **Kosher salt and black pepper** – kosher salt is easier to sprinkle evenly.
- **High-heat oil** – a neutral oil with a high smoke point for the sear.
- **Butter** – for the baste at the end.
- **Garlic** – smashed, skin and all is fine. It flavors the butter without burning as fast as minced garlic.
- **Fresh thyme or rosemary** – a few sprigs, for that steakhouse aroma.

That's it. Six things. The steakhouse doesn't have a longer list. It has a bigger bill.

## The method, the steakhouse way

I'm not giving the measurements here, but here's the rhythm:

1. **Salt ahead.** Overnight in the fridge if you can, at least forty-five minutes if you can't.
2. **Dry and pepper.** Pat the steaks dry and add black pepper just before cooking.
3. **Heat the pan.** Heavy skillet, high heat, a thin film of oil, wait for the first wisp of smoke.
4. **Sear.** Lay the steaks in away from you and don't touch them until a crust forms. Flip and sear the other side. Hold thick steaks on their edges briefly to render the fat strip.
5. **Baste.** Lower the heat a little, add the butter, garlic and herbs, tilt the pan and spoon the butter over the steaks.
6. **Check the temperature.** Use an instant-read thermometer in the thickest part.
7. **Rest.** Move the steaks to a board or rack for a few minutes, then slice against the grain and pour the pan butter over the top.

## Use a thermometer, not a guess

Steakhouse cooks press the steak with a finger and just know. They've also cooked a few thousand of them this month. At home, an instant-read meat thermometer is the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy for an expensive piece of meat.

For food safety, the USDA's guideline for beef steaks is a minimum internal temperature of 145°F, followed by a three-minute rest. That's the number to go by, especially when you're cooking for guests, kids or anyone who prefers to play it safe. Plenty of people order their steak cooked less than that at restaurants as a matter of personal preference, but the official safe minimum is 145°F with the rest.

Remember the temperature keeps climbing a little while the steak rests, so pull it from the pan when it's close and let the rest finish the job. Then check again before you slice.

## Common mistakes

- **Cooking a wet steak.** Gray, steamed, sad. Dry it.
- **Salting right before cooking.** Either salt well ahead or salt right as it goes in the pan. The in-between is the worst of both.
- **A pan that isn't hot enough.** If it doesn't sizzle loudly the second it lands, take it out and wait.
- **Crowding the pan.** Two steaks in a big skillet, fine. Four steaks in a small one, and they steam. Cook in batches.
- **Butter at the start.** It burns and turns bitter long before the steak is done.
- **Minced garlic in the baste.** Tiny bits burn fast. Smashed cloves give you the flavor without the bitterness.
- **Cutting right away.** Give it the rest. Slice too soon and the juices end up on the board instead of in your mouth.
- **Slicing with the grain.** Look at the lines in the meat and cut across them. Same steak, much more tender bite.
- **Guessing doneness.** Use the thermometer. Every time.

## How to serve it

A steakhouse plate is a steak plus two sides and a sauce, and every one of those is easy at home.

- **Mashed potatoes.** The obvious partner. Read the [restaurant mashed potatoes secrets](../restaurant-mashed-potatoes-secrets/) for why theirs taste so rich.
- **Extra garlic butter.** If the pan butter isn't enough, my [garlic butter sauce](../garlic-butter-sauce/) is free and takes minutes. Spoon it over the sliced steak.
- **Something green.** Roasted green beans, a simple salad or sautéed spinach to cut the richness.
- **For a holiday or date night**, slice the steak and fan it out on a warm platter with the herbs on top. It looks like it came from a steakhouse kitchen, because it's made exactly the same way.

Leftovers? Slice them cold for a steak sandwich or toss them into fried rice the next day. Reheat gently, if at all, so they don't turn tough.
