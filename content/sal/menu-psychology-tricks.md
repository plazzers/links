You think you're choosing dinner. Mostly, you're reading an ad. A restaurant menu is one of the most carefully designed pieces of paper you'll hold all week, and it has one job: move your eye, and your money, toward the dishes the house most wants to sell.

Those are usually not the dishes that cost the kitchen the most to make. They're the dishes with the best margin, meaning the biggest gap between what the plate costs the restaurant and what it costs you.

Nothing here is illegal, and it's not evil. It's business. But once you see the tricks, you can't unsee them, and you'll start ordering what *you* want instead of what the layout wants. Let's walk through it.

## The menu is a sales tool, not a list

A home cook writes a shopping list. A restaurant writes a sales pitch. Every choice on that menu, from the order of the sections to the font size to how much white space sits around a dish, is there for a reason.

Menu designers think in terms of "stars" and "workhorses." A star is popular and profitable, so it gets the best seat on the page. A dish that's expensive to make and barely earns anything gets tucked away where fewer eyes land. Sometimes that hidden dish is the one the cook is proudest of. Funny how that works.

So my first rule is simple. **Read the whole menu.** Every page, every corner. The good stuff is often hiding.

## Where your eye lands first

Open a one-page or two-page menu and notice where you look first. Most people go to the middle, then drift up to the top right, then the top left. People in the menu business often call this the golden triangle, or the sweet spot.

Whatever sits in those spots is not there by accident. On a lot of menus, it's the dish the house most wants you to order. Not the dish the chef thinks is best. The dish with the best numbers.

A few more placement habits to watch for:

- **First and last in a section.** People remember the top and the bottom of a list better than the middle. High-margin items like to sit there.
- **Big type, lots of space.** A dish with breathing room around it looks important. A dish crammed in a long column looks like filler.
- **Section order.** Appetizers and drinks often come first, while you're hungry and haven't decided anything yet.

None of this means the spotlight dish is bad. It might be lovely. Just ask yourself: do I want this, or did my eye simply land on it first?

## The box, the border and the little chili pepper

Now look for the box. A thin border around one dish. A shaded panel. A little icon, like a chef's hat, a chili pepper, or a star that says "house favorite."

Your eye goes straight there. It can't help it. That's the whole point.

And what's usually inside the box? You guessed it. Very often it's a dish built from inexpensive ingredients with a comfortable price on top. A pasta with a cream sauce. A chicken plate. Something the kitchen can make fast and cheap, all night long.

Here's my trick: when a dish is wearing a box, **read around the box**. The dishes next to it, the ones without the decoration, are where the cook's real favorites tend to live. The braise. The fresh pasta. The fish that came in this morning. I wrote more about how I pick in [what to order at a restaurant](../what-to-order-at-a-restaurant/).

## Photos: who picks the dish that gets one

You'll notice that fancy places almost never put photos on the menu. A photo says "family restaurant." It says "casual." So the upscale spots skip them.

But on a casual menu, a single good photo can make a dish sell like crazy. People see a glossy picture of cheese pulling off a fork and they're done. Decision made.

So who decides which dish gets its picture taken? It's rarely about which dish is the best food. It's about which dish the business wants to sell more of. Often that's the one with the friendliest margin.

Enjoy the pictures. They're fun. Just remember a photo is an ad, not a review.

## Fancy words: "hand-crafted", "slow-roasted", "artisan"

Read the descriptions on almost any menu and you'll find the same words over and over:

- Hand-crafted
- Slow-roasted
- Farm-fresh
- Artisan
- House-made
- Nonna's, Grandma's, Old-fashioned

Some of these mean something real at some places. Plenty of the time, they're just nice-sounding words. "Artisan bread" can arrive par-baked and frozen. "Slow-roasted" tells you something was cooked slowly. Where? When? It doesn't say. If you want to know how to spot that, see [how to tell if restaurant food is frozen](../how-to-tell-if-restaurant-food-is-frozen/).

The reason these words show up is that a prettier description makes a dish sound better, and a dish that sounds better can carry a higher price. Same food, nicer words.

My advice: **read the menu like a contract.** Skip the adjectives. Find the noun. "Chicken." "Pasta." "Potatoes." That's what you're actually buying. Then decide if the price makes sense for that noun.

## How to read a menu like a cook

Here's the little routine I'd use every time you sit down:

1. **Look at the whole thing before you settle.** Don't let the first box or photo decide for you.
2. **Notice the decorations.** Box, icon, photo, big type. Ask yourself why that dish is dressed up.
3. **Strip out the adjectives.** What's the actual food? Would you pay this for it at the grocery store plus a fair markup for cooking?
4. **Ask the server one honest question.** "What's the kitchen proudest of tonight?" A server who lights up and tells you a story about a dish is pointing you to the real thing.
5. **Then check the price on its own.** Menus play price games too. I break those down in [menu pricing tricks](../menu-price-tricks/).

This takes about a minute. And it's a lot more fun than you'd think. You start to see the menu the way the people who designed it see it.

## The short version

A menu is designed to guide your eye toward the dishes with the best margin, using placement, boxes, photos and pretty words. Read the whole thing, look around the box instead of inside it, and ignore the adjectives until you find the noun. Then order what you actually want. Mangia.
