Let me say this first, and loudly: frozen food is not a crime. My own freezer is full. Freezing is one of the best tools a cook has.

My problem is something else. At a lot of sit-down restaurants, some dishes arrive frozen, par-baked or sealed in a bag, get reheated, and land on your table at a fresh-cooked price. You're paying for cooking. Sometimes you're getting warming.

So this isn't about scaring anybody. It's about value. Here's how to tell what's likely made in the building, what probably isn't, and how to spend your money where the kitchen actually cooks.

## Why so much restaurant food starts frozen

Busy casual restaurants have a hard job. They need every plate to look and taste the same, at every location, on every shift, with whoever's working that night. Big food distributors solve that problem. They sell restaurants ready-made items by the case: breaded, cooked, portioned, frozen.

It's consistent. It's fast. It cuts labor. For the business, it makes perfect sense.

For you, the question is just: am I paying a price that matches what's happening in that kitchen?

## The usual suspects

These are dishes that, across the casual dining world, very often come in ready-made. Not everywhere. But often enough that I'd ask.

### Mozzarella sticks

Breaded cheese sticks very often come frozen from the distributor. Into the fryer for a few minutes, out with a little cup of marinara. Breading your own in a busy kitchen is slow and messy, so hardly anybody does. They're fun. Hot cheese, crunchy outside. But a box of very similar sticks sits in your grocery freezer for about the price of one restaurant order.

### The molten lava cake

You'll find it on almost every casual menu. Distributors sell them frozen, individually wrapped, by the case. A quick reheat, a scoop of ice cream, a drizzle on the plate. And the funny thing is, a real lava cake is one of the easiest "fancy" desserts there is. More on desserts in [restaurant dessert tricks](../restaurant-dessert-tricks/).

### The "slow-braised" short rib

"Slow-braised." "Fall-off-the-bone." "Six hours." Beautiful words. And the short rib may well have been braised for hours... just not in that kitchen. A lot of these are cooked at a food plant, vacuum-sealed, chilled or frozen, shipped, and reheated in hot water before plating, sometimes with sauce from another bag.

### Par-baked bread

"Fresh-baked" bread and rolls often start as dough that was partly baked somewhere else, frozen, then finished in the restaurant oven. The smell is real. The oven is real. "Baked here" and "finished here" are just two different sentences.

### Other common ones

- Onion rings, poppers and most fried appetizers
- Soups that arrive in bags and get heated by the batch
- Cheesecakes and layer cakes from an outside bakery
- Mac and cheese that's scooped from a heated pan rather than baked to order

## Clues to watch for

You can't walk into the kitchen. But you can notice things.

- **The menu is huge.** A menu with eighty items across six cuisines can't all be made from scratch by one kitchen crew. Something is coming out of a freezer.
- **The food arrives impossibly fast.** A braise or a lasagna on the table five minutes after you ordered wasn't made for you.
- **Every piece looks identical.** Perfectly uniform breading, identical shapes, the same size every time. Hands don't work that precisely. Machines do.
- **Fancy words, no details.** "Hand-crafted" and "slow-roasted" with nothing about where or how. Menu writers use those words because they sell.
- **The server hesitates.** Ask, "What's made here in the kitchen?" A proud kitchen's staff answers right away. A long pause tells you something.
- **The edges and the middle disagree.** Hot outside, lukewarm in the center is a classic sign of reheating rushed a little too much.

None of these prove anything alone. Put two or three together and you can make a pretty good guess.

## Frozen isn't the enemy: price is

I want to be fair here, because frozen gets a bad name it doesn't always deserve. A fish frozen hard on the boat can be better than a "fresh" one that sat on ice for days. Peas are often frozen within hours of picking. Plenty of great kitchens freeze their own stocks, sauces and doughs, made by their own hands.

And think about how you cook at home. You make a double batch of meatballs and freeze half. You keep biscuit dough in the freezer and bake it on a Sunday morning. That's frozen done right. You still did the cooking. You just did it earlier.

So the line I draw isn't fresh versus frozen. It's **made here versus made somewhere else**, and **cooking price versus warming price**. A reheated dessert at a modest price is honest enough. The same dessert, plated with a fancy drizzle and priced like a pastry cook spent the morning on it? That's where I start to grumble.

## The one question to ask

If you remember nothing else, remember this: **"What's made here, in the building, today?"**

The good servers know. And at a kitchen that cooks, they're proud to tell you. "The soup, the bread, the pasta, and the chef's doing a pork shoulder tonight." Now you know exactly where to point your money.

## What to order instead

My rule is to order what's hard to fake from a bag:

- **A steak or chop grilled to order.** You can see and taste the cooking.
- **Eggs cooked to order.** An omelet can't be held for hours.
- **A salad tossed right then**, if it's a good one.
- **Fish from the grill**, after you ask when it came in. (Why I ask that is in [what chefs never order](../what-chefs-never-order/).)
- **Whatever the server says the kitchen made today.**

And for the classic reheated stuff, make it at home. Breadsticks, biscuits and a proper baked dish taste fresher from your own oven. Try my [garlic butter breadsticks](../copycat-garlic-butter-breadsticks/) or [cheddar garlic biscuits](../copycat-cheddar-garlic-biscuits/) and you'll see.

## Bottom line

Lots of casual restaurant food starts frozen or par-cooked, and that's fine, as long as the price matches. Watch for giant menus, very fast plates and pretty words without details, ask what's made in the building today, and spend your money on dishes the kitchen actually cooks for you.
