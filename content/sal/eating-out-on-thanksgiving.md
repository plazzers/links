Some years you just don't want to do it. No twenty-pound bird, no five pans fighting for one oven, no sink full of dishes at ten o'clock at night. You want to sit down, be served, and go home full. I understand completely.

Eating out on Thanksgiving can be a wonderful day. It can also be an expensive surprise, because holiday dining works a little differently from a normal night out. The menu is different, the pricing is different, and the fine print matters more.

So before you book that table, here's what to know and what to ask.

## Holiday menus are their own animal

On a normal night you order from the regular menu, one dish at a time. On Thanksgiving, many restaurants switch to a set holiday menu. You'll see words like "prix fixe," "three-course holiday dinner" or "family-style feast."

Why do restaurants do this? It's not a trick, it's survival. Thanksgiving is one of the busiest, most packed days of the year for the places that open. A short, fixed menu lets the kitchen prep huge amounts ahead of time, cook fast and get every table served. That's good for you too, because a focused kitchen usually cooks better.

But it changes how you should read the price:

- **A per-person price looks simple.** It isn't always. The number on the website might not include drinks, tax, gratuity or certain courses.
- **The regular menu may be gone.** If someone in your group doesn't eat turkey, ask whether there's an alternative, and whether it costs extra.
- **Upgrades show up.** "Add a lobster tail." "Upgrade to prime rib." These are the holiday version of the add-ons I talk about in [restaurant upselling tricks](../restaurant-upselling-tricks/). Decide before you sit down.

## Book early, and read the booking terms

Thanksgiving tables go fast at popular places, sometimes weeks ahead. If you have a place in mind, call or book online early in November, or even now.

When you book, slow down and read the terms. On a busy holiday, some restaurants:

- **Ask for a credit card to hold the reservation**, with a fee if you cancel late or don't show up.
- **Set a time limit on the table**, like ninety minutes or two hours, because there's another seating right behind you.
- **Require a deposit for large groups.**
- **Offer only certain seating times.** An early afternoon slot can feel very different from a late evening one, and sometimes the price is different too.

None of this is unfair. A holiday no-show costs a restaurant real money. You just want to know it before you click "confirm," not after.

## What to ask before you book

Here's my checklist. Ask it on the phone, or check the menu page carefully. Polite, quick, and nobody will think twice.

### "What's included in the price?"

This is the big one. A holiday prix fixe might include soup or salad, the main course, a choice of sides and a slice of pie. Or it might be the main plate alone, with sides and dessert charged separately. Ask exactly which courses are included and whether the sides are family-style for the table or individual.

### "Are drinks included?"

Usually they're not. Coffee might be, might not. A glass of wine or a holiday cocktail can add a lot per person, and drinks are already where restaurants make some of their best margins, as I explain in [the restaurant drink markup](../restaurant-drink-markup/). Know the plan before the server arrives with the cocktail list.

### "Is gratuity added for our group?"

Many restaurants add an automatic gratuity for larger parties, and some do it for every table on a big holiday. That's fine, as long as you see it. The problem is when people don't notice and tip again on top. Ask when you book, then check the bill before you sign.

### "Is there a holiday surcharge or service fee?"

Some restaurants add a holiday surcharge or a service fee to cover extra staff pay on the day. It's usually written somewhere on the menu or booking page, sometimes in small print at the bottom. Ask so it isn't a surprise.

### "What's the price for kids?"

Some restaurants have a kids' holiday menu or a lower kids' price for the prix fixe. Some charge full price above a certain age. Some let little ones eat free. If you're bringing children, this question alone can change the total a lot.

### "Can we take leftovers or order extra to go?"

Thanksgiving without leftovers is half a holiday. Some restaurants pack up what's left on your plate as usual. Some offer extra turkey, stuffing or a whole pie to take home for an added price. A few are strict about it on busy days. Ask, so you know whether tomorrow's turkey sandwich is happening.

## On the day: how to keep the bill sane

You booked, you asked the questions. Now enjoy it, with a few small habits:

- **Eat lightly earlier in the day**, but not nothing. Show up starving and you'll say yes to every add-on.
- **Order water first** and decide on drinks as a group. One bottle of wine for the table often costs less than everyone ordering separately.
- **Say upgrade prices out loud.** "How much is the prime rib upgrade?" Same rule as with specials and market price, which I cover in [restaurant specials and market price](../restaurant-specials-and-market-price/).
- **Check the bill.** Look for the automatic gratuity, the surcharge and any upgrades. Mistakes happen on crazy-busy nights.
- **Tip fairly.** The people serving you on Thanksgiving are working on a holiday. If gratuity is already included, you're covered. If it isn't, please don't cut corners here.

## Restaurant or home: doing the math

Here's the honest comparison. A restaurant Thanksgiving for a family can add up fast once you multiply a per-person price by everyone at the table, then add drinks, tax, tip and any holiday fees.

A home Thanksgiving costs groceries, time and work. The turkey itself is often surprisingly affordable around the holiday, since many stores put it on sale. The sides are mostly potatoes, bread, butter, vegetables and cranberries. Per person, cooking at home is almost always cheaper, sometimes by a lot. What you're really paying for at the restaurant is no cooking, no cleanup and no stress.

That's a fair thing to buy. Just buy it on purpose. Plug your own numbers into the [Restaurant or Home? calculator](../../restaurant-or-home/) and see the real difference for your group. For the bigger picture, read my guide on [eating out vs. cooking at home](../eating-out-vs-cooking-at-home/).

### The middle road

There's an option a lot of people forget. Cook part of it, buy part of it.

- Make the turkey and the mashed potatoes at home, and buy the pies.
- Or pick up a holiday to-go meal that some restaurants and grocery stores offer, and make one or two of your own sides to make it feel like yours.
- Or eat out on Thanksgiving Day, and cook a smaller feast on Friday with the leftovers and a few dishes you love.

If you go the home route, the [restaurant mashed potatoes secrets](../restaurant-mashed-potatoes-secrets/) will make your potatoes taste like the ones you'd pay for.

## The short version

Thanksgiving at a restaurant can be a gift to yourself. Book early and read the terms. Ask what's included, whether drinks are extra, whether gratuity or a holiday surcharge is added, what kids pay and whether you can take leftovers home. Decide on upgrades before you sit down, check the bill before you sign, and tip the people working on a holiday properly. Then run the numbers against cooking at home, and pick the Thanksgiving that makes your table happiest. Mangia.
