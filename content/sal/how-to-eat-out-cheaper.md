Eating out should feel like a treat, not a punishment. So I'm not going to tell you to order the cheapest thing and drink tap water in silence. That's no fun.

What I will tell you is where the money really goes on a restaurant bill, and how to cut the parts that don't add much joy. Most of the savings come from the edges of the meal: drinks, starters, little add-ons, specials you didn't price, and dessert you didn't really want. Keep the main event. Trim the rest.

Here's my plan, start to finish.

## Before you go: pick the place and the time

The cheapest savings happen before you leave the house.

- **Pick a place that cooks.** You'll get more for your money at a kitchen that makes things from scratch than at one that reheats. Ask what's made in the building, and look for a short, focused menu.
- **Go at lunch.** Many restaurants serve similar dishes at lunch for less, sometimes in slightly smaller portions that are honestly plenty.
- **Look for weeknight deals and happy hour.** Quiet nights are when restaurants try hardest to fill tables.
- **Look at the menu online first.** Decide roughly what you'll order while you're calm and not hungry.
- **Set a number.** Know your budget, tip included, before you sit down.

## Drinks: the easiest place to save

Drinks are often the highest-margin part of the meal. That's exactly why they're the easiest place to save.

- **Start with water.** Tap water is free and goes with everything.
- **Choose one drink you'll really enjoy** instead of a few you barely notice.
- **Ask for the best value on the wine list** rather than guessing.
- **Skip the after-dinner coffee** if you're heading home anyway.

For the details on soda, coffee, wine and cocktails, see [restaurant drink markup](../restaurant-drink-markup/).

## Starters, sides and the little add-ons

When the server asks, "Can I start you off with the dip or the wings?", you can simply say, "We'll go straight to the mains, thanks." Appetizers are often some of the most profitable items on the menu, and many arrive frozen from a distributor.

Then come the little yeses. "Add chicken?" "Make it a large?" "Upgrade your side?" Each one is small. Together they can add up to a whole extra main course.

**My trick:** say the price out loud before you answer. "Six dollars for avocado?" It's amazing how often that settles it.

If you do want a starter, share one for the table. One dish passed around feels generous and costs a fraction of everyone ordering their own.

## Mains: order what you can't make at home

This is the part of the meal worth spending on. But spend smart.

- **Order something hard to make at home.** A braise, fresh pasta, a whole fish, a properly grilled steak. That's what a kitchen's skill is for.
- **Skip the dishes you can make easily for very little.** A plain salad, a bowl of pasta with red sauce, a basic chicken breast. They often carry big margins.
- **Look at the expensive part of the plate.** Compare how much protein you get, not how big the pile of fries is.
- **Consider sharing a big main** if portions are generous, and add a side.
- **Take leftovers home.** That's tomorrow's lunch, already paid for.

### Specials and "market price": always ask

The specials read out loud often have no printed price. And "market price" means whatever the restaurant decides tonight. Neither is a problem, as long as you ask.

Two questions, every time:

1. "What came in fresh today?"
2. "How much is the special?"

Nobody worth impressing will think you're cheap. They'll think you know what you're doing.

## Dessert: share it or skip it

By dessert you're full, relaxed, and the music is slow. That's exactly when it's easiest to say yes.

- **Decide before the menu arrives.**
- **Ask, "Are the desserts made here?"** If yes, share one. Somebody made it by hand.
- **If they're not**, a nice treat at home may be just as satisfying.

### Loyalty clubs, coupons and birthday deals

Loyalty programs, birthday freebies and "buy nine, get one free" cards can be genuinely nice. Use them! A free dessert is a free dessert.

But be honest with yourself about what they're for. A birthday freebie works because people rarely celebrate alone. They bring the whole family. A points card keeps you coming back to the same place instead of trying the new spot down the street.

Take the deal when it fits what you were already going to do. Don't plan your whole night around it.

## The tip: don't save money here

This is the one place I don't want you to cut. In places where tipping is customary, servers often depend on tips for a big part of their income, and they didn't set the prices.

**Tip on the bill you agreed to, at the normal rate where you live.** All the savings above come from ordering smarter. None of them should come out of your server's pocket.

## When cooking at home is the better deal

Some nights, the smartest move is to stay in and cook the restaurant dish yourself. Breadsticks, alfredo, a smash burger or a pan of fajitas can cost a small fraction of the menu price at home.

## The short version

Keep the main dish, trim the edges. Drink water first, skip or share the starter, say add-on prices out loud, ask about specials and market price, and decide on dessert before the menu arrives. Tip fairly on what you ordered. And on some nights, cook it at home and save the restaurant for the dishes you really can't make yourself.
