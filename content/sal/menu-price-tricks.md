The most interesting thing on a restaurant menu isn't the food. It's the numbers. How they're written, where they sit, and what sits next to them can all change how much you spend, often without you noticing a thing.

Menu pricing tricks aren't secret, and they aren't a scam. They're standard practice, and menu consultants talk about them openly. But they work best on people who don't know about them. So let's fix that.

Here are the ones I see most, and the simple counter-move for each.

## The missing dollar sign

Look at the prices. Something's gone. No "$". Just "24." Or "twenty-four," spelled out in pretty lowercase letters.

That's on purpose. A dollar sign reminds you that this is real money leaving your real wallet. Take it away and the price turns into just a number on a page. It feels softer. Menu designers commonly recommend dropping it for exactly that reason.

**The counter-move:** put it back, in your head, every single time. "Twenty-four" is twenty-four dollars. Plus tax. Plus tip. Say it that way to yourself and the number stops being decoration.

## How the price ends: 9, 5, or round

Now look at the last digit.

- **Ending in 9 or 99.** Fast food and family places love this. A price like 9.99 whispers "deal" and "bargain." Your brain reads the first digit and rounds down.
- **Ending in 5 or 95.** A slightly softer version of the same idea. Friendly, value-minded.
- **Round numbers.** Upscale places almost always go round: "24," "38." A round number doesn't say "cheap." It says "quality, and don't think too hard about it."

Neither ending tells you anything about the food. 9.99 isn't automatically a deal. 38 isn't automatically worth it.

**The counter-move:** ignore the punctuation and look at the plate. What's the main ingredient? How big is the portion of the expensive part? That's what you're paying for.

## The decoy dish that anchors everything else

This one is my favorite. Sneaky... but beautiful.

Almost every menu has one dish with a crazy price. The giant seafood tower. The huge steak for two. The truffle-everything special. And you look at it and think, "Who orders that?"

Not many people. And that's fine with the restaurant, because that dish has a different job. It's the **anchor**. Once you've seen a very high number, everything else on the page looks reasonable next to it. Suddenly the mid-priced steak feels like the sensible, grown-up choice. You feel careful. Responsible.

Meanwhile, that "sensible" steak might carry a very healthy margin. The decoy did its work without ever leaving the kitchen.

You'll see the same idea with sizes. A small, a large, and a "medium" priced just a little under the large. The medium is there to make the large look like the smart buy.

**The counter-move:** when you spot the crazy price, smile at it. "I see what you're doing." Then cover it with your thumb and judge every other dish on its own. Would you pay this for this dish if the decoy wasn't there?

## No neat price column

Old-style menus lined all the prices up in a tidy column on the right. Many modern menus don't. The price sits right after the description, same font, same size, tucked into the end of a sentence.

Why? Because a neat column makes comparison shopping easy. Your eye runs down the list, finds the cheapest thing, and stops there. Scatter the prices into the text and you read the descriptions first, fall in love with a dish, and only then notice what it costs.

**The counter-move:** do the column yourself. Before you decide, scan just the numbers. Find the range. Then go back and read the dishes. It takes thirty seconds.

## The wine list's second-cheapest bottle

Wine lists have their own little game. Nobody likes ordering the cheapest bottle. It feels a bit embarrassing in front of a date or the boss. So a lot of people reach for the second-cheapest one instead. It feels modest but not cheap.

Some places know this habit very well, and the bottle sitting in that second spot can carry an even bigger markup than its neighbors. Not every restaurant does it. But enough do that I'd never assume the second-cheapest bottle is the safe value pick.

**The counter-move:** ask the server, "What's the best value on this list?" A good server or sommelier knows exactly where the deal is, and most of them enjoy being asked. I go deeper on this in [restaurant drink markup](../restaurant-drink-markup/).

## Prices you can't see at all

The trickiest price on a menu is the one that isn't printed. The special the server reads out loud. "Market price" on the lobster. The add-on that sounds tiny when it's offered. These deserve their own article, and they've got one: [restaurant specials and market price](../restaurant-specials-and-market-price/).

Same goes for the little upgrades. "Add shrimp?" "Make it a double?" I cover those in [restaurant upselling tricks](../restaurant-upselling-tricks/).

My rule for all of them: if you don't know the price, ask. Out loud. Nobody worth impressing will think less of you.

## A quick price check you can do at the table

Here's a simple habit. Before you order, ask yourself three questions:

1. **What's the main ingredient, and roughly what would it cost at the store?** Pasta, rice, potatoes and chicken are cheap. Fish, good beef and shellfish are not.
2. **How hard is this to make?** A braise that took hours is skill and time. A plate of pasta with jarred-style sauce is not.
3. **Would I pay this if there were no decoy, no fancy words, and a dollar sign in front?**

If the answers line up, order it happily. If they don't, look around. You'll often find better value on the same page. I've got more on that in [what to order at a restaurant](../what-to-order-at-a-restaurant/).

## Bottom line

Missing dollar signs, clever price endings, a decoy dish and scattered prices are all designed to make spending feel easier. Put the dollar sign back, ignore the last digit, cover the anchor with your thumb, and compare the numbers yourself. Then enjoy your dinner, because you picked it, not the menu.
